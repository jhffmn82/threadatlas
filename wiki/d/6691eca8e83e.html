The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916 · 19 sections

Abstract written by a model

This document is a historical account of French- and English-speaking relations in Canada, centered on Lower Canada’s political conflicts and the rebellions of 1837–38. It begins with the British conquest, stressing that British policies, protections for property, religion, civil law, and political rights, helped keep French Canadians loyal through the American Revolution and the War of 1812, even as they preserved a distinct national identity. Early legislative cooperation gave way to racial and political bitterness, especially after disputes over prison funding, Sir James Craig’s suppression of Le Canadien, and conflict between agrarian French Canadians and commercially oriented English Canadians. Louis Joseph Papineau becomes the central political figure after entering the Assembly in 1812 and serving as speaker from 1815. Leading the Patriote party, he demands greater Assembly control over revenue and the Executive, against the Legislative Council and the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique. His resistance to measures such as the Union Bill, his quarrels with Lord Dalhousie, and later his increasingly uncompromising leadership deepen the deadlock. After 1830, tension escalates through violence, the Ninety-Two Resolutions, and a split between Papineau’s radical supporters and moderates including John Neilson. Britain responds by sending Lord Gosford and royal commissioners, but their rejection of responsible government and an elective Legislative Council, followed by Lord John Russell’s authorization for the governor to spend without Assembly approval, convinces many Patriotes that conciliation has failed. Public meetings, boycotts, the Fils de la Liberte, and republican rhetoric lead to armed rebellion in 1837. The rebels win at St Denis under Wolfred Nelson, but are defeated at St Charles, Moore’s Corners, and St Eustache; leaders flee, are captured, killed, or die by suicide. The account portrays the revolt as badly planned and weakly supported, particularly because the Church opposed it. The rebellions prompt Britain to suspend Lower Canada’s constitution and appoint the Earl of Durham, whose report identifies racial feeling and irresponsible government as central causes and recommends union and responsible government. A second rebellion in 1838, led by Robert Nelson, quickly fails; executions and transportation follow. The conclusion calls for recognition of both English abuses and Patriote failures, and follows former Patriotes, including Louis Hippolyte LaFontaine, George Etienne Cartier, and Papineau, into later public life and the development of responsible government.

The 'Patriotes' of '37 front matter

{1} CHAPTER I CANADIANS, OLD AND NEW The conquest of Canada by British arms in the Seven Years' War gave rise to a situation in the colony which was fraught with tragic possibilities. body

The British conquest of Canada in the Seven Years' War placed French inhabitants under British rule, but the two communities generally lived together peacefully. French Canadians largely remained loyal to Great Britain during the American Revolution and the American invasion of Canada in 1775, encouraged by the policies of Murray and Carleton and the Quebec Act. They also fought alongside English Canadians during the War of 1812–1814, including Major de Salaberry's victory at Chateauguay. The later Rebellion in Canada was the only significant armed conflict between the two groups, with fighting at St Denis, St Charles, and St Eustache.

{7} CHAPTER II THE RIGHTS OF THE DEFEATED The British did not treat the French inhabitants of Canada as a conquered people; not as other countries won by conquest have been treated by their victorious invaders. body

The British granted the French inhabitants of Canada property, civil, religious, and political protections after the conquest, including French civil law and equal political rights in Upper Canada and Lower Canada. These concessions helped preserve French Canadian loyalty to the British crown while strengthening French national identity and demands for wider liberties. In the first Parliament of Lower Canada, French-speaking members secured the use of the French language alongside English in the Assembly's proceedings and publications. Early debates remained generally civil, although racial bitterness emerged later during the regime of Sir James Craig.

{13} CHAPTER III 'THE REIGN OF TERROR' During the session of 1805 the Assembly was confronted with the apparently innocent problem of building prisons. body

A dispute over how to fund prisons in 1805 intensified racial conflict between the agrarian French Canadians and commercially oriented English Canadians in Lower Canada. The French response included founding Le Canadien in 1806 to defend their language, institutions, laws, and political position. After Sir James Craig became governor in 1807, he dismissed militia officers associated with the paper, suppressed Le Canadien, and arrested its printer and proprietors, including Pierre Bedard. Craig’s policy failed to suppress opposition and worsened relations between the two groups, while Sir George Prevost later pursued conciliation and restored Bedard to public office.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century. body

Louis Joseph Papineau entered the House of Assembly for Lower Canada in 1812 and became its speaker in 1815, guiding the Patriote party for much of the next twenty-two years. French Canadians sought greater constitutional power, especially control over revenue and the Executive, which were dominated by the Legislative Council and the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique. Papineau opposed successive attempts to limit French-Canadian influence, including the Union Bill of 1822, and became embroiled in a bitter conflict with Lord Dalhousie. Financial disputes continued despite British efforts at compromise, and Papineau's uncompromising leadership contributed to a prolonged political deadlock.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity. body

The racial and political conflict in Lower Canada intensified after 1830, with violence in Montreal, attacks on Lord Aylmer, and increasingly coercive conduct by Papineau in the Assembly of Lower Canada. Papineau moved from criticism of the 1791 constitution toward admiration for the United States and advocacy of an elective Legislative Council. The Ninety-Two Resolutions, passed in 1834 by a large majority, identified genuine abuses but also used revolutionary language, demanded Lord Aylmer's impeachment, and offered no clear plan for responsible government. Their passage divided the Patriote party, as John Neilson and other moderates broke with Papineau, while several prominent future Canadian leaders remained among his supporters.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country. body

After the Ninety-Two Resolutions, Papineau and the Patriote party gained overwhelming control of the Assembly of Lower Canada, while Britain delayed responding to the colony's grievances. The British government recalled Lord Aylmer and appointed Lord Gosford as governor and chief royal commissioner, with Sir Charles Grey and Sir George Gipps, to investigate and settle the dispute. Gosford pursued conciliation with French Canadians, but Sir Francis Bond Head's publication of his instructions exposed Britain's opposition to major constitutional changes and undermined Gosford's policy. Papineau's followers then restricted supplies, producing a severe supply crisis, while the royal commissioners' reports rejected responsible government and an elective Legislative Council.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid. body

In 1836-37, the legislature and Assembly of Lower Canada continued refusing supplies until grievances and demands for political change were addressed, prompting the British government to adopt Lord John Russell's resolutions. The resolutions rejected an elective Legislative Council and responsible Executive Council while authorizing the governor to pay government expenses without Assembly approval. The Patriotes, led publicly by Papineau and Dr Wolfred Nelson, organized mass meetings, boycotted English goods, formed the Fils de la Liberte, and increasingly used republican and separatist language. As agitation intensified, Lord Gosford warned that conciliation had failed, the Church opposed rebellion, and armed Patriote activity contributed to the crisis that culminated in the rebellion of 1837.

] {69} CHAPTER VIII THE DOGS OF WAR As the autumn of 1837 wore on, the situation in Lower Canada began to assume an aspect more and more threatening. body

In autumn 1837, Lower Canada became increasingly unstable as the Patriotes organized military drills, intimidated loyalists, and prepared for armed resistance. Rioting in Montreal on November 6 between the Doric Club and the Fils de la Liberte was followed by warrants for Louis-Joseph Papineau, Wolfred Nelson, Thomas Storrow Brown, Edmund Bailey O'Callaghan, and others. The Chambly–Longueuil skirmish encouraged the Patriotes, while Nelson and Brown gathered armed forces at St Denis and St Charles. Colonel Charles Gore's British force was defeated at the Battle of St Denis, but the murder of Lieutenant Weir and the later killing of Chartrand intensified the conflict.

{82} CHAPTER IX _FORCE MAJEURE_ The check administered to Colonel Gore's column at St Denis, in the first engagement of the rebellion, was the only victory which fell to the rebel forces. body

The unit describes the British advance against rebel positions at St Charles and the defeat of the rebels there on November 25, following the earlier rebel victory at St Denis. Lieutenant-Colonel Wetherall delayed his advance, while Thomas Storrow Brown commanded the poorly armed and weakly organized rebel force at St Charles before fleeing to St Denis. The defeat at St Charles effectively ended the rebellion around the Richelieu, and rebel leaders fled or were captured. Colonel Gore later re-entered St Denis and British soldiers burned much of the village despite the absence of opposition.

{89} But the end of rebellion was not yet in sight. body

The unit describes two late encounters of the rebellion: the failed Patriote invasion defeated at Moore's Corners and the larger confrontation at St Eustache. At Moore's Corners, Captain Kemp's Missisquoi Volunteers routed about two hundred invaders from Swanton, including Robert Shore Milnes Bouchette. At St Eustache, Amury Girod and Dr J. O. Chenier organized a poorly disciplined rebel force that was crushed by Sir John Colborne's troops, with Chenier killed and Girod later committing suicide. The aftermath included the burning of much of St Benoit by loyalist volunteers despite the village's surrender.

It is usual to regard the rebellion of 1837 in Lower Canada as no less a fiasco body

The rebellion of 1837 in Lower Canada is portrayed as a hopeless and poorly planned movement led by figures who were largely unfamiliar to their followers. It was concentrated in the county of Two Mountains and the valley of the Richelieu, while Quebec and Three Rivers remained largely quiet. The Church officially opposed the movement, and only the cures of St Charles and St Benoit encouraged it. The rebellion was defeated because most French-Canadian people followed the Church's opposition, though stronger popular support might have endangered Sir John Colborne's ability to maintain control.

{104} CHAPTER X THE LORD HIGH COMMISSIONER The rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada profoundly affected public opinion in the mother country. body

The rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada shocked public opinion in Britain and led the Melbourne government to suspend Lower Canada's constitution and appoint the Earl of Durham as Lord High Commissioner. Durham arrived in Quebec in May 1838, reorganized the administration, granted a general amnesty with exceptions for rebel leaders, and issued an ordinance transporting some prisoners to Bermuda and excluding others from Canada. After the ordinance was attacked in Britain and disallowed by the government, Durham resigned but completed his inquiries and issued a proclamation defending his conduct before leaving Canada. His Report on the Affairs of British North America attributed the troubles chiefly to racial feeling and the union of representative and irresponsible government, and recommended legislative union and responsible government.

' {117} CHAPTER XI THE SECOND REBELLION The frigate _Inconstant_, with Lord Durham on board, was not two days out from Quebec when rebellion broke out anew in Lower Canada. body

The second rebellion in Lower Canada broke out in November 1838 after months of agitation and planning by Canadian refugees along the American border. Robert Nelson proclaimed a provisional government, but his poorly armed rebel force was defeated at Lacolle and Odelltown and then dispersed by Sir John Colborne's advancing troops. The rebellion lasted scarcely a week, and British forces also burned parts of Beauharnois. Nearly one hundred lesser ringleaders were tried; twelve, including Hindenlang, were executed, while most others were transported to Australia.

{128} CHAPTER XII A POSTSCRIPT The rebellion of 1837 now belongs to the dead past. body

The text reflects on the rebellion of 1837, urging Canadians to recognize both the abuses of English opponents and the failures of the Patriote leaders. It praises the loyalty of British and French-Canadian supporters of the Empire and the commitment to liberty shown by rebels such as Robert Bouchette. It traces the later careers of former Patriotes, including Louis Hippolyte LaFontaine, George Etienne Cartier, and Papineau, noting that several entered public service and helped shape responsible government. Papineau later returned to Canada, entered parliament, supported radical ideas, and retired to La Petite Nation after becoming estranged from the new political order.
