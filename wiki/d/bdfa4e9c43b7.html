Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905 · 30 sections

This document is a study of Laurence Sterne’s reception, translation, influence, imitation, and eventual critical backlash in Germany from the eighteenth into the early nineteenth century. Its introduction places Sterne within Germany’s broad dependence on foreign, especially English, literary models: English fiction offered everyday life, feeling, action, and humor against German pedantry and theory. Chapter II follows Sterne’s initially slow and uncertain arrival through inaccurate translations of Tristram Shandy, notices in journals, and responses by Zückert, Bode, Lessing, Herder, Hamann, Riedel, and Wieland. Although Shandy attracted informed admirers, its specifically British humor was difficult for many German readers; Sterne’s durable standing depended chiefly on A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy. Chapter III recounts the Journey’s 1768 publication and immediate German success. Bode rapidly translated and promoted it, with Lessing and Ebert assisting, while Mittelstedt supplied a rival version. The work’s travel form, sentiment, and Maria episode fostered a Yorick cult. Bode’s continuation, however, substantially omitted, altered, softened, and added material, adapting Sterne to German sentimental taste while often losing his whimsy. Chapter IV shows Sterne becoming a recognized German literary leader. Journals praised Yorick’s delicacy and moral feeling; Bode’s versions, Sterne’s letters, sermons, and even dubious or spurious works shaped the German image of Yorick. German readers also learned of Sterne’s borrowings, generally treating them as creative adaptation, and encountered English continuations and imitations. Chapters V and VI trace widespread cultural and literary influence. Sterne became a humane and emotional model, inspiring Jacobi’s “Lorenzodosen,” travel writing, concern for animals, humor, narrative discontinuity, typographical play, direct addresses, hobby-horses, and attention to small details. Wieland, Goethe, Herder, and Lessing are considered, with Goethe’s connection characterized as admiration and assimilation rather than readily provable direct imitation. Detailed attention goes to imitators including Jacobi, Schummel, Bock, Göschen, Hedemann, Göchhausen, and Wezel; their works range from sentimental travel narratives to satire, often borrowing Sterne’s forms and devices unevenly. Chapter VII describes the fading of Sterne’s influence as an active principle. Opposition chiefly attacked German imitators and affected Empfindsamkeit rather than Sterne himself. Lichtenberg led the criticism, joined by Sturz, Goeckingk, Campe, Möser, Knigge, and Timme. Timme’s Der Empfindsame particularly exposes exaggerated sympathy that fails to become humane action, while still admiring Sterne’s narrative whimsy. The study ends with protests against sentimentalism and Sterne’s German disciples.

NEW YORK, May 1, 1905. body

The introduction examines Germany’s literary dependence on foreign models, especially the predominant English influence in the latter half of the eighteenth century. It explains how English writers, translations, periodicals, and the English novel helped shape German fiction, particularly through Robinson Crusoe, Richardson, Rousseau, and Fielding. The German novel is described as constrained by pedantry, pedagogy, and theoretical speculation, while English narrative fiction offered models of everyday life, feeling, action, and humor. The unit presents Laurence Sterne’s reception in Germany as an example of widespread popularity, imitation, and opposition arising from concerns about originality and national literary independence.

] CHAPTER II STERNE IN GERMANY BEFORE THE PUBLICATION OF THE SENTIMENTAL JOURNEY It is no exaggeration to assert that the works of Yorick obtained and still retain a relatively more substantial position of serious consideration and recognized merit in France and Germany than in the countries where Sterne’s own tongue is spoken. body

The unit traces Sterne’s early reception in Germany before and shortly after the publication of A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy, emphasizing the delayed and initially uncertain spread of his reputation. It describes the first German notice of Sterne, Johann Friedrich Zückert’s inaccurate translation of Tristram Shandy, its publication history, and contemporary reviews. It also discusses Johann Joachim Christoph Bode’s possible connection with an early review and the role of German literary journals in introducing Sterne’s works. The account places this reception within the broader context of British supremacy in Germany and the influence of British authors on German literature.

The unit traces the early reception of Laurence Sterne, Tristram Shandy, and Yorick’s Sermons in German periodicals and among German writers before and shortly after The Sentimental Journey. It shows that German reviews initially knew little about Sterne or his works, while the Sentimental Journey later brought Yorick’s sentimentalism and characteristics into wider recognition. The unit also examines possible connections between Sterne’s characters and the works of Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, especially Minna von Barnhelm and the lost Yorick’s sermon for Alberti. It concludes that similarities involving Corporal Trim and Just may indicate recollection or influence, although several proposed parallels are rejected as coincidental or unsupported.

The unit traces the reception of Laurence Sterne’s works among German and Swiss literati, emphasizing that Herder, Hamann, Riedel, Wieland, and others knew or discussed Tristram Shandy before or around the appearance of the Sentimental Journey. Herder repeatedly used Shandean names and references, read the Sentimental Journey in 1768, and admired Sterne while criticizing his ambiguities. Riedel praised Shandy’s psychological and humorous qualities, while Wieland enthusiastically encountered Sterne in 1767 and considered translating Tristram Shandy. The unit concludes that Sterne’s lasting position in Germany was secured primarily by the Sentimental Journey, since the specifically British humor of Shandy was difficult for German eighteenth-century readers to appreciate.

] CHAPTER III THE PUBLICATION OF THE SENTIMENTAL JOURNEY On February 27, 1768, the Sentimental Journey was published in London,[1] less than three weeks before the author’s death, and the book was at once transplanted to German soil, beginning there immediately its career of commanding influence and wide-spread popularity. body

The Sentimental Journey was published in London on February 27, 1768, and quickly achieved wide influence and popularity in Germany. Its success was supported by existing German knowledge of Laurence Sterne, the strong contemporary interest in travel literature, and the sentimental and emotional tendencies developing in Germany. Johann Joachim Christoph Bode rapidly translated and promoted the work, with assistance from Gotthold Ephraim Lessing and Ebert, while Pastor Mittelstedt produced a competing translation. The Maria incident and the book’s treatment of sentiment helped strengthen the Yorick cult and connected Sterne’s work with German sentimentalism.

Bode’s translation of the third and fourth volumes of Yorick’s Journey,[38] that body

The unit examines Bode’s translation of the third and fourth volumes of Yorick’s Journey, which continued Eugenius’s work and was published in England in 1769. Bode gave no adequate explanation of his role, leading many readers to mistake him for the author, while Böttiger and the Allgemeine deutsche Bibliothek contributed to differing interpretations of the translation. The text details Bode’s extensive omissions, alterations, and original additions, including Das Mündel, Das Hündchen, Die Moral, and other episodes. It argues that Bode adapted the work to German literary tastes and sentimentalism, often softening indecency but failing to reproduce Sterne’s characteristic whimsicality.

] CHAPTER IV STERNE IN GERMANY AFTER THE PUBLICATION OF THE SENTIMENTAL JOURNEY The publication of the Sentimental Journey, as implied in the previous chapter, brought Sterne into vital connection with literary impulses and emotional experiences in Germany, and his position as a leader was at once recognized. body

The publication of A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy connected Laurence Sterne with German literary culture, where he was quickly recognized as a literary leader and Yorick was widely admired. German journals reviewed the Sentimental Journey and its translations, especially the versions by Johann Joachim Christoph Bode and Mittelstedt, often idealizing Yorick’s delicacy and moral sensibility. Bode’s translations of the Sentimental Journey, its continuation, and Tristram Shandy were praised for preserving Sterne’s spirit, though later critics attacked their inaccuracies and Benzler revised them. The unit also discusses the German reception of Tristram Shandy, the publication of Sterne’s letters, and Lydia Sterne de Medalle’s role in editing her father’s correspondence.

The unit examines the reception in Germany of Sterne’s Yorick-Eliza letters, their translations, reviews, and disputed authenticity, especially in Hamburg and Leipzig. Johann Joachim Christoph Bode is identified as the probable translator of several Hamburg volumes, whose work was generally praised by German reviewers. It also traces German translations and misattributions of Sterne’s sermons and other spurious works, including “Sermons to Asses,” Yorick’s meditations, and The Koran. German criticism often accepted works as Sterne’s despite doubts about their authenticity, helping shape the German image of Yorick.

Sterne’s unacknowledged borrowings, his high-handed and extensive appropriation body

The unit examines Laurence Sterne’s extensive unacknowledged borrowings and the largely moderate German response to them. George Ferriar’s investigations became known in Germany chiefly through Friedrich Nicolai’s 1795 summary, which defended Sterne’s creative adaptation, while Gotthold Ephraim Lessing had apparently noticed similar borrowings but remained silent. The unit also traces English imitations of Sterne, especially works modeled on the Sentimental Journey, and their German translations or adaptations. These included imitations attributed to Samuel Paterson, Schink, and others, as well as works presented as continuations or companions to Yorick’s travels.

] CHAPTER V STERNE’S INFLUENCE IN GERMANY Thus in manifold ways Sterne was introduced into German life and letters. body

The unit describes Sterne’s broad influence in Germany, where he was regarded as a humane guide, emotional mentor, and model for conduct as well as literature. Johann Georg Jacobi’s “Lorenzodosen,” horn snuff-boxes associated with Yorick and Franciscan Lorenzo, became a popular sentimental fad and inspired proposed orders and rituals. Sterne’s influence also shaped German attitudes toward animals, travel writing, humor, whimsy, and literary imitation. Wieland absorbed Sterne’s style and motifs in several works while remaining an independent thinker who later criticized slavish imitation of Sterne.

[37] Herder’s early acquaintance with Sterne has been already treated. body

The unit examines Laurence Sterne’s continuing influence on German writers, especially Johann Wolfgang Goethe, while also discussing Johann Gottfried Herder and Gotthold Ephraim Lessing. It traces Goethe’s early exposure to Sterne in Strassburg, his later references to Sterne, possible parallels between their works, and Goethe’s explicit statements of indebtedness. It also investigates Goethe’s use of material from The Koran in Wilhelm Meister’s Journeyman Years and considers whether this constituted literary plagiarism. The unit concludes that Goethe’s relationship to Sterne involved admiration and cultural assimilation more than easily demonstrable direct imitation.

The German literature of the latter part of the eighteenth century and the beginning of the nineteenth was influenced by Laurence Sterne’s example in form, style, and ideas. Sterne’s narrative discontinuity, typographical eccentricities, direct appeals to readers, hobby-horse theory, traveler classifications, and emphasis on insignificant details were adopted or imitated in Germany. Yorick’s traveler classification and the hobby-horse became recurring conversational, literary, and fictional references. Jacob Minor argued that Sterne sharpened observation of self-deception and helped influence Wieland, Goethe, and Tieck in exposing false ideals and life-lies.

] CHAPTER VI IMITATORS OF STERNE Among the disciples of Sterne in Germany whose literary imitation may be regarded as typical of their master’s influence, Johann Georg Jacobi is perhaps the best known. body

This unit examines German imitators of Laurence Sterne, especially Johann Georg Jacobi and Johann Gottlieb Schummel, and their use of Sterne’s sentimental and whimsical techniques. Jacobi’s Winterreise and Sommerreise imitate A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy but emphasize sentimentalism, instruction, animals, and humane religious figures more than Sterne’s whimsicality or descriptions of place. Schummel’s Empfindsame Reisen durch Deutschland is presented as a particularly extensive imitation, borrowing forms, motifs, narrative devices, characters, and expressions from both A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy and Tristram Shandy. The discussion also considers contemporary responses to these works and the role of translations in spreading Sterne’s influence in Germany.

The unit examines Schummel’s imitation of Laurence Sterne and Yorick in the second volume of his work, contrasting its sentimental episodes, digressions, typographical devices, and other Shandean features with their often awkward execution. It explains that the third volume largely abandons Sterne’s influence, incorporates several independent narratives, and concludes with Schummel’s unusually severe criticism of his own three-volume work. The unit also records Goethe’s harsh review of the second volume and the more varied reactions of contemporary German periodicals.

The unit traces Sterne’s influence on German writers and works, especially Schummel, Johann Christian Bock, Göschen, and H. J. C. Hedemann. Schummel gradually abandoned sentimental imitation, while Bock’s Die Tagereise and related volumes directly adapted motifs and attitudes from Sterne and Jacobi. Göschen’s Reise von Johann combined Yorick-like travel, whimsy, and sentiment with German descriptive and informational tendencies. Hedemann’s Empfindsame Reise von Oldenburg nach Bremen used Sterne-like narrative self-consciousness and whimsy chiefly to satirize earlier sentimental travelers and sentimentalism.

The unit surveys German works influenced by Sterne, ranging from sentimental and comic adaptations to more direct imitations of Yorick’s Sentimental Journey. It discusses works by Andreas Geo. Fr. von Rabenau, Johann Moritz Schwager, Blankenburg, and others, while emphasizing E. A. A. von Göchhausen’s M . . . R . . . as an unusually sustained and self-conscious imitation. Göchhausen’s book imitates Sterne through digressions, readerly interruptions, typographical jokes, sentimental episodes, and borrowings from the Lorenzo episode, while gradually developing more independent material. The literary journals recognized the imitation, criticized its dependence on Sterne, and nevertheless praised aspects of the work.

The unit examines Johann Karl Wezel’s Tobias Knaut as a major German adaptation of the style of Tristram Shandy, combining the influence of Laurence Sterne and Henry Fielding with satire of Empfindsamkeit. It identifies numerous structural, typographical, narrative, and thematic parallels between Tobias Knaut and Sterne’s works, while emphasizing that Wezel’s episodes are more extraneous and that his satire sometimes opposes German sentimentalism. The unit also discusses Wegener’s Raritäten as a vulgar and pernicious imitation of Sterne, surveys scattered references to Sterne and Yorick in German literature, and assesses the limited or indirect Sternean qualities of writers including Adolph von Knigge, Moritz August von Thümmel, Theodor Gottlieb von Hippel, and Jean Paul. It concludes by noting that detailed studies of Thümmel, Hippel, and Jean Paul in relation to Sterne were intended as a continuation of the essay.

] CHAPTER VII OPPOSITION TO STERNE AND HIS TYPE OF SENTIMENTALISM Sterne’s influence in Germany lived its own life, and gradually and imperceptibly died out of letters, as an actuating principle. body

Sterne’s influence in Germany gradually faded as an active literary principle, though Sterne himself remained a recognized prophet and leader. Opposition focused mainly on the unsuccessful adaptations of Yorick’s style and sentimentalism by German imitators, while critics such as Lessing, Goethe, and Herder continued to value Sterne. Georg Christopher Lichtenberg was the chief opponent of the sentimental tendency, attacking both Sterne’s personal character and the literary methods of Sterne’s disciples. Lichtenberg’s satires targeted sentimental journeying, eccentricity, lack of originality, and other tendencies associated with the Sentimentalists and Kraftgenies.

The unit examines German opposition to the Sterne cult through Helfreich Peter Sturz, Goeckingk, Campe, Möser, Knigge, and especially Christian Friedrich Timme. It describes how critics distinguished genuine moral feeling from exaggerated or affected Empfindsamkeit and satirized imitators of Yorick, Werther, and Siegwart. It also discusses Timme’s Der Empfindsame, which attacks sentimental excesses while distinguishing Sterne from his German followers and borrowing devices from Tristram Shandy and the Sentimental Journey.

The unit examines Timme’s satirical treatment of sentimentalism through Frau Kurt and Pankraz, whose exaggerated sympathy repeatedly fails to produce humane action. It argues that Timme ridicules Sterne’s sentimental influence, especially motifs from The Sentimental Journey, while adapting Sterne’s narrative whimsicality with admiration rather than burlesque. The unit also discusses contemporary reviews of Timme’s novel and compares it with Wezel’s more serious but less successful satire, Wilhelmine Arend, oder die Gefahren der Empfindsamkeit. It concludes by noting other protests against sentimentalism and against German imitations of Sterne.
