The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · 87 sections

Abstract written by a model

This is Volume II of Apollodorus’s The Library in the Loeb Classical Library, translated into English by Sir James George Frazer. It contains the continuation of Book III, the Epitome, an appendix, notes, and an index. Its main mythological narrative moves through divine and heroic genealogies into the histories of Troy, Athens, the Trojan War, and its aftermath. It begins with Atlas and Pleione’s Pleiades and follows lines of descent including Hermes, Aesculapius, the Dioscuri, Dardanus, Priam, Paris, Achilles, and the Trojan royal house. It recounts foundations, sacred objects, prophecies, abductions, crimes, transformations, and successions, notably the Palladium’s arrival at Ilium, Paris’s survival and later abduction of Helen, and Achilles’s upbringing and departure for Troy. A major central sequence concerns Athens: Cecrops, Athena’s victory over Poseidon for Attica, the kings Cranaus, Erichthonius, Pandion, and Erechtheus, and tragedies involving Procne, Philomela, and Tereus. It then follows Aegeus and Theseus: the Minotaur tribute and labyrinth, Theseus’s clearing of the road to Athens, his killing of the Minotaur with Ariadne’s help, Aegeus’s death, Daedalus and Icarus’s escape, and Theseus’s later downfall after the Dioscuri recover Helen. The Epitome develops the Pelopid conflict of Atreus and Thyestes, then the expedition against Troy prompted by Helen’s abduction. It traces the Greeks’ preparations, Iphigenia’s rescue by Artemis, the siege, Patroclus’s death, Achilles’s killing of Hector and later death, the recovery of Philoctetes and Neoptolemus, theft of the Palladium, and the Wooden Horse. Troy is sacked, followed by disastrous Greek returns, Agamemnon’s murder and Orestes’s revenge, Menelaus’s restoration, and Odysseus’s wanderings, homecoming, and death at Telegonus’s hands. The appendix compares selected Greek tales with accounts from many other peoples: fire and renewal, magical revivification, Oedipus-like incest stories, twins and fertility, Peleus and Thetis as a supernatural-spouse tale, Idomeneus’s vow, and especially widespread variants of the Ulysses and Polyphemus story. Notes and the extensive alphabetical index provide genealogical, textual, and mythological reference material.

The notes concern the Pleiades and ancient accounts of their names, ancestry, and identity. Ancient sources generally agreed on the names of the seven Pleiades, with Aratus giving the same names and order as Apollodorus. A Scholiast on Theocritus offered a different list except for Maia and reported, citing Callimachus, that the Pleiades were daughters of the queen of the Amazons. Their father was commonly identified as Atlas, leading to the alternative name Atlantides, while the origin of the name Pleiades was disputed.

Because Pollux refused immortality while Castor was dead, Zeus granted them alternating existence among the gods and mortals.
