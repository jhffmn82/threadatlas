The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes

Arthur Conan Doyle · about 1892 to 1893 · 40 sections

Abstract written by a model

This document is a sequence of Sherlock Holmes cases narrated or framed by Dr. John Watson, moving from investigations of theft, murder, blackmail, and family secrets to Holmes’s apparent final confrontation with Professor Moriarty. Holmes’s methods, close observation, reconstruction of motives, and attention to overlooked clues, drive every case, while Watson accompanies him, records events, and sometimes supplies the personal connection that brings a client. The collection opens with the disappearance of the racehorse Silver Blaze and John Straker’s death: Holmes clears Fitzroy Simpson, finds the horse concealed by Silas Brown, and proves Straker drugged Ned Hunter and tried to lame the horse before Silver Blaze killed him in self-defence. Other cases reveal Jim Browner’s jealous murder of his wife and Alec Fairbairn; Effie Munro’s concealed daughter Lucy and Grant Munro’s acceptance of them; and a fraud in which the Pinner brothers use Hall Pycroft’s handwriting to infiltrate Mawson & Williams. Holmes then recounts formative earlier cases. In the Gloria Scott matter, Victor Trevor’s father is exposed as James Armitage, a transported convict whose past aboard the mutinied ship returns through Hudson’s blackmail. In the Musgrave Ritual, Holmes follows an ancestral catechism to a hidden chamber, Brunton’s body, and a crown concealed for Charles II. Later investigations identify the Cunninghams as burglars and William Kirwan’s murderers; explain Colonel Barclay’s death through Henry Wood’s history of betrayal; and uncover Blessington as Sutton, an informer killed by former bank-robbery accomplices. The remaining cases broaden Holmes’s circle and stakes. Mycroft Holmes introduces the kidnapped Greek interpreter Melas, leading to the death of Paul Kratides and the escape of Sophy with her captors. Holmes restores Percy Phelps’s stolen naval treaty by catching Joseph Harrison retrieving it from beneath a floorboard. Finally, Holmes identifies Moriarty as the force behind extensive crime in London. Pursued across England and Europe, Holmes and Watson reach Reichenbach Falls, where Moriarty lures Watson away. Watson returns to find signs that Holmes and Moriarty struggled and fell into the chasm; neither body is recovered, but Holmes’s evidence exposes Moriarty’s gang.

Pinner’s suicide attempt is explained as his response to learning that his brother’s crime had been exposed.

Sherlock Holmes tells Dr. Watson about the case of the Gloria Scott, which was the first criminal investigation in which Holmes was involved. As a college student, Holmes visited Donnithorpe and observed that Victor Trevor's father, Mr. Trevor, feared a past connection involving the sailor Hudson and another man named Beddoes. Hudson gained power over Mr. Trevor, and a strange coded message sent from Hampshire caused Mr. Trevor to suffer a fatal stroke. The narrative describes the mutiny aboard the transport ship Gloria Scott, in which the prisoners and some sailors seize the vessel and massacre most of its officers and soldiers. Prendergast leads the surviving mutineers, while Evans and others refuse to participate in the execution of the remaining prisoners and escape in a boat. The Gloria Scott is destroyed when the first mate threatens to ignite a powder barrel and an explosion kills those aboard. Years later, Evans and Beddoes are threatened and blackmailed by Hudson, a sailor who survived the wreck, but all three men ultimately disappear.

VI. The Musgrave Ritual body

Sherlock Holmes tells Dr. John Watson about the Musgrave Ritual case, an early investigation that helped establish his professional reputation. Reginald Musgrave asks Holmes to investigate the disappearance of his butler, Brunton, and the subsequent disappearance of Rachel Howells at Hurlstone. Brunton had been caught studying the family’s ritual document, while Rachel’s footprints led to the mere, where a linen bag containing rusted metal and dull pebbles was recovered. Holmes begins by asking to examine the ritual document.

Musgrave shows Watson a paper containing the Musgrave catechism, a seventeenth-century family text of questions and answers. The catechism concerns ownership, succession, the positions of the sun and shadow, a sequence of steps, and a trust. Holmes believes the paper is practical and that Brunton had consulted it while comparing it with a map or chart. Holmes and Musgrave plan to travel to Sussex to investigate the mystery on the spot.

Sherlock Holmes follows the Musgrave Ritual’s measurements from the oak and former elm at Hurlstone to the cellar beneath the old house. There he and Reginald Musgrave find Brunton’s muffler, a concealed chamber, a brass-bound box, and Brunton’s dead body. Holmes infers that Brunton and Howells opened the chamber, that Howells may have caused or concealed Brunton’s death, and that she took the box’s contents. The recovered objects are identified as the ancient crown of the Kings of England, a Royalist treasure hidden during the troubles after Charles I’s execution and intended for Charles II.

Sherlock Holmes, recovering from a severe illness after an international investigation, visits Reigate with Dr. Watson and stays at Colonel Hayter's house. Colonel Barclay and his wife, Nancy Barclay, had appeared to have a happy marriage, despite his occasional violence, depression, superstition, and fear of being alone after dark. Holmes has several threads of the case but believes that Watson may help him reach a solution.

Mrs. Barclay returns to Lachine after meeting Miss Morrison, quarrels with Colonel James Barclay in the morning-room, and is found insensible beside his dead body. Miss Morrison reveals that Mrs. Barclay encountered Henry Wood shortly before the quarrel, and Holmes traces Wood to lodgings in Aldershot. Holmes believes Henry Wood alone can explain what happened in the room and plans to question him with Dr. Watson present.

Sherlock Holmes and Watson visit Henry Wood in Aldershot to investigate Colonel James Barclay's death and the possible murder accusation against Nancy Barclay. Wood explains that James Barclay betrayed him during the Mutiny, leading to his capture, torture, and lifelong deformity, and that Barclay later died after seeing him confront him and Nancy. Wood also says that he accidentally took the door key and left his mongoose, Teddy, behind at the Barclay house. The inquest concludes that Barclay died of apoplexy, and Holmes realizes that Nancy's use of the name David referred to Barclay's betrayal involving Uriah and Bathsheba.

The released gang members took revenge on Sutton, entering his room with help from the page, conducting a concealed judicial proceeding, and hanging him. The page disappears, while the three murderers are never found and are later suspected of having died aboard the steamer Norah Creina.
