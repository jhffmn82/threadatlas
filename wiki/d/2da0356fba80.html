RAG LLMs are Not Safer: A Safety Analysis of Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models

Bang An · 2025-04-29 to 2025-05-04 · 16 sections
"RAG LLMs are Not Safer: A Safety Analysis of Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models" by Bang An, Shiyue Zhang, Mark Dredze (2025), licensed CC BY. Shown here as extracted text, with facts drawn from it. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.naacl-long.281

Abstract written by a model

This unit studies whether Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) makes large language models safer than non-RAG systems when responding to harmful questions. It defines RAG as a pipeline in which a retriever, such as BM25, selects relevant documents for an LLM to use alongside a user query, unlike non-RAG models that rely on stored knowledge and a different instruction. Across 5,592 harmful questions and eleven LLMs, evaluated with BM25 over English Wikipedia and Llama Guard 2, the study finds that RAG is generally not safer. Eight of eleven models show substantially changed safety behavior, often producing more unsafe responses; for Llama-3-8B, unsafe responses rise from 0.3% without RAG to 9.2% with RAG. RAG also changes risk profiles in model-dependent ways, with Llama-3-8B-Instruct becoming vulnerable in all 16 risk categories. The analysis examines inherent model safety, document safety, and models’ ability to perform RAG tasks. RAG retains many non-RAG vulnerabilities while adding new unsafe behavior, even where both the model and retrieved documents are safe. Unsafe documents increase risk, but most unsafe outputs come from safe documents, because models may repurpose retrieved information or add internal knowledge. Weak information extraction, refusal behavior, and incomplete attention to documents can further affect apparent and actual safety. The unit then tests red-teaming methods GCG and AutoDAN under access to the model and retriever but not the corpus. Jailbreak prompts optimized for non-RAG models usually do not transfer to RAG, though AutoDAN transfers somewhat better. Methods optimized directly for RAG can succeed on training data, but testing success falls when prompts retrieve different documents. The study concludes that RAG-specific red-teaming and aligned training/testing retrieval conditions are needed. It recommends RAG-tailored safety fine-tuning, re-retrieval during attack optimization, penalties for unstable retrieval, and prompts optimized across many documents. Limitations include the exclusion of RAG-trained LLMs such as Command R2, use of BM25 rather than dense retrievers, complex model-query-document interactions, and constraints involving red-teaming assumptions, risk definitions, user settings, and corpora. The paper acknowledges harmful generated language and possible misuse, while aiming to expose vulnerabilities and support safer models and defenses.

Abstract body

The unit examines how Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) affects the safety of large language models (LLMs) compared with non-RAG frameworks. A comparative analysis of eleven LLMs finds that RAG can make models less safe and alter their safety profile. The analysis shows that unsafe generations can occur even when both the models and retrieved documents are safe. It also finds that existing red-teaming methods are less effective for RAG settings than for non-RAG settings, highlighting the need for RAG-specific safety research.

Introduction body

The introduction examines whether Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems are safer than non-RAG large language models when answering harmful questions. The authors compare eleven popular models on more than 5,000 harmful questions and find that RAG can introduce unsafe behavior even when the model and retrieved documents are safe. For example, Llama-3-8B's unsafe responses increase from 0.3% to 9.2% in the RAG setting. The authors also find that existing red-teaming methods may fail against RAG-based models and argue that RAG-specific methods are needed.

Related Work body

The unit reviews safety research on LLMs, including fine-tuning, system-level guardrails, red-teaming, controllable generation, interpretability, unlearning, and benchmark evaluation. It notes that most safety studies focus on non-RAG settings, while safety research on RAG LLMs emphasizes corpus poisoning and also examines corpus leakage, retriever robustness, and document perturbations. The unit focuses on RAG scenarios with a carefully controlled corpus and relates them to the safety of LLM agents using tools. It also surveys red-teaming and states that no red-teaming methods have been designed specifically for RAG LLMs.

Definitions body

The unit defines a standard RAG pipeline as consisting of an LLM G and a retriever R, such as BM25 or a dense retriever. Given a user query, the retriever selects the top k relevant documents, which the LLM uses with the query and an instruction to generate a response. A standard non-RAG LLM pipeline instead relies on knowledge stored in the model and uses a different instruction. The figure reports that most LLMs in the RAG setting produce a significantly higher percentage of unsafe responses than in the non-RAG setting.

RQ1: Are RAG-based LLMs safer than body

The unit reports a large-scale evaluation of eleven LLMs comparing non-RAG, RAG (Docs), and RAG (Docs + Model Knowledge) settings on 5,592 harmful questions. It finds that RAG-based LLMs are generally not safer: eight of eleven models show markedly different safety behavior, often with increased unsafe responses, including Llama-3-8B-Instruct, large models, and GPT-4o. RAG also changes models’ risk profiles in a model-dependent way, with Llama-3-8B-Instruct becoming vulnerable across all 16 risk categories. The evaluation uses BM25 over English Wikipedia and Llama Guard 2 to judge response safety.

RQ2: What makes RAG-based LLMs .. Factor 3: An LLM’s Capability on RAG body

The unit investigates why RAG-based LLMs can become unsafe, focusing on inherent model safety, retrieved-document safety, and capability on RAG tasks. It finds that RAG carries over many non-RAG vulnerabilities while introducing additional unsafe behavior, even when retrieved documents are safe. Unsafe documents increase the probability of unsafe responses, but most unsafe responses arise from safe documents because models repurpose document information or supplement it with internal knowledge. The unit also shows that weak extraction, refusal behavior, and incomplete attention to documents affect both apparent safety and actual safety.

RQ3: Are red-teaming methods .. Applying Jailbreaking Methods to RAG body

The unit evaluates whether red-teaming methods can jailbreak RAG-based models, focusing on GCG and AutoDAN under a threat model with access to the model and retriever but not the corpus. Jailbreaking prompts trained in the non-RAG setting generally fail to transfer to the RAG setting, although AutoDAN transfers slightly better than GCG. When the methods are optimized directly in the RAG setting, they achieve high training-set attack success, but success drops during testing because the optimized prompts change the retrieved documents. The results show that training and testing conditions must be aligned and that more effective red-teaming methods are needed for RAG models.

Discussions on Future Directions body

The unit concerns RAG-based LLMs and their safety behavior, which can be more vulnerable than that of non-RAG models. It argues that safety fine-tuning should be tailored to RAG tasks because these models synthesize information from retrieved documents. It proposes new red-teaming methods, including re-retrieval during optimization, penalties for retrieval instability, and prompts optimized across many retrieved documents. It also calls for research into why safe documents can produce unsafe responses and for safety strategies suited to dynamic corpora.

Limitations body

The study examines general LLMs and their safety behaviors in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) settings. It excludes LLMs trained particularly for RAG, such as Command R2, and uses BM25 rather than dense retrievers because BM25 is strong and widely used. The analysis identifies three factors that may explain safety behaviors but acknowledges possible complex interactions among the model, query, and retrieved documents. It also discusses limitations involving white-box red-teaming, differing risk definitions, fixed user settings, and the influence of corpora on safety violations.

Ethics Statement body

The paper contains potentially harmful AI-generated language. Its jailbreaking methods may be used for malicious activities. The paper aims to uncover overlooked vulnerabilities of LLMs in the RAG setting, increase awareness of those risks, and accelerate the development of safer models and defense mechanisms.
