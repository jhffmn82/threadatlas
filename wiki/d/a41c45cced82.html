Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860 · 22 sections

This document is a descriptive and travel account of Morocco, its neighboring Tunisian Sahara, and the Jereed, combining social observation, political history, geography, commerce, and personal narrative. It begins with Morocco’s Jewish communities, especially at Mogador and in the Atlas, describing changing treatment, customs, beliefs, marriage, and vulnerability, including Mogador’s later devastation. It then traces the Maroquine dynasties to the ruling Shereefs, whose descent from Ali and Fatima underpins their religious and political authority. Muley Suleiman and Muley Abd Errahman are central figures, alongside discussions of slavery, European relations, and British influence. The middle chapters survey Morocco as an empire of uncertain bounds and uneven imperial control. They describe its peoples, Berbers, Arabs, Moors, Jews, and Blacks, its Atlas Mountains, climate, products, mines, trade, horses, tribal regions, ports, coastal towns, imperial cities, oases, and caravan routes. Fez, Mequinez, Morocco, and Tafilett receive particular attention, while the text repeatedly emphasizes local tribal independence, uncertain population figures, neglected resources, and perceived urban decline. The Figheegh oasis cluster illustrates both productive irrigation and conflict over water. The narrative then shifts to events at Mogador, where disputes involving Jewish youths and Moors occur and the Governor states that the Sultan will resist anti-slavery appeals unless compelled by superior force. It moves to Tunisian territory, explaining the date-palm economy of El-Jereed before following Captain Balfour, Richard Reade, and Sidi Mohammed, Bey of the Camp, on an expedition toward Kairwan, Ghafsa, Toser, and Nefta. Their journey records the Bey’s camp and administration, justice and bastinado, saints, tribute collection, hunting, ruins, springs, oasis agriculture, date harvesting, waterworks, and harsh weather. Sheikh Tahid’s imprisonment, sale of his gardens, and release illustrate the Bey’s fiscal power. The travelers finally return to Tunis, and the narrator leaves Mogador on the Renshaw schooner, reaching England after a month of gales. An appendix recounts the French bombardment and capture of Mogador in August 1844, the flight of authorities and residents, the sacking and burning by wild tribes, the escape of European residents, and severe losses suffered by Jews and other inhabitants. Notes further distinguish Shereefs from Marabouts and summarize Barbary’s peoples, places, geography, and traditions.

CHAPTER I. body

The unit describes the social conditions, customs, appearance, religion, and treatment of Jewish communities in Morocco, especially Mogador and the Atlas Mountains. It contrasts the improved position of European and some native Jews with the oppression described by Ali Bey and the continuing fluctuations of imperial policy. It recounts the devastation of Mogador during the French bombardment, the independence and beliefs of the Atlas Jews, and customs involving marriage, polygamy, beauty, superstition, and white brandy. It concludes with anecdotes about the Moroccan Court and the dangers faced by physicians and quacks.

CHAPTER II. body

The unit traces the succession of Maroquine dynasties from the Edristees in 789 through the Shereefs, who have ruled Morocco for more than three centuries. It describes the Shereefian genealogy and explains the dynasty’s descent from Ali and Fatima, the daughter of Mahomet. It profiles Muley Abd Errahman, who was selected by his uncle Muley Suleiman and strengthened imperial authority through frugal, conservative rule. It also discusses Morocco’s religious and political importance, the status of its Shereefs, and the limits of Abd-el-Kader’s ability to replace the reigning dynasty.

The unit recounts the succession crisis after Sidi Mohammed’s death, the reigns of Yezeed, Muley Hisham, and Muley Suleiman, and Muley Abd Errahman’s succession among the Shereefs. It praises Muley Suleiman’s peaceful rule and abolition of Christian slavery, while discussing Morocco’s relations with France, England, and Europe. It examines the slave trade and the Shereefian justification of it under Islam. It also criticizes the British-Moroccan treaties, especially their provisions for disputes involving British subjects, and describes British influence in Morocco, including at Gibraltar and Mogador.

CHAPTER III. body

The unit describes the Empire of Morocco’s uncertain extent, military and commercial resources, political influence, geography, and population. It reviews the successive settlement and conquest of North Africa by Libyans, Getulians, Phoenicians, Romans, Vandals, and Arabs, and distinguishes Berbers, Arabs, Moors, Jews, and Blacks among Morocco’s inhabitants. It explains that the Sultan’s authority is strong in religious and nominal terms but limited in many southern, northern, and mountainous regions. It also discusses Morocco’s natural boundaries, Atlas mountains, trade, and the mixed origins of its urban population.

The nature of the soil and climate of Morocco are not unlike those of Spain and body

The unit describes Morocco’s climate, geography, agriculture, commerce, mineral resources, and animal life. It compares Morocco’s soil and climate with those of Spain and Portugal and identifies the Atlas Mountains as its dominant geographic feature. Morocco produces grain, fruits, vegetables, timber, and regionally distinctive argan oil, while its northern and southern regions export different staple goods. The unit also reports that Morocco possesses extensive but largely neglected mineral resources and describes the desert horse called sherb-errech.

CHAPTER IV. body

The unit describes Morocco’s political, tribal, and natural divisions, distinguishing the northern Rif, the central Atlas, and the southern quasi-Saharan region. It questions the reliability of Moroccan population statistics and surveys coastal towns, ports, and Spanish presidios along the Mediterranean. Particular attention is given to the Jafarine Isles, Melilla, Alhucemas, Penon de Velez, Gomera, Tetouan, and Ceuta, including their populations, strategic positions, commerce, and relations with Spain. It also describes the Berbers of the Rif and Garet and recounts Abd-el-Kader’s refuge among them.

The unit describes towns, rivers, tribes, fortifications, populations, commerce, and historical events along Morocco's Atlantic coast, from Arzila southward to Mogador. It discusses Arzila, El Araish, Mehedia, Salee, Rabat, Fidallah, Dar-el-Beida, Azamor, Mazagran, Saffee, and El-Waladia, including their ports and relations with Portuguese, Spanish, Moorish, and European powers. It also connects ancient sites such as Lixus and Shella with Roman, Phoenician, Carthaginian, and Berber history. The text highlights the activities of the Salee rovers and the historical importance of Almanzor and the Bouragrag River.

CHAPTER V. body

The unit describes the imperial cities of Morocco, El-Kesar, Mequinez, Fez, and Morocco, as well as Tafilett, the birthplace of the ruling Shereefian dynasty. It records their locations, populations, defenses, religious and commercial institutions, historical origins, and apparent decline. Fez is presented as the Empire’s principal center of learning and commerce, Mequinez as a fortified royal residence and treasury, Morocco as the southern capital, and Tafilett as a caravan mart and place associated with the Imperial family. The account concludes that the cities and the wider Moorish civilization are declining and may require reconstruction by European hands.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit describes towns, cities, rivers, provinces, tribes, and trade routes in Morocco, especially the Kingdom of Fez and the southern regions of Sous, Draha, and Tafilett. It discusses places including Wazen, Muley Driss, Taza, Oushda, Aghmat, and many smaller settlements, noting their populations, commerce, agriculture, fortifications, and political organization. The text emphasizes the influence and near-independence of Amazirgh, Berber, and Shelouh communities, as well as the economic importance of Jews in many towns. It concludes by questioning existing population estimates and describing the uncertainty surrounding the interior geography of Morocco.

Of the Maroquine Sahara, I have only space to mention the interesting cluster of body

The Maroquine Sahara includes the oasis cluster of Figheegh, also called Figuiq, which consists of twelve villages. The villages lie along the descent of Wal-el-Khalouf and depend on wells and regulated water for their gardens, where date-palms, fruit trees, madder-root, tobacco, and barley are cultivated. The villages frequently fight over water, despite having commercial relations with Fez, Tafilett, and Touat. The people of Figheegh are generally prosperous through commerce, industry, and mining as well as agriculture.

CHAPTER VII. body

The narrator describes tensions involving London Jew-boys, Moors, and the British Consul at Mogador, including the imprisonment of a Jewish youth after he cursed a Moor. He visits Saneeates-Sultan, also called the Garden of the Emperor, and describes the barren landscape, the Argan tree, and legends of the Argan forests. In a further interview, the Governor of Mogador says that the Sultan of Morocco will not accept an anti-slavery appeal except under superior force. The narrator also records Moorish opinions that abolition might have gained more support through stronger official representation, gifts, or influence from Tunis and Constantinople.

CHAPTER VIII. body

The unit describes El-Jereed, the Tunisian Sahara, its salt lake El-Sibhah, and its dependence on dates and the date-palm. It explains how date-palms are propagated, cultivated, irrigated, tapped for sap, and used for food, drink, building, and crafts. It also portrays palm-groves, date production and trade, and the importance of tribute collected by the Bey of the Camp. The unit concludes by introducing a forthcoming Tour to the Jereed, including accounts of Toser, Nefta, and Ghafsa.

CHAPTER IX. body

Captain Balfour and Richard Reade joined Sidi Mohammed, Bey of the Camp, on an expedition through the Jereed toward Kairwan. The Bey's camp included Mamelukes, Turkish Infantry, Spahis, Arab camp followers, artillery, attendants, and water-carriers, and followed a formal daily order of march. The tourists observed Tunisian customs, justice, saints' shrines, poor water supplies, ruined bridges, and the severe-looking but often merely formal bastinado. They also hunted partridges, hares, gazelles, wild boar, foxes, wolves, and hyaenas across plains and hills.

Rode to Kairwan. body

R. and his party rode to Kairwan, where they observed the city, its religious figures, the Kaed's household, and the fortress. They encountered Sidi Amour Abeda, a revered but reportedly fanatical Moorish saint, and learned about local beliefs concerning saints and holy places. The Bey of Tunis administered justice, seized camels for government use, inspected Arabs, and received or distributed animals and hunting birds. R. hunted gazelles, was involved in the punishment of an Arab who insulted him, and later secured the man's release from bastinadoing.

The expedition and camp traveled through the desert from Gilma and Wady Tuckah toward Ghafsa, encountering ancient ruins, saline plains, springs, and desert wildlife. The Bey directed the movement of water, received horses and other gifts, hunted, and was accompanied by increasing numbers of Arabs. At Ghafsa, the travelers examined hot springs containing fish and snakes, the ruined Kasbah, local oil-making, domestic life, and surrounding gardens. The account also describes hunting with hawks and dogs, the use of camels, and the neglected agricultural potential of the region.

CHAPTER X. body

The travelers reach Toser in the Jereed, describing its mud-and-brick houses, date gardens, inhabitants, crafts, customs, and the Bey’s administration. They visit Nefta, a larger oasis settlement nearer the Sahara, and observe its saints, waterworks, commerce, and social organization. Sheikh Tahid is imprisoned and repeatedly bastinadoed because the Bey’s tribute is not ready, while the travelers witness other punishments, diversions, and snake-charming performances. They also travel to Wedyen, attempt unsuccessfully to hunt deer, and view the Sahara’s salt flats and mirages.

The Sheikh Tahib's gardens were sold for 15,000 piastres, his wife also added to body

Sheikh Tahib's gardens were sold for 15,000 piastres, and his wife added 1,000 more, after which he was set at liberty. Dates were being harvested in the Jereed, with several varieties described, and their superior ripeness was attributed to only one day of rain during the past year. Moorish feastday entertainment included masked men performing lascivious and indecent actions, which the text presents as a negative aspect of the Moors. Sultan Kaed of the Hammama died at an extremely old age, while Sheikh Ali brought in two loteds after being ordered by the Bey to return with some.

The tourists’ return march to Tunis passed through Sheesheeah, Ghortabah, Ghafsa, Byrlafee, Sidi-Mahammedeah, and Momakeeah, amid severe heat, cold, wind, rain, hail, locusts, and heavy losses of men and animals. Santa Maria arrived safely from Ghabs, and the text records information about Ghabs, Toser, the Jereed, ancient ruins, waterworks, and the tribute collected there. After reaching Tunis, the tourists observed the Bardo, discussed their Boab’s account of marriage and divorce, and received or brought home various Jereed objects and animals. The narrator then describes leaving Mogador aboard the Renshaw schooner and enduring a month-long sea voyage marked by successive gales before reaching England.

APPENDIX. appendix

The unit describes the French bombardment and capture of Mogador in August 1844, during which the Moorish authorities and much of the population fled and wild tribes sacked and burned the city. British and other European residents, who had been detained by Sidi Abdallah Deleero, escaped through great danger with assistance from Mr. Lucas and others. It also records the destruction suffered by the Jews and inhabitants of Mogador and summarizes commerce at Tangier, Mogador, Rabat, and Mazagan, including imports, exports, shipping, and trade restrictions.

notes notes

The notes discuss the political and religious distinction between the Shereefs and Marabouts, describing the Shereefs as combining regal and sacerdotal authority and the Marabouts as a priestly family. They defend Morocco and the Moors of Barbary against a report of human sacrifice, while recounting the cruelty of Yezeed. The text also describes the peoples, cities, geography, and historical traditions of Barbary, including the Berbers, Algiers, Tunis, Fez, Tangier, the Atlantic System, the Atlas Mountains, and the Sahara.
