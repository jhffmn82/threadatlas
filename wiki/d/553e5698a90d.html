Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · 54 sections

Abstract written by a model

The document is a sequence of elegiac love letters and love poems attributed to P. Ovidius Naso. It begins with Heroides, letters in which women, and later men, address absent, faithless, endangered, or desired partners. Its central concerns are separation, betrayal, erotic desire, marriage, abandonment, danger, and the destructive pressure of love. The Heroides opens with Penelope’s faithful appeal to Ulysses amid intrusive suitors, then moves through complaints and pleas by Phyllis, Briseis, Phaedra, Oenone, Hypsipyle, Dido, Hermione, Deianira, Ariadne, Canace, Medea, Laodamia, Hypermestra, and Sappho. These writers recall promises, wars, forced marriages, rival women, lost homes, children, and impending death; several threaten or resolve upon suicide. The sequence also includes exchanges: Paris pursues Helen, while Helen resists yet admits attraction and fear; Leander and Hero long to meet across dangerous waters; and Acontius seeks to enforce Cydippe’s oath, while Cydippe condemns his deceit before yielding to the bond that Diana appears to enforce. The document then introduces Amores, presented as a reduced collection of three books. In Liber Primus, Cupid turns the poet from epic into elegy. The speaker pursues a beloved woman, especially Corinna, through secret messages, banquets, closed doors, sexual encounters, quarrels, and refusals. Dipsas instructs a girl to exploit wealthy lovers, while the poet insists that poetry, service, and devotion should matter more than payment. Liber Secundus expands the unstable lover’s world: he claims to be unable to resist women, suspects and commits infidelity, mourns Corinna’s parrot, loves two women at once, fears Corinna’s sea voyage and pregnancy, sends a ring, and argues about jealousy, guardianship, and poetic vocation. Love repeatedly diverts him from epic and tragedy. In Liber Tertius, Elegy and Tragedy debate the poet’s future, but he continues erotic poetry. The poems treat false oaths, adultery, jealous husbands, delays caused by a river, the low social value of poetry, Tibullus’s death and poetic survival, painful attachment, Corinna’s public notoriety, local cult and homeland, and the speaker’s sexual failure. Across the whole document, letters and poems portray love as compelling, creative, comic, jealous, and often fatal.

P. OVIDI NASONIS HEROIDES / i body

Penelope writes to Ulysses, urging him to return and lamenting that he has sent no reply. She recalls her fears during the Trojan War and rejoices that Troy has fallen and Ulysses survived, but his continued absence leaves her uncertain and distressed. Suitors from Dulichium, Samos, and Zacynthus occupy Ulysses's hall, consume his resources, and pressure Penelope to remarry. Penelope remains faithful to Ulysses while protecting Telemachus and caring for the aged Laertes.

II body

Phyllis writes to Demophoon from Rhodope, complaining that he has not returned after promising to come within a stated time. She recalls how she welcomed him, aided him, gave him gifts and her virginity, and trusted his words, tears, oaths, and promises of marriage. She watches the sea for his ships, imagines excuses for his delay, and fears that he now has another wife. In despair, Phyllis threatens suicide and declares that Demophoon will be named on her tomb as the cause of her death.

III body

Briseis writes to Achilles, pleading with him to reclaim her after Agamemnon demanded her return. She accuses Achilles of delaying her restoration and refusing the gifts offered by Agamemnon, while recalling the destruction of her home and the deaths of her family and husband. She urges Achilles to end his anger, return to battle against Hector, and allow her to accompany him. Briseis declares that without him she would rather die, but asks him to preserve the life he once gave her and order her to come with him.

IV body

Phaedra writes to Hippolytus, confessing that Love has overcome her modesty and caused her to desire him. She presents her late and intense passion as a fate inherited from her family, citing Europa, Pasiphae, and Ariadne. Phaedra praises Hippolytus's appearance and hunting skills, urges him to abandon his exclusive devotion to Diana, and asks him to return her love. She also attacks Theseus for neglecting their marriage and wronging both her and Hippolytus, then begs Hippolytus to accept her despite their relationship as stepmother and stepson.

Oenone Paridi 2 body

Oenone writes to Paris, recalling their former love and accusing him of abandoning her for Helen. She describes Paris’s departure by ship, his return with Helen, and the grief caused by seeing the new wife cling to him. Oenone argues that Helen’s former abduction and infidelity make her untrustworthy, while Oenone herself has remained faithful. She warns that Paris’s choice will bring war and offers her own healing knowledge, though she cannot cure her love for him.

VI body

Hypsipyle writes to Jason after hearing that he has returned safely from his voyage with the Golden Fleece and has taken Medea as a wife. She recalls their marriage on Lemnos, Jason’s promises, his departure, and the birth of their twin sons. She condemns Medea’s magic and betrayal, contrasts Medea with herself, and urges Jason to return to her and the children. She ends by wishing suffering on Jason and Medea for abandoning her.

VII body

Dido addresses Aeneas as he prepares to leave Carthage for Italy, accusing him of abandoning her and breaking his promises. She recalls how she received Aeneas, gave him power in Carthage, and entered into a love affair with him, but now fears that he will abandon her while she is pregnant. Dido urges him to remain, warning that the sea is dangerous and that faithlessness will bring punishment. She ultimately prepares for suicide, describing the sword in her lap and asking Anna to give her final funeral offerings.

Dido writes to Aeneas, asking him to delay his departure because the sea, his weary comrades, and his damaged fleet require rest. She appeals to their past kindnesses, his possible debt to her, and her hope of wedlock, while asking for time to calm her love and endure her sorrow. If Aeneas refuses, she threatens to take her own life, describing the sword in her lap and the tears falling upon it. She asks her sister Anna to perform the final rites and declares that Aeneas provided both the cause of her death and the sword.

VIII body

Hermione writes to Orestes that Pyrrhus (Neoptolemus) holds her captive and has dragged her into his palace despite her resistance. She urges Orestes to reclaim her, arguing that he is both her husband and her kinsman, while Pyrrhus has no rightful claim superior to theirs. Hermione recalls her unhappy childhood, her mother's absence, and the destruction of Troy, which left her as Pyrrhus's captive. She declares that she will either die young or become Orestes' wife, and identifies the suffering of Tantalid women as an inherited family curse.

IX body

Deianira writes to Hercules after hearing that he has conquered Oechalia but yielded to Iole, whom he may take as his wife. She contrasts Hercules's former labours and victories with his humiliation under Iole and recalls his association with Omphale. After Hercules is reportedly dying from the poisoned cloak she sent, Deianira blames herself, resolves to die, and bids farewell to her family and Hercules.

X body

Ariadne writes to Theseus from the shore where he has abandoned her while she slept. She describes waking alone, seeing Theseus’s ship sail away, and unsuccessfully signaling and calling for him. She recalls aiding Theseus with the thread in the Labyrinth and reproaches him for breaking his promise to remain hers. Alone on the island, Ariadne fears death, burial without rites, and the dangers of the sea, beasts, and strangers.

XI body

Canace writes to her brother Macareus while holding a pen in one hand and a sword sent by Aeolus in the other. She recounts their incestuous love, her pregnancy and childbirth, the nurse's failed attempt to conceal the infant, and Aeolus's order that the child be exposed to wild beasts. Aeolus then sends Canace a sword as punishment, and she announces that she will kill herself and asks Macareus to bury her with their infant son.

XII body

Medea recalls how she used her magic to help Jason win the golden fleece and escape Colchis, abandoning her father, homeland, and family for him. Jason later orders Medea to leave his palace because he is marrying Creusa, and Medea describes her grief at the wedding procession. She declares that Creusa will suffer through the poison and fire Medea has prepared, and that no enemy of Medea will remain unpunished.

Quodsi forte preces praecordia ferrea tangunt, body

The speaker pleads with her husband to restore the marriage bed and reminds him that their two children resemble him and may suffer under a cruel stepmother. She appeals to the gods, her services to him, and their children, saying that she once helped him and became the mother of his children with him. She claims that the golden ram, the Grecian youth, and her husband's survival and prosperity constitute her dowry. Her anger is preparing great threats, though she does not yet state what punishment she will inflict.

XIII body

Laodamia writes to Protesilaus after his departure for Troy, describing her grief, anxiety, prayers, and longing for his return. She warns him to avoid danger in the Trojan War, especially the prophecy concerning the first Danaan to land at Troy, and urges him to fight cautiously and come home unwounded. She imagines the wives of Troy preparing their husbands for battle and keeps a wax image of Protesilaus as a substitute for him. She vows to follow Protesilaus wherever he is called, whether he survives or not, and closes by urging him to care for himself if he cares for her.

XIV body

Hypermestra writes to Lynceus from prison after refusing to kill him during the massacre in which her sisters killed their bridegrooms. She describes raising the sword three times, but fear and piety prevented her from striking, so she warned Lynceus to escape before dawn. Danaus discovered that Lynceus alone survived, had Hypermestra dragged away, and imprisoned her. Hypermestra asks Lynceus either to rescue her or to kill and bury her, affirming that she does not repent her piety.

XV body

Sappho writes to Phaon about his departure and her continuing love for him. She recalls their intimacy, praises his beauty, and says that neither the women of Lesbos nor her poetry now gives her pleasure. A Naiad tells Sappho to seek the Leucadian cliff, where Deucalion was freed from love by leaping into the sea. Sappho resolves to make the leap and dedicate her lyre to Phoebus if it cures her passion.

Cur tamen Actiacas miseram me mittis ad oras, 185 body

Sappho asks Phaon why he sends her toward the shores of Actium when he could return himself. She says that Phaon would heal her better than the Leucadian waters and fears that, if she dies, he will bear responsibility for her death. Sappho claims that grief has silenced her lyre and weakened her poetic genius, and she asks the Lesbian women to help bring Phaon back. She urges Phaon to release his ship, return without delay, and understand that his absence has caused her suffering.

XVI body

Paris writes to Helen, declaring his love and asking her to receive him kindly. He recounts how Venus promised him Helen after he judged Venus, Juno, and Pallas, and how this promise led him to sail from Troy to Lacedaemon. Paris describes seeing Helen, finding her beauty greater than fame had proclaimed, and placing her above the rewards offered by Juno and Pallas. He promises that only death will end his love and asks Helen not to let his hopes fail.

nun ego coniugium generosae degener opto, body

Paris urges Helen to accept him as her husband, claiming that his Trojan ancestry, wealth, beauty, and martial abilities make him a worthy match for her. He describes his intense love and jealousy, especially when he watches Helen with Menelaus, and asks her to meet him secretly. Paris promises Helen honor and luxury in Troy, argues that her abduction will not necessarily provoke war, and boasts that Troy and Asia can withstand the Greeks. He concludes that either Helen should leave with him peacefully or the Greek forces will yield to his strength.

XVII body

Helen replies to Paris's letter, condemning him for violating hospitality and attempting to win a lawful wife away from her husband. She defends her honor, contrasts her conduct with the assaults of Theseus, and questions whether Paris's love is genuine. Helen acknowledges Paris's beauty and flattering advances but insists that she will preserve her reputation and remain with Menelaus. She also challenges Paris's claims about his ancestry, the Judgment of Paris, and Venus's promise that she would become his bride.

Prima mea est igitur Veneri plaeuisse voluptas ; body

Helen writes to Paris about her attraction to him and her struggle between desire and fear. She warns that Menelaus's absence does not make their affair safe, since his reputation, authority, and suspicions still constrain her. Helen fears public disgrace, retaliation from Sparta and Troy, and the dangers of following Paris to Troy, while Paris's past abandonment of Oenone makes her doubt his constancy. She postpones a secret meeting and ends the letter, leaving further discussion to Clymene and Aethra.

XVIII.. body

Leander of Abydos sends Hero of Sestos a letter because violent winds and rough seas prevent him from swimming to her. He recalls his earlier crossings of the Hellespont, when Hero welcomed him, dried him, and shared the night with him. He compares his love for Hero to a guiding light and vows to attempt the crossing again, even if death ends his troubled love.

183 Am I, then, never to embrace you except when body

The speaker longs to embrace the addressee and questions whether the sea must govern their meetings. He promises to cross the swollen sea even in stormy weather, trusting either to survive through daring or to end his anxious love in death. He hopes to be cast onto the addressee’s shores, where the addressee will mourn him and acknowledge responsibility for his death. He asks for a brief calm during the crossing, but wishes winter to continue once he reaches the addressee’s shore, while his letter remains with the addressee until he arrives.

XIX body

Hero writes to Leander, urging him to come because every delay postpones their joy and she cannot patiently endure love. She describes her nights of waiting, watching the Hellespont, lighting a beacon, imagining his swim, and fearing that his love may wander or that another woman may keep him away. The stormy sea prevents Leander's swimming voyage, so Hero appeals to Neptune, recalls his past boldness, and proposes that they meet midway if necessary. Auspicious signs from the lamp, the nurse, and the weather give Hero hope that Leander will return.

165 Perhaps you fear the time may fail you for body

The girl urges Leander to meet her in mid-sea if he cannot make the full journey, while lamenting that their secret love is opposed by fear of public speech. She warns him that swimming is dangerous even for ships and begs him to attempt it only in calm waters. She recounts a dream in which a dolphin is cast onto the sand and dies, treating it as an omen of danger. She asks Leander to spare himself for her sake and sends a letter to ease the delay while the sea remains impassable.

XX body

Acontius writes to Cydippe, urging her to keep the oath she made to marry him after reading his apple-inscription in Diana's temple. He argues that his deception was motivated by love and that Diana witnessed and approved Cydippe's words. He warns that Diana causes Cydippe's recurring illness whenever she tries to break her oath, while also expressing jealousy of anyone who attends her during sickness. Acontius threatens further schemes or even force, but presents himself as willing to endure Cydippe's anger and devoted to winning her as his wife.

nam quod habes et tu gemini verba altera pacti, 155 body

Acontius argues that Cydippe’s oath to him, sworn before Diana, binds her more closely than her father’s promise to another suitor. He warns that the rival suitor’s pursuit has caused Cydippe’s illness and placed her under Diana’s suspicion, while keeping the oath would restore both their safety. Acontius urges Cydippe to tell her mother how they met, how the apple conveyed his written oath, and why she should marry him. He promises that, if she does so, a golden image of the apple will be dedicated at Delos.

XXI body

Cydippe writes to Acontius while suffering an unexplained illness that threatens her approaching marriage. She recalls how, during her voyage to Delos, Acontius deceived her into reading an oath written on an apple and thereby nearly swearing to marry him. She blames Acontius and Diana for her suffering, while Acontius and Cydippe's suitor remain in rivalry over her. Cydippe argues that Acontius should have sought her consent honestly rather than compelled her through deceit.

Cum tamen haec dixi, cum me tibi firma negavi, body

Heroides XXI presents the speaker’s letter to Acontius after his deceptive written oath has bound her to him. She explains that Diana’s anger seems to cause her recurring illness and the repeated failure of her weddings, while her intended husband grows suspicious of her rejection of him. The speaker asks Acontius to seek her recovery, admits that she now wishes to be united with him, and ends by yielding to the oath and bidding him farewell. The unit then introduces the manuscripts, editions, and literary character of Ovid’s Amores.

P. OVIDI NASONIS AMORES / LIBER PRIMUS body

P. Ovidius Naso presents the Amores as a reduced collection of three books and begins by describing how Cupid redirected his intended epic poetry into elegy. The speaker acknowledges that Cupid has wounded him and submits to Love, while asking a beloved woman to accept his devotion and inspire his poetry. At a banquet, the speaker anxiously instructs the beloved woman how to communicate secretly and avoid intimacy with her husband. Later, Corinna visits the speaker, removes her clothing after a feigned resistance, and they have sex.

VI body

The unit concerns a lover who spends the night begging a janitor to open a barred door so that he may reach his mistress. Love gives the lover courage to move secretly at night, but the janitor repeatedly refuses, and the lover's pleas, threats, and appeals to past gratitude fail. The lover then blames anger and violence against the mistress, regretting that he struck and disfigured her before begging forgiveness. At dawn, he remains remorseful and asks that his own hands be punished.

VIII body

Dipsas, an old bawd skilled in magic, counsels a young woman on how to attract and exploit wealthy lovers. She tells the young woman to use feigned modesty, rivalry, tears, deception, and repeated demands for gifts and payment. The narrator and poet-lover overhears the speech while hidden behind doors and nearly attacks Dipsas in anger. Dipsas presents the wealthy young lover as a desirable source of money, while dismissing the narrator's poetry as inadequate compensation.

IX body

The unit compares the lover with the soldier, presenting both as sleepless, enduring, watchful, and capable of overcoming danger for the sake of love or war. It says that love is not idleness and that the lover is driven to action by Venus and by the beloved. It then contrasts unbought love with love sought for payment, arguing that Venus, sexual pleasure, and gifts should not be treated as commercial transactions. The speaker condemns women who demand payment while allowing gifts from wealthy lovers and praises the poor lover's service, devotion, and poetry.

XI body

Naso asks Nape to deliver wax tablets to Corinna and ensure that she reads and answers them promptly. Nape is described as skilled in arranging hair, serving during secret nighttime activities, and giving signals, and as someone who has often helped Naso. The message says that Naso lives in hope of a night with Corinna and asks her to come. Naso would dedicate the tablets to Venus if Corinna wrote only “Come!” in reply.

XII body

The speaker laments tablets that return from the beloved woman bearing a refusal, then curses the tablets and the tree from which they were made. He reproaches Aurora for ending his night with the beloved, while praising the pleasures of sleep and intimacy. He mourns the beloved woman’s damaged hair, attributes its loss to her own treatment, and consoles her that it can be replaced. Finally, he defends poetry against Envy, claiming that poetic fame outlasts rulers, monuments, and death.

LIBER SECUNDUS body

Ovid presents himself as a poet of love whose elegies are composed at Amor’s command and intended for lovers. He recalls abandoning grand epic subjects when his beloved mistress closed her door, after which song softened the barrier and brought her to him. He urges the mistress’s guardian to permit a discreet affair, arguing that secrecy will benefit the guardian and that jealous surveillance harms everyone. He also confesses that he is attracted to women of every kind and cannot control his continual inclination to love.

V body

The speaker/lover accuses the beloved girl of infidelity after secretly watching her exchange coded signals and kisses with another man at a drinking party. He angrily claims that her kisses belong to him and asks why a third person shares them. When the girl blushes, looks down, and appears sorrowful, his anger collapses into supplication, and he begs her for kisses. Her new kisses please and torment him because he suspects another man taught her how to give them.

VI body

The unit first mourns the death of Corinna’s parrot, praising its Indian origin, mimicry, loyalty to a turtle-dove, and final cry of “Corinna, vale!” It then presents the speaker’s defenses against Corinna’s jealousy, especially her accusation that he has slept with Cypassis. The speaker later admits that he did have an affair with Cypassis and threatens to reveal its details if she refuses him. The final poems portray Cupid and love as forces that repeatedly restore the speaker’s desire, including his simultaneous love for two women.

X body

The unit presents the speaker’s divided love for two beautiful women, his preference for a life consumed by erotic love, and his wish to die during its pleasures. It then describes his anxiety over Corinna’s intended sea voyage and his hopes for her safe return. The speaker celebrates gaining Corinna as a triumph achieved without military aid. Finally, he prays for Corinna’s survival during a dangerous pregnancy and condemns abortion as harmful to women and unborn children.

XV body

The speaker sends a ring to his fair beloved and wishes that she will welcome it, wear it, and feel his love conveyed with it. He imagines becoming the ring so that he could touch her body, remain on her finger, and fall into her bosom. In Sulmo, the Paelignian country is fertile, watered, and healthy, but the speaker finds no comfort there because his beloved is absent. He declares that any journey would be easy with her, while separation makes even his native land seem as harsh as Scythia, Cilicia, Britain, or the rocks stained with Prometheus’s blood.

XVII body

The speaker defends serving a beautiful woman and argues that Corinna’s beauty makes her arrogant and haughty. He compares unequal lovers and verse, asking his mistress to accept him on whatever terms she chooses, while declaring that only she will be sung in his books. He explains to Macer that Love has diverted him from epic and tragic poetry toward love poetry and letters. In addressing a husband, he claims that jealousy, refusal, and obstacles strengthen love, and warns that the husband’s excessive tolerance may cause Corinna to cease being his.

LIBER TERTIUS body

The poet encounters Elegy and Tragedy in a sacred grove, where they argue over the kind of poetry he should write. Tragedy urges him to abandon love poetry for heroic subjects, while Elegy defends her influence over the poet and over Corinna. The poet asks Tragedy to allow him time to continue writing about love, and Tragedy agrees. At a circus, the poet courts a woman who favors one of the charioteers, invokes Venus for success in love, and receives an encouraging smile and implied promise from the woman.

III body

The speaker complains that a faithless girl has broken her oath without losing her beauty, while the gods punish men but seem to tolerate beautiful women who swear falsely. He argues that guarding a wife cannot make her faithful, since desire is strengthened by prohibition, and advises the hard husband to indulge his wife instead. He then recounts a dream in which a white heifer is disturbed by a crow, leaves her bull, and joins other bulls, and the nocturnal dream augur interprets this as a sign of adultery. The unit presents beauty, female chastity, divine punishment, and forbidden desire as forces that shape human and divine behavior.

VI body

The narrator hurries to reach his lady-love but is stopped by a swollen, unnamed river, which he reproaches for delaying him. He recalls rivers that aided or experienced love, especially Anio, who sees Ilia, rescues her from drowning, and takes her as his bride. The narrator then condemns the stream for being a muddy, temporary torrent and wishes it dry. In the next poem, he laments that poetry and the liberal arts have less value than military service and wealth, while a newly rich soldier is preferred to him.

IX body

The unit mourns Tibullus, whose death and funeral provoke grief from Elegy, Venus, Delia, Nemesis, his mother, and his sister, while poetry is presented as surviving the body and death. It argues that death can overcome the good and profane sacred things, though Tibullus may live on in poetic fame and in Elysium with other poets. The poem then describes Ceres’s festival and the goddess’s past love for Iasius, which once caused agriculture to fail everywhere except in Crete. Finally, the speaker declares his intention to escape a painful affair, but admits that love still conflicts with his hatred of his beloved woman and may continue to prevail.

XII body

Ovid says that his books of verse have made Corinna known to many and have turned her into a woman shared by others. He asks Corinna to conceal her infidelity and deny what he sees, because discovering or hearing her confess her affairs causes him intense distress. The poems also describe Falerii’s annual rites to Juno and trace them to Halaesus, while Ovid celebrates Sulmo and his Paelignian origin. In another episode, Ovid suffers sexual impotence with a willing girl, despite having recently performed successfully with Chlide, Pitho, Libas, and Corinna.
