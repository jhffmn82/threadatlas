An Astronomer's Wife

Angelo Hall · 1908 · 20 sections

Abstract written by a model

An Astronomer’s Wife, by Angelo Hall, is a biography of his mother, Chloe Angeline Stickney Hall, written for his daughter Peggy and the public. It follows Angeline from her impoverished childhood in Rodman, Jefferson County, through education, marriage, motherhood, reform commitments, and death. Her ancestry is traced through the Cook and Stickney families; her father, Theophilus Stickney, abandoned the family when she was young and died in 1842, leaving her mother and daughters in hardship. Angeline endured domestic labor, illness, and poverty but pursued study at Rodman Union Seminary, became a teacher, and developed interests in science, history, writing, religion, abolition, and women’s rights. Determined to attend college, she earned money through teaching, painting, and making tissue-paper flowers before studying at McGrawville and New York Central College. There she excelled, taught mathematics, embraced abolition, woman suffrage, and the bloomer costume, wrote prose and poetry, and graduated with an A.B. despite financial strain and illness. The book then introduces Asaph Hall 3rd, a former carpenter with self-developed mathematical ability, who met Angeline at McGrawville. Their affectionate courtship led to marriage in April 1856. The couple lived in Ann Arbor, taught at Shalersville Institute in Ohio, and moved to Cambridge in 1857 so Asaph could pursue astronomy. Angeline sustained their household, taught him German, encouraged his work, and participated in intellectual and political life, although their marriage included disputes about suffrage, poetry, wages, and dress. During the Civil War, they moved to Washington, where Asaph joined the U.S. Naval Observatory, observed Mars, and rose to a professorship; Angeline managed the family, aided friends and relatives, and supported his career. At their Georgetown home, Angeline raised Asaph, Sam, Angelo, and Percival with discipline, education, gardening, and moral care; all four sons graduated from Harvard College. She continued studying history, philosophy, genealogy, poetry, science, and religion, gradually moving from her mother’s theology toward Unitarianism and a faith reconciled with scientific inquiry. She encouraged Asaph’s search for the satellites of Mars. After recurring illness and lifelong labor, she died of apoplexy at North Andover on July 3, 1892. Later letters, a tribute by Augusta Larned, and an epilogue portray her charity, intellectual vigor, domestic devotion, spiritual generosity, and enduring union with science and Asaph’s astronomical achievements.

An Astronomer's Wife front matter

Angelo Hall introduces An Astronomer’s Wife as a biography of his mother, Angeline Hall, written for his daughter Peggy and the public. The unit traces Angeline’s ancestry through Elisha Cook, Electa Cook, Moses Stickney, and Theophilus Stickney, whose families were connected to the American Revolution and the westward settlement of New York. It describes Theophilus Stickney’s struggles as a carpenter, his eventual intemperance, and the birth of his daughter Chloe Angeline Stickney in Rodman, Jefferson County. Before Angeline was three, Theophilus had left home, and he died among strangers in a Rochester hospital in 1842.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER II. front matter

The unit recounts Chloe Angeline Stickney’s childhood in Rodman, her father’s absence and death, and the hardships faced by her mother and sisters. After Theophilus Stickney’s death, Angeline lived with Charlotte Ingalls in Wilna and later endured demanding labor in her mother Electa Cook’s second household with Milton Woodward and the Woodward children. Despite poverty, domestic work, illness, and difficult winters, Angeline pursued education at the Rodman Union Seminary and developed strong interests in history, science, grammar, arithmetic, music, and writing. Her early compositions on the sugar maple and slavery show her industriousness, developing literary ability, and opposition to slavery.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER IV. front matter

This unit describes Angeline Stickney’s work as a teacher and her continuing education during the 1840s and 1850s. She taught in district schools near Rodman and Cape Vincent, tutored in a family, attended the seminary at Rodman, and later began a college course at McGrawville. Her patience, firmness, and goodness made her an admired teacher, while her religious faith shaped her diary reflections on prayer, illness, death, and salvation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ [Illustration: AN OLD DAGUERREOTYPE] ------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER V. front matter

Angeline Stickney continues pursuing education despite limited money and religious scruples about ambition, choosing McGrawville so Ruth can accompany her. She travels through several towns trying to earn money by teaching monochromatic painting and making tissue-paper flowers, but often struggles to form classes. Her second journal records these journeys, expenses, teaching efforts, discouragements, and eventual arrival at McGrawville in May 1852. In 1853 and 1854, she enters the junior and senior years at New York Central College.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER VI. front matter

New York Central College at McGrawville admitted students regardless of race or sex and served as a center of reform and abolition. Angeline Stickney studied there from 1852 to 1855, excelled academically, taught mathematics, adopted woman suffrage and the bloomer costume, and graduated with an A.B. degree. She and her sister Ruth struggled financially and physically to pursue college education, with Ruth leaving because of poor health and Angeline suffering a breakdown shortly before graduation. The college's students and reformers included idealists whose debates, writings, and later wartime service reflected their political and social commitments.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER VII. front matter

The unit examines Angeline Stickney’s literary productions while she was at Central College, including prose on education, abolition, poetry, religion, nature, and Astronomy. Her writings express intense loyalty to Central College, opposition to the fugitive slave law and slavery, and exalted religious feeling. The unit concludes by introducing Asaph Hall, a carpenter and later astronomer, and describing his distinguished family background and the loss of his inheritance.

notes notes

opening body

Asaph Hall 3rd, born in 1829, grew up helping his widowed mother and worked for eight years as a carpenter before beginning serious study. He developed mathematical ability independently, studied geometry and algebra, and distinguished himself after entering McGrawville. His carpentry experience led him to solve the problem of constructing a house roof scientifically, which helped awaken his ambition to become an architect. In 1854 he traveled to McGrawville to study under its advertised manual-labor system and saw Angeline Stickney when he arrived.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER IX. body

The unit discusses courtship and marriage, especially the relationship between Angeline Stickney and Asaph Hall. It argues that differences in religious faith need not prevent a happy marriage and contrasts Angeline’s fortunate marriage with a friend’s denominationally motivated one. Angeline and Asaph exchanged affectionate letters, endured separation, and planned to marry despite Angeline’s poor health and limited worldly means. They married on a Monday morning in early April 1856 and settled in Ann Arbor, where Asaph pursued his studies and cared for his frail wife.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER X. body

Asaph Hall and Angeline Hall remained in Ann Arbor for about three months, where Professor Brünnow encouraged Asaph to continue his studies and arranged for him to attend college without tuition fees. They then went to Ohio, spending a month with Angeline’s aunt before engaging to teach at the Shalersville Institute from August 6, 1856, until about May 1, 1857. Although teaching was not financially profitable, they were well liked, received a purse of sixty dollars from the townspeople, and found their work personally successful. During this period Asaph joined the Campbellites and was baptized, while Angeline supported him through illness and continued to suffer from poor health.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XI. body

Asaph Hall and his wife, Angeline, moved to Cambridge in 1857 so he could pursue astronomy despite poverty and Angeline’s poor health. Hall worked at the Harvard College Observatory for low wages, supplemented his income with observations and computations, and gradually earned recognition and a higher salary. Angeline supported his studies through domestic economy, teaching him German, and encouraging his scientific ambitions while her health improved. The couple also became involved in Cambridge’s intellectual and political life on the eve of the Civil War.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XII. body

This unit describes the marriage of Angeline Hall and Asaph Hall, including their quarrels over woman suffrage, poetry, wages, and the bloomer costume. After these conflicts, their household settled into a division of labor, with Asaph Hall concentrating on astronomy and Angeline Hall on domestic responsibilities. From 1859 to 1862, the family lived at the Bond cottage on Observatory Hill in Cambridge, where they kept a vegetable garden and welcomed the birth of Asaph Hall, Jr. The unit also recounts Angeline Hall’s visits with relatives and her growing interest in the Stickney family history.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XIII. body

During the Civil War, Asaph Hall joined the U.S. Naval Observatory in Washington and eventually became a professor there. He and his wife, Angeline S. Hall, endured battles, disease, family losses, and repeated threats to the city while caring for wounded friends and relatives. Hall observed Mars, served in Washington’s defenses during the Confederate threat in 1864, and later showed Abraham Lincoln objects through the Observatory telescope. After the war, Hall devoted the rest of his life to science, supported by Angeline’s management of their household and advocacy for his and George W. Hill’s careers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XIV. body

This unit describes the Hall family’s home at 18 Gay Street in Georgetown, where Angeline Hall raised Asaph, Sam, Angelo, and Percival. It details the house, gardens, household routines, moral training, education, illnesses, work, and recreation of the Hall boys. Angeline Hall is portrayed as a devoted, disciplined mother whose sacrifices helped all four sons graduate with distinction from Harvard College. The unit concludes with her love of flowers and her poem “ASPIRATION,” which expresses the soul’s desire to transform hope into beauty.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XV. body

This unit portrays Angeline Hall as an intellectually gifted, morally courageous woman who combined support for women’s education with devoted work as a wife, mother, homemaker, and aid to the poor. Her religious faith developed from her mother’s narrow theology toward Unitarianism, scientific inquiry, and a search for a renewed form of faith. She encouraged Professor Hall’s astronomical work, including his search for the satellites of Mars, while also pursuing history, philosophy, poetry, and family genealogy. After years of demanding labor and recurring illness, Angeline Hall died of apoplexy at North Andover, Massachusetts, on July 3, 1892.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XVI. body

This unit presents letters written mainly by Angeline Hall, also signing as C. A. S. Hall, to Asaph Hall, her sister Mary, and her sons Samuel, Angelo, and Percival. The letters describe family health, births and deaths, household work, gardening, travel, education, college life, and visits among relatives in New York, Washington, D.C., and elsewhere. Angeline writes about Asaph Hall’s astronomical work, her own planned work on Prometheus Unbound, and the relationship between philosophy, ethical teaching, and science. The letters are introduced as evidence of the astronomer’s wife’s everyday life and character.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XVII. body

Augusta Larned’s tribute honors an unnamed woman whose spiritual sweetness, charity, justice, and unselfishness made her the center of an ideal home. She welcomed people regardless of social position, supported the poor from her own limited means, and saw the good in every soul. A devoted student of nature and a seeker after truth, she reconciled science and religion while continuing her daily duties as mother, wife, and bread-giver. As her strength silently failed, she prepared for death and finally died peacefully, as if gathered into the Everlasting Arms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ EPILOGUE. body

The land from which the speaker's mother came is described as lying along an amber Adirondack river flowing to blue Ontario, with rock-maples, wheat fields, and bitter northern winters. The mother came from this land to college, where she questioned the future years and learned under the light of her lamp. Science became her bridegroom, and together they loved and learned. The mother's husband found and gave her the moons of Mars.
