The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497 · 67 sections

The document is a collection of Pindar’s victory odes, Olympian, Pythian, Nemean, and Isthmian, followed by fragments of other song types. Its central purpose is to celebrate athletic victors, their families, cities, patrons, gods, and inherited renown. The poems repeatedly present victory as the product of personal excellence, training, wealth and generosity, divine favor, and public song, which preserves fame beyond a single contest. The Olympian odes begin with Hieron of Syracuse’s horse-race victory through Pherenikos and the myth of Pelops, rejecting the tale that Tantalos served Pelops to the gods. They honor figures including Theron of Akragas, Psaumis of Kamarina, Agesias, Diagoras of Rhodes, Alkimedon, Epharmostos, Agesidamos, Ergoteles, Xenophon, and Asopichos. These songs connect individual triumphs to cities, ancestors, gods, and myths such as Herakles’ founding of the Olympic games. The Pythian odes again prominently honor Hieron, as well as Arkesilas of Kyrene, Xenokrates, Megakles, Aristomenes, Telesikrates, Hippokleas, Thrasydaios, and Midas. Their narratives range through Typhon, Ixion, Asklepios, Jason and Medea, Perseus, and Orestes. They also offer counsel on truthful rule, moderation, repaying favors, political restoration, mortality, and the instability of fortune. The Nemean and Isthmian odes celebrate many victors, especially men of Aigina and Thebes, including Chromios, Timodemos, Aristokleides, Timasarchos, Pytheas, Alkimidas, Sogenes, Deinis, Theaios, Aristagoras, Herodotos, Melissos, Phylakidas, Strepsiades, and Kleandros. Aigina’s Aiakid lineage, Aiakos, Peleus, Telamon, Achilles, and Aias, recurs strongly, alongside Herakles and other heroic stories. Several poems warn against envy, slander, excess, tyranny, and ambitions beyond mortal limits. The closing fragments classify Pindar’s remaining work and preserve songs for Dionysos, Delos, and Thebes, plus dirges describing the blessed dead, judgment, atonement, and the survival of a shadow of life after bodily death. The document ends with “THE END.”

OLYMPIAN ODES. / I. body

The ode celebrates Hieron of Syracuse, who won the horse-race at Olympia with his horse Pherenikos. It praises the Olympian games as the greatest of contests and recounts the story of Pelops, their founder. Pelops wins Hippodameia from Oinomaos with Poseidon’s aid, while the ode rejects the tale that Tantalos served Pelops’s flesh to the gods. The speaker praises Hieron’s justice, wealth, excellence, and hope for further glory.

notes notes

The Olympic games were sacred to Zeus, and a horse won a race for Hieron. The notes discuss Pelops and the legend that his ivory shoulder replaced flesh eaten by the gods at Tantalos' table. Pindar rejects that legend and explains that people may believe an untrue tale because of its beautiful presentation, although such tales do not endure.

II. body

The poem celebrates Theron of Akragas for winning the four-horse chariot-race and praises him as a god-fearing, generous, and civic-minded ruler. It honors Akragas and its ancestors, whose prosperity and fame are linked to their long-established settlement in Sicily. The poem invokes Zeus to protect Theron's native land and reflects on divine favor, changing fortune, and the fate of the dead. It describes judgment after death, punishment for the guilty, and a blessed existence for the virtuous before returning to praise Theron and his generosity.

notes notes

The notes state that Theron traced his descent from the son of Polyneikes. They identify Pindar's views on future life as being especially preserved in surviving fragments of his Dirges. Pindar seems to have been influenced by Pythagoreanism in these ideas.

III. body

The ode celebrates Theron's victory in the chariot-race and his Olympian crown at Akragas. It was sung at the Theoxenia feast, which Theron gave in the name of the Dioskouroi, Tyndareus' sons. The poem recounts how Herakles brought the olive tree from the land of Ister for Zeus's precinct and established the Olympic games, including the fifth-year feast. It concludes that Theron and the Emmenidai have attained the furthest limit of mortal achievement through their victory and devotion to the Dioskouroi.

notes notes

The notes explain that the Dorian invasion of Peloponnesos was associated with Oxylos, who was said to be one of its leaders. Oxylos, of Elean descent but living in Aitolia, became king of Elis after the invasion. The judge at the Olympic games was thought to be descended from Oxylos or from an Aitolian who came with him. The Olympic games were held at the full moon in the middle of Hekatombaion, and Herakles was understood to have wanted to institute them at that favorable time.

IV. body

This ode concerns Psaumis of Kamarina and his victory in the mule-chariot-race at Olympia in 452. It praises Psaumis for his horsemanship, hospitality, love of peace, and devotion to his city. The ode invokes Zeus, asks for favor in future prayers, and presents the victory as an enduring sign of valor. It was apparently written immediately after the victory for singing beneath the moon by Psaumis's returning companions.

notes notes

The unit contains two footnotes. One directs the reader to the introduction to Pythian ix. The other suggests that Psaumis probably had grey hair.

V. notes

The ode celebrates Psaumis of Kamarina as winner of the mule-chariot race and was intended for performance after his return home. Psaumis is praised for honoring Kamarina through sacrifices, contests, and the public display of his Olympian victory, while also glorifying his father Akron and the restored city. The poem invokes Kamarina, Pallas, Zeus, and local rivers and places, and praises the city’s rebuilding and prosperity. It ends by asking Zeus to grant Psaumis a serene old age with his sons and advising contentment with wealth and good repute.

VI. notes

The ode celebrates Agesias of Syracuse, a citizen of both Syracuse and Stymphalos, who won the mule-chariot race at Olympia. It traces his descent from the Iamid clan through the story of Iamos, the prophetic son of Euadne and Apollo, whose descendants became renowned seers at Olympia. The poem praises Syracuse, Stymphalos, Hieron, and the gods associated with Agesias’s victory, and asks for continued prosperity for both of his communities.

VII. notes

This unit presents a victory ode for Diagoras of Rhodes, who won the boxing-match at Olympia and returned home celebrated. It praises Rhodes, its three cities, its divine origins through Helios and Rhodos, and the noble lineage of Diagoras and the Eratidai. The poem recounts Tlepolemos's accidental killing of Likymnios, his consultation of Apollo's oracle, and the founding traditions of Rhodes. It also lists Diagoras's victories at Olympia and other Greek games and asks Zeus to honor him and his city.

VIII. notes

The unit celebrates Alkimedon of Aigina, who won the boys’ wrestling match at Olympia in 460 B.C. His victory is connected with the glory of his brother Timosthenes, his trainer Melesias, and the Blepsiad clan. The ode praises Aigina’s reputation for justice and hospitality and recalls Aiakos’s role in the building of Troy. It asks Zeus to grant continuing honor, health, and prosperity to Alkimedon’s family and community.

IX. notes

The ode celebrates Epharmostos of Opous as victor in the wrestling-match at Olympia and recounts his other athletic successes. It praises Opous and the Lokrians through myths of Zeus, Protogeneia, Pyrrha, Deukalion, Lokros, Menoitios, Patroklos, and Achilles. It also tells of Herakles' struggle with gods at Pylos and concludes that Epharmostos' victory arose from natural and divinely granted prowess, marked by his crowning of the altar of Aias Oileus.

X. notes

The ode concerns Agesidamos of Epizephyrian Lokris, son of Archestratos, who won the boys' boxing-match. It was to be sung at Olympia on the night after his victory, while a longer ode was promised for the later celebration in his Italian home. Pindar promises to honor Agesidamos with sweet songs and to remember the Lokrians' colony in the West. The poem praises the glory of Olympian winners and invokes the Muses to sing of a noble and valiant people.

XI. notes

The unit is a delayed victory ode by Pindar for Agesidamos of Epizephyrian Lokris, who won the boys' boxing-match at Olympia. Pindar asks the Muse and Truth to release him from blame for having forgotten his promise, and he celebrates Agesidamos, his trainer Ilas, and the Lokrian people. The poem recounts Herakles' foundation of the Olympic games at Pisa, including the establishment of the Altis, the Hill of Kronos, and the first Olympiad. It ends by praising the value of song in preserving a victor's fame.

XII. notes

The unit concerns Ergoteles of Himera, who won the long foot-race at Olympia after leaving Knosos because of civil dissension and becoming a citizen of Himera. It describes Fortune as governing ships, wars, councils, and the uncertain outcomes of human hopes. Ergoteles also won twice at Pytho and once at Isthmos, bringing glory to the Sicilian hot springs where the nymphs bathe. The ode's prayer asks Fortune to protect Himera, in apparent reference to the expulsion of Thrasydaios of Akragas by the Himeraeans.

XIII. notes

The unit celebrates Xenophon of Corinth, who in 464 B.C. won the Stadion race and the Pentathlon at Olympia. It praises Corinth, its athletic traditions, and the Oligaithidai, while listing Xenophon’s and his family’s victories at several Greek games. It also recounts the Corinthian hero Bellerophon’s divine-guided taming of Pegasos and his exploits against the Amazons, the Solymoi, and Chimaira. The ode concludes by asking Zeus to grant honour and prosperity to Xenophon and his family.

XIV. notes

The ode concerns Asopichos of Orchomenos, who won the boys' short foot-race in the Olympic games in 476 B.C. It was probably sung by a chorus of boys at Orchomenos, likely in the temple of the Graces. The poem praises the Graces and asks Echo to carry news of Asopichos's victory to his dead father in Persephone's realm.

THE PYTHIAN ODES. body

The unit is titled “THE PYTHIAN ODES.” No further content or details are provided.

I. body

The unit is Pindar’s victory ode for Hieron of Aitna, celebrating his chariot-race victory at the Pythian Games. It praises Aitna city, founded by Hieron under Dorian laws, and recalls the Syracusans’ victories over the Carthaginians at Himera and the Etruscans before Cumae. The poem invokes Zeus, Apollo, the Muses, and the Lyre, and describes Typhon imprisoned beneath Mount Etna. It also counsels Hieron to govern truthfully, preserve his reputation, and seek lasting happiness and glory.

notes notes

This unit consists of four footnotes. One identifies Typhon, another records the reading “erion,” and a third refers to Plataea. The fourth explains that it is better to be envied than pitied.

II. notes

The unit presents Pindar's ode for Hieron of Syracuse, celebrating his victory in the four-horse chariot-race and his protective action toward the Epizephyrian Lokrians against Anaxilaos. It praises Syracuse, Hieron's military valor, wealth, wisdom, and generosity, while using the story of Ixion's crimes and punishment to teach that favors should be repaid and that one must judge one's powers and circumstances rightly. The closing section condemns flattery, slander, envy, and crooked conduct, and commends sincerity and direct speech under every form of government.

III. notes

The ode celebrates Hieron of Syracuse and the Pythian victory won by his horse Pherenikos at Kirrha. It recounts how Apollo rescued Asklepios from the burning corpse of Koronis and had him trained by Cheiron as a healer, but Zeus later killed Asklepios for restoring a dead man. The poem reflects on human mortality, the limits of healing, the instability of happiness, and Hieron's illness while praising his prosperity and rule. It concludes that song preserves the fame of heroes such as Nestor and Sarpedon.

IV. notes

The ode celebrates Arkesilas of Kyrene's victory in the chariot-race and connects his lineage with the founding and colonization of Kyrene in Libya. It recounts Medea's prophecy, the Argonaut expedition led by Jason, the dispute between Jason and Pelias over the kingship of Iolkos, and Jason's quest for the Golden Fleece with Medea's aid. The poem traces the descent of Euphemos's line from the Argonauts to Battos and ultimately to Arkesilas. It also urges Arkesilas to restore Kyrene's political order and praises the exiled Demophilos for his wisdom and loyalty.

[Footnote 1: Libya. Epaphos was son of Zeus by Io.] notes

Libya is connected with Epaphos, who was the son of Zeus and Io, and with a future colony founded by Battos. Euphemos was told that his descendants would first colonize Thera and, after seventeen generations, found Kyrene in Libya. Thera was identified as the island where a clod dropped by Euphemos would wash ashore. Kyrene is identified with the city whose fountain was called Kyra.

V. notes

Ode V celebrates Arkesilas of Kyrene's victory in the chariot-race at the Pythian games. Karrhotos, Arkesilas's wife's brother, drove the chariot safely through twelve turns and returned with the honor of victory, while the chariot's equipment was dedicated at Delphi. The ode praises Arkesilas's wealth, kingship, divine favor, and lineage from Battos, and recounts Apollo's role in the foundation and protection of Kyrene. It also describes the sacred procession along Apollo's street and prays that Zeus will preserve Arkesilas's prosperity and grant the seed of Battos comparable renown at Olympia.

VI. notes

The unit celebrates Xenokrates of Akragas as winner of the chariot-race victory won in B.C. 494 and presents the ode as a treasure of song for Xenokrates, the Emmenidai, and Akragas. It praises the victory at Krisa and links Xenokrates with the honor owed to parents, especially through the examples of Achilles and Antilochos. Antilochos is remembered for dying to save his father Nestor from Memnon, while Thrasyboulos is praised for wisdom, generosity, and honorable use of wealth. The poem also invokes Apollo's sanctuary at Delphi and Poseidon, the Earthshaker who devised ventures of steeds.

VII. notes

The unit celebrates Megakles of Athens, winner of the four-horse chariot-race in B.C. 490, the year of the battle of Marathon. Megakles belonged to the great house of the Alkmaionidai and was probably the nephew, or possibly the son, of Kleisthenes. The poem praises Athens and the Alkmaionidai, recalls victories won by Megakles and his father at Isthmos, Olympia, and Kirrha, and acknowledges the envy that accompanied Megakles's success. Megakles was twice ostracized.

VIII. notes

The ode celebrates Aristomenes of Aigina as winner of the wrestling-match at the Pythian games and praises his athletic successes at several other contests. It invokes Peace and Righteousness, praises Aigina and the deeds of the sons of Aiakos, and credits Apollo with granting Aristomenes his victory. The poem also recalls Amphiaraos, Alkmaion, and the prophetic story of the Epigonoi before praying for the continued prosperity of Xenarches and his family. It ends by asking Aigina, with Zeus, Aiakos, Peleus, Telamon, and Achilles, to guard the city.

IX. notes

The unit celebrates Telesikrates of Kyrene, winner of the foot-race in full armour at Pytho, and praises the honour he brought to Kyrene. It recounts how Apollo carried the huntress-maiden Kyrene from Pelion to Libya, where she became queen and joined with Apollo, and how their son Aristaios was established as a benefactor of flocks and agriculture. It also links Telesikrates and Kyrene with Thebes, Herakles, and earlier contests, especially through praise of heroic deeds and timely action. The closing story compares Telesikrates’s victory with Alexidamos’s winning of the daughter of Antaios through a foot-race arranged as a marriage contest.

X. notes

The ode celebrates Hippokleas of Thessaly, winner of the two-stadion foot-race of boys at Pytho. Pindar praises Hippokleas, his father, his host Thorax, and the Aleuadai, while linking Thessaly and Lakedaimon to Herakles and Perseus. A long digression describes Perseus's visit to the Hyperborean folk and his slaying of the Gorgon. The unit is identified as Pindar's earliest surviving complete poem, written when he was twenty years old.

XI. notes

The unit celebrates Thrasydaios of Thebes, winner of the boys' short foot-race at Kirrha in B.C. 478. His victory made his father's house glorious, adding a third wreath to its honors, while his father had previously won at Pytho. The ode invokes Theban and heroic figures, recounts the murder of Agamemnon and the vengeance of Orestes, and praises modest service to the common good. It also declares that the middle course is most enduring in the commonwealth and condemns tyranny.

XII. notes

This early ode celebrates Midas of Akragas, winner of a flute-playing match, whose victory was won either in 494 or 450. It welcomes Midas and his crown from Pytho to Akragas, where the poem is probably performed in procession to the city’s tutelar shrine. The ode attributes the invention of the flute to Athene, who created it to imitate the lament of the Gorgons after Perseus killed Medusa. It concludes that human success requires labor and that fate may grant unexpected blessings while withholding others.

THE NEMEAN ODES. body

The unit consists solely of the title “THE NEMEAN ODES.” It concerns a work identified as The Nemean Odes. No further information is provided about its contents, subjects, or events.

Nemean Ode I body

Nemean Ode I celebrates Chromios of Aitna, winner of the chariot-race at Nemea, and is presented as a song performed before his house in Ortygia. It praises Aitna, Syracuse, Sicily, Zeus, and Chromios’s generosity and abilities. Much of the ode recounts Herakles’ childhood victory over the serpents sent by Hera and Teiresias’s prophecy of Herakles’ future labors, battle against the giants, rest among the immortals, and marriage to Hebe.

notes notes

The note explains the phrase “so honoured by Artemis.” It indicates that the subject receives enough honor from Artemis to rank with her native Delos. The comparison establishes Delos as a place especially associated with Artemis.

Nemean Ode II notes

The ode celebrates Timodemos of Athens, who won the pankration at Nemea. It predicts that he will win further victories at the Isthmian and Pythian games. Timodemos is praised as a worthy descendant of the Timodemidai of Salamis and Acharnai, whose family had already gained many athletic crowns. The citizens are urged to welcome him home and sing a victory song in his honor.

Nemean Ode III notes

The ode celebrates Aristokleides of Aigina, who won a pankration victory at the Nemean games. It praises Aigina and Aiakos’ heroic lineage, recounting the deeds of Peleus, Telamon, Achilles, and other figures of that race. The poet asks the divine Muse to bring songs of triumph and declares that Aristokleides has brought new renown to Aigina through his victory at Nemea.

Nemean Ode IV notes

The ode celebrates Timasarchos of Aigina as winner of the boys' wrestling-match at Nemea and praises his victories at several games. It invokes Zeus, Nemea, and the Aiakids, while recalling Timasarchos's deceased father Timokritos and the family's athletic honors. Much of the poem recounts the deeds and descendants of Herakles, Telamon, Peleus, and Thetis. It also praises Timasarchos's clan, his wreaths and renown, his uncle Kallikles, and his trainer Melesias.

Nemean Ode V notes

Nemean Ode V celebrates Pytheas of Aigina, son of Lampon, for winning the boys' pankration at Nemea. The ode praises Aigina, the Aiakidai, and Pytheas's maternal uncle Euthymenes, while briefly recounting the ancestry and legends of Aiakos, Phokos, Peleus, and Thetis. It also credits the Athenian trainer Menander and praises Themistios for victories in boxing and pankration at Epidauros.

Nemean Ode VI notes

The ode celebrates Alkimidas of Aigina, winner of the boys' wrestling-match at Nemea. It praises his lineage, especially Praxidamas, and presents his victory as evidence that athletic distinction returns among successive generations. It also honors the Bassid clan and the Aiakidai by recalling their victories at Olympia, Isthmos, Nemea, and Pytho. Melesias is praised as the trainer who guided Alkimidas's hands and strength.

Nemean Ode VII notes

The ode celebrates Sogenes of Aigina, son of Thearion, for winning the boys' pentathlon and praises his family and city. Pindar reflects on how song preserves great deeds, comparing the fame of Odysseus with the neglected excellence of Aias. He recounts the death and posthumous honors of Neoptolemos at Delphi and insists that he has not dishonored him. The poem concludes by invoking Zeus, Aiakos, and Herakles while praying for lasting prosperity for Sogenes, Thearion, and their descendants.

Nemean Ode VIII notes

Nemean Ode VIII celebrates Deinis of Aigina, winner of the short foot-race, and honors his father Meges and their clan. It invokes Aiakos and recalls the union of Zeus and Aigina, from which Aiakos was born to rule Oinone. The ode contrasts honorable virtue with envy, treacherous speech, and slander through the story of Aias and Odysseus. It presents song as a means of praising achievement, preserving reputation, and making labor painless.

Nemean Ode IX notes

Nemean Ode IX celebrates Chromios of Aitna for winning a chariot race at the Pythian games at Sikyon. The song leads the triumph from Apollo at Sikyon to the newly founded city of Aitna and praises Chromios's wealth, courage, military service, and divine good fortune. It recalls Adrastos's foundation of the contest and the disastrous expedition of Amphiaraos and the other heroes against Thebes. The ode ends by renewing the victory through song, wine, and the honor bestowed by the Muses and the Charites.

Nemean Ode X notes

The ode celebrates Theaios of Argos, winner of the wrestling-match at the Hekatombaia at Argos. It praises Argos, Hera's city, through its heroes, gods, women, and many athletic victories. Theaios is credited with victories at Pytho, the Isthmos, Nemea, the Gates of the Sea, the plains of Adrastos, and the festival of the Athenians. The closing myth recounts how Kastor was killed by Idas, how Polydeukes avenged him, and how Polydeukes chose to share immortality with his brother.

Nemean Ode XI notes

The ode celebrates Aristagoras of Tenedos on his election as Prytanis, apparently the President of the Senate. It welcomes Aristagoras and his fellow senators to Hestia's sanctuary during the initiatory ceremonies and prays that he complete his twelve-month term with fair fame and an untroubled heart. Aristagoras is praised for his bodily excellence, courage, ancestry, and sixteen neighboring athletic victories in wrestling and the pankration. The ode also reflects on mortal uncertainty, warning against both excessive boasting and excessive mistrust of one's abilities.

THE ISTHMIAN ODES. body

The unit is titled “THE ISTHMIAN ODES.” It identifies the subject as The Isthmian Odes. No further information is provided about the work or its contents.

Isthmian Ode I body

Pindar sets aside an intended song for Apollo at Delos in order to celebrate Herodotos of Thebes and his chariot victory. He praises Thebes, noting that Isthmus has granted its people six crowns, and compares Herodotos’ driving with the legendary chariot skills of Kastor and Iolaos. The ode also honors Herodotos’ father Asopodoros and his new home Orchomenos, which sheltered him after a shipwreck. Pindar prays that Herodotos will win further victories at Pytho, Alpheos, and Olympia and bring honor to seven-gated Thebes.

notes notes

The unit contains a footnote identifying the Pentathlon. It refers the reader to the Introduction to Ol. xiii and the note on Nem. vii, p. 129. No further information about the Pentathlon is provided here.

Isthmian Ode II notes

The ode honors Xenokrates of Akragas for winning the Isthmian chariot-race and other victories at Krisa, Athens, and Olympia. It is addressed to Thrasyboulos, Xenokrates' son, and urges him not to remain silent about his father's achievements. Xenokrates is praised as a distinguished citizen, supporter of horse-racing, worshipper at festivals, and generous host. Nikesippos is instructed to deliver this message to Thrasyboulos.

Isthmian Ode III notes

Isthmian Ode III celebrates Melissos of Thebes, winner in the pankration and a victor in the chariot-race at Nemea. It praises the Kleonymid clan's ancient athletic fame, its losses in war, and the revival of its glory through Melissos. Melissos's small stature is contrasted with his courage and skill, especially through comparison with Herakles's wrestling match against the giant Antaios. The ode also honors Herakles and describes the sacrifices and yearly games held at Thebes.

Isthmian Ode IV notes

Isthmian Ode IV celebrates Phylakidas of Aigina for winning the pankration and praises his brother Pytheas, who helped guide his training. The ode invokes Theia and reflects on wealth, success, fame, and the divine source of human strength. It honors Aigina and the sons of Aiakos, recalling their heroic deeds and Aigina’s seamen’s role in the deliverance of Salamis during the battle of Salamis. The poem concludes by calling for a crown, a fillet, and a new song to celebrate Phylakidas’s victory.

Isthmian Ode V notes

The ode honors Phylakidas of Aigina, who has won the pankration at the Isthmian Games, and celebrates his family’s athletic victories. It praises Lampon, Phylakidas’s father, for cultivating excellence, hospitality, moderation, and a consistent character. The poem recalls Herakles’ expedition with Telamon, the birth and naming of Aias, and the heroic fame of Aiakos’ descendants. It concludes with the poet offering Lampon a drink of song from the sacred spring of Dirke.

Isthmian Ode VI notes

The ode celebrates Strepsiades of Thebes, winner of the pankration at Isthmos. It recalls Thebes’ mythic deeds and says that its ancient glory sleeps unless song preserves it. Strepsiades shares his crown with his uncle, who died fighting in war while defending his country. The poem ends by asking Loxias to grant another crown at the Pythian games and warns against aspiring beyond mortal limits through the example of Bellerophon.

Isthmian Ode VII notes

The ode celebrates Kleandros of Aigina for winning the pankration at the Isthmian Games and for showing strength at Nemea. It is meant to be sung before the house of his father, Telesarchos, and urges young men to honor Kleandros with song and a myrtle wreath. The poet connects Aigina’s glory with Aiakos, Peleus, and Achilles, recounting how Themis’ oracle concerning Thetis’ son led to Thetis’ wedding with Peleus. The ode also praises Nikokles, Kleandros’ kinsman, for winning a boxing victory at the Isthmian Games, while celebrating the expulsion of the Persians and the return of freedom to Hellas.

FRAGMENTS. body

The unit concerns the Fragments and their classification. Nearly two-thirds of the Fragments cannot be assigned to any distinct class. The remaining Fragments are divided among ten classes, including Epinikia, Hymnoi, Paianes, Dithyramboi, Prosodia, Parthenia, Hyporchaemata, Enkomia, Skolia, and Thraenoi. These classes comprise various types of odes, hymns, processional songs, dance songs, banquet songs, and dirges.

FRAGMENT OF A DITHYRAMB, body

The singer (speaker) calls on the Olympian gods to dance at Athens and receive crowns and drink-offerings. The singer (speaker) has come from the house of Zeus to sing again for Bromios, the ivy-crowned god of clamorous shout, and to sing the offspring of the Highest and Kadmeän mothers. The unit describes spring at Argive Nemea, when violets and roses appear, flute-accompanied songs sound, and hymns honor Semele.

notes notes

Bockh proposed an explanation involving the temple of Zeus at Nemea and its sacred palm. At the Nemean games, a winner received a branch of the palm together with a crown. Pindar had attended the games in winter and suggested that, like the temple’s priest, he could foresee from the branch that spring and the vernal Dionysia at Athens were approaching.

FRAGMENTS OF A PROCESSION-SONG ([Greek: prosodion]), notes

The fragments are from a procession-song (prosodion) in honour of Delos. Delos is described as a god-reared daughter of the sea and as an immoveable marvel of the earth, called Delos by mortals and the far-seen star of the dark earth by the gods of Olympus. The island once drifted before waves and winds, but four pillars sprang from deep roots in the earth when Leto arrived in childbirth. Leto was delivered there and looked upon her blessed brood.

FRAGMENT OF A SONG WITH ACCOMPANIMENT OF DANCE notes

The fragment of a song with accompaniment of dance was written on the occasion of an eclipse of the Sun, probably that of April 30, B.C. 463. It addresses the Sun as the supreme star and asks why it has hidden itself during the day. The speaker prays to Zeus that the eclipse may bring unimpaired good fortune to Thebes. The speaker fears that it could signal war, destruction, civil strife, storms, floods, or the creation of a new race of men.

FRAGMENTS OF DIRGES (thraenoi). notes

The blessed dead dwell in a land of crimson-flowered meadows, frankincense-trees, golden fruits, pleasures, and continual fragrance, while the sun shines below for them. The body is subject to death, but a shadow of life remains alive, coming from the gods and revealing in dreams the judgment approaching with sorrow or joy. Persephone sends back to the light of the sun the souls for whom she accepts atonement for an ancient woe after nine years. From those souls arise noble kings, men swift and strong and wise, who are later called holy heroes among men.
