Pioneer Press · 1862~ · 19 sections

This document is a collection of historical reminiscences and accounts centered on St. Paul and Minnesota from territorial and pioneer years through the Civil War and later developments. Its principal subjects are the city’s newspapers and printers, civic institutions, entertainment, fires, transportation, politics, the 1862 Sioux conflict, and Minnesota’s wartime experience. It opens with the growth and rivalry of St. Paul daily papers, the Pioneer, Minnesotian, Times, Press, North Star, and Weekly Journal, covering state-printing patronage, political campaigns, telegraph and Atlantic-cable news, Minnesota’s admission, and Civil War reporting. Printers and editors emerge as important civic and military figures: Capt. E.Y. Shelly and others served in the war, Charles R. Stuart was celebrated for typesetting, and Frank Moore’s career runs from a Minnesotian printer’s “devil” in 1858 to long service with the Pioneer Press. The accounts trace production from early presses to stereotyping and linotype machinery. Other sections portray a growing river city: busy upper-Mississippi steamboat traffic; the Five Million Loan Election and railroad-bond collapse; the Sons of Malta; volunteer fire companies becoming a paid department; destructive hotel and public-building fires; and halls and theaters that hosted music, political meetings, conventions, military activity, fundraisers, and performers including Dan Emmet, the Hutchinson family, Adalina Patti, Frederick Douglass, and James J. Hill. One political satire imagines Robert A. Smith ruling a transformed Mississippi city while the Okons dominate patronage. The Civil War forms the collection’s central crisis. It records printer patriotism, preparations for draft resistance, Minnesota soldiers at Mill Springs and Pittsburg Landing, the First Minnesota battery, Captain William Henry Acker’s death, and public rejoicing at Union successes. On July 4, 1863, while the Pioneer moved offices, the Press alone issued an extra announcing Gettysburg and Vicksburg, helping ignite celebration in St. Paul. The narrative closes the war with St. Paul’s mourning for Abraham Lincoln and its memorial service led by Frederick A. Noble. A major Minnesota emergency is the Great Sioux Outbreak of 1862: attacks at Fort Ridgely, New Ulm, Birch Coulie, and elsewhere; Alexander Ramsey’s emergency response; Henry Hastings Sibley’s campaign; Other Day’s rescue of refugees; Wood Lake and Camp Release; the liberation of captives; convictions and the execution of thirty-eight Indians; Little Crow’s escape and death; and the loss of Sioux lands. A final personal recollection of winning a school spelling match adds a small-scale memory of life before railroads and telegraphs.

James M. Goodhue describes the technological development and working conditions of St. Paul daily newspapers from the 1850s through the Civil War. He recounts competition among the Pioneer, Minnesotian, Times, and later the Press for state-printing contracts, political news, telegraph reports, and presidential messages. It also describes patriotism among printers, the First Battle of Bull Run, and preparations for resistance to the United States Army draft.

The unnamed narrator describes newspaper publishing in St. Paul before and after the Civil War, including holiday publication practices and competition among local papers. On July 4, 1863, the Pioneer was moving offices while the Press alone issued an extra reporting the Union victories at Gettysburg and the surrender of Vicksburg, prompting a major celebration in St. Paul. The narrator began working for the Daily Minnesotian in 1858 and explains how the Pioneer scooped competing papers with news of Minnesota's admission into the Union before telegraphic communication existed. The unit also traces technological changes in newspaper production from single-cylinder presses to stereotyping and linotype machines.

THE FIVE MILLION LOAN ELECTION. body

The unit describes steamboat travel on the upper Mississippi, especially the activity of the Galena, Dunleith, Dubuque and Minnesota Packet company and St. Paul's levee. It recounts the Five Million Loan Election, the issuance and later compromise of Minnesota state railroad bonds, and the collapse of the associated railroad construction scheme. It also describes a 1858 battle near Shakopee between the Sioux and Chippewas and St. Paul's elaborate celebration of the successful laying of the Atlantic cable, attended by representations of Queen Victoria and James Buchanan.

Some time during the early '50s a secret order known as the Sons of Malta was or body

The Sons of Malta organized a successful lodge in St. Paul in 1858, attracting many prominent local men through elaborate and humiliating secret initiations. The order later declined after Rev. John Penman exposed its secret work in Harper's Weekly. The Republican Party's victory in 1859 led to a contested effort to award the state printing patronage to the Minnesotian and Times, which was briefly consolidated before separating again. St. Paul also briefly supported the Democratic Daily North Star and the antiwar St. Paul Weekly Journal, while the St. Paul Daily Press eventually absorbed the Minnesotian.

THE GREAT SIOUX OUTBREAK IN 1862. body

The Great Sioux Outbreak in 1862 began on August 18 during the Civil War, when Sioux forces attacked settlers and government personnel in western Minnesota. The attacks spread across the reservation and surrounding communities, including Fort Ridgely, New Ulm, and Birch Coulie, causing extensive deaths, destruction, and displacement. Gov. Alexander Ramsey called for emergency measures, while Henry Hastings Sibley led partially equipped forces against the Sioux. Other Day helped sixty-two refugees reach safety, and St. Paul organized home guards as fear of further attacks spread.

As soon as Gen. body

Gen. Sibley led about 2,000 men in an offensive expedition that culminated in the Wood Lake battle on September 23, 1862, where Col. Marshall routed an approximately equal force of Sioux. His command reached Camp Release, where a large Indian camp surrendered and about 250 mixed bloods and white women and children were freed. A military tribunal convicted 321 Indians, but the President of the United States authorized the execution of only thirty-eight at Mankato on December 26, 1862. Little Crow escaped and was later killed, while the Sioux lost their Minnesota lands and the military struggled to transport supplies to frontier troops.

FIREMEN AND FIRES OF PIONEER DAYS. body

This unit recounts the development of the St. Paul Fire Department from bucket brigades and volunteer companies to a paid department with steamers and other modern equipment. It describes the organization of Pioneer Hook and Ladder Company No. 1, Hope Engine Company No. 1, and Minnehaha Engine Company No. 2, as well as the department's first parade in 1859. The account also recalls the work of pioneer firemen, the service of Mrs. Bartlett Presley, and an anecdote about Michael E. Ames during a house fire.

HOTEL FIRES. body

The unit recounts hotel fires and other major fires during St. Paul’s early history, describing destroyed buildings, causes, rescues, losses, and the development of the fire department. It concerns hotels including the Daniels house, Sintominie hotel, Rice house, Winslow house, American house, Cosmopolitan hotel, International hotel, and others. The account also describes the March 16, 1860 fire, the February 3, 1869 International hotel fire, the May 1870 Concert Hall fire, and several later hotel fires. It names people who fought fires, rescued occupants, managed hotels, or were associated with the buildings and events.

THE FIRST AMUSEMENT HALLS IN ST. body

The unit recounts the early amusement halls and musical entertainment venues of St. Paul, including Irvine hall, Concert hall, the Athenaeum, Market hall, and Ingersoll block. Dan Emmet performed at Irvine hall, later wrote "Dixie," and sent it to his friend R.C. Munger, whose music store hosted its first Western performance. The Hutchinson family frequently performed in St. Paul and was known for patriotic and abolitionist songs. The text also describes political meetings, speeches, concerts, military activity, and library fundraising held in the city's halls.

The People's theater, an old frame building on the corner of Fourth and St. body

The unit describes St. Paul's early theaters, halls, concerts, balls, political meetings, and wartime fundraisers. The People's theater hosted performers including the Wallacks and Col. R.E.J. Miles, while Mazurka hall, Mozart hall, Masonic hall, and the old opera house served as entertainment venues. Adalina Patti performed at the old capitol, and the house of representatives hosted civic meetings, Republican conventions, and other events. The account also covers wartime support for the American government, the activities of the Pioneer Guards, and appearances by Frederick Douglass and James J. Hill.

PRINTERS AND EDITORS OF TERRITORIAL DAYS. body

This unit describes territorial printers and editors in Minnesota, focusing especially on Capt. E.Y. Shelly, the first printer to permanently locate in the territory. It recounts Shelly's printing career in St. Paul, his Mexican War service, and his Civil War service with Brackett's battalion. It also discusses other territorial printers who served in the Civil War and notes that few of the original printers remained. The unit provides detailed accounts of printing offices, military service, and the exceptional typesetting speed of Charles R. Stuart.

TERRITORIAL PRINTERS IN CIVIL LIFE. body

The unit profiles territorial printers and editors in Minnesota, especially those connected with St. Paul newspapers and printing offices. It describes their occupations, political and civic careers, artistic interests, personal eccentricities, and notable incidents. The account also explains that early editors and printers worked closely together, sometimes living in their offices, while mechanization diminished the sociability of composing rooms.

THE DECISIVE BATTLE OF MILL SPRINGS. body

The Battle of Mill Springs was an early decisive Union victory in the Civil War, fought after the Second Minnesota Infantry Regiment joined George H. Thomas's division near Logan's Cross Roads, Kentucky. The Second Minnesota fought Confederate troops near Beech Grove, using rifle fire and bayonets, and suffered twelve killed and thirty-five wounded. Confederate General Zollicoffer was killed by Colonel Fry, while Bailie Peyton was killed after shooting Lieutenant Stout. The Union victory captured Confederate artillery, wagons, ammunition, and supplies, disrupted the Confederate right flank, and prompted widespread rejoicing in Minnesota, Louisville, and St. Paul.

THE GREAT BATTLE OF PITTSBURG LANDING. body

The unit describes the Battle of Pittsburg Landing, also called the Battle of Shiloh, fought on April 6–7, 1862, between Ulysses S. Grant's Union forces and the Confederate forces under Albert Sidney Johnston and P. G. T. Beauregard. The Confederates initially drove back parts of the Union army, but Union resistance, gunboat support, and reinforcements under Don Carlos Buell and Lew Wallace prevented their victory. The Union army counterattacked on April 7 and forced the Confederates to retreat toward Corinth. The battle caused heavy losses, including the deaths of Albert Sidney Johnston and William H. L. Wallace, and later became the subject of disputes over Grant, Buell, and the conduct of the campaign.

The unit recounts the service of the First Minnesota battery during the American Civil War, especially its actions at the Battle of Pittsburg Landing. Organized at Fort Snelling under Captain Emil Munch, the battery fought in the Hornet's Nest and suffered several casualties while helping hold the Union lines. The text also memorializes Captain William Henry Acker, a St. Paul military leader who was killed at Pittsburg Landing and later honored through the naming of Acker Post.

THE DEATH OF PRESIDENT LINCOLN. body

Abraham Lincoln was assassinated on April 14, 1865, and news of his death plunged the United States into widespread mourning. In St. Paul, the House of Hope church held a solemn memorial service led by its pastor, Frederick A. Noble, who praised Lincoln’s life, leadership, and commitment to the Union cause. The service also mourned Secretary of State Seward, who had been attacked but survived. The text contrasts the universal grief following Lincoln’s assassination with the less-universal shock caused by the later assassinations of two other presidents.

AN ALLEGORICAL HOROSCOPE IN TWO CHAPTERS. body

The unit presents an allegorical prophecy concerning Robert A. Smith, the city that will arise along the Mississippi River, and the political influence of the Okons. A dusky maiden predicts that Robert will become the long-reigning chief of a great city and will preserve political support by avoiding firm positions, withdrawing from controversies, and distributing offices to the Okons and their followers. More than eighty years later, the voyageur returns to find the city transformed into a large, modern metropolis, with Robert ruling from a magnificent public building and the Okons controlling every office within his gift. The voyageur leaves in anguish at the Okons’ pervasive power.

SPELLING DOWN A SCHOOL. body

The narrator describes growing up in a small village in northern Pennsylvania before railroads and telegraphs reached the area. At about twelve, the narrator secretly rode under a sleigh seat to attend a grand spelling match involving four schools. The schoolmaster from the narrator's district entered the narrator into the contest, where the narrator correctly spelled "phthisic" and outlasted the other pupils. The narrator's school was declared the champion, and the narrator's reputation as a speller was established.

HALF A CENTURY WITH THE PIONEER PRESS. body

Frank Moore celebrated the fiftieth anniversary of his connection with the Pioneer Press in 1908 at an informal dinner attended by a dozen former employees. He began working for the Minnesotian in St. Paul in 1858 as a printer’s “devil” and later joined the Pioneer Press when it absorbed the Minnesotian. Moore became superintendent of the composing room and had held that position for about forty years. His service was continuous except for his time as a Civil War volunteer, and he was recognized for his loyalty and efficiency.
