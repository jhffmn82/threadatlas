Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912 · 22 sections

The document traces the ruin and aftermath of Oedipus’s family across Oedipus the King, Oedipus at Colonus, and Antigone. In Thebes, plague drives Oedipus to seek Laius’s murderer under Apollo’s command. He curses the culprit and clashes with Teiresias, who identifies him as both murderer and unknowingly polluted kin. Creon denies plotting for the throne, while Jocasta’s account of Laius’s death at a crossroads makes Oedipus fear his own past. A Corinthian Messenger and Laius’s Herdsman reveal that Oedipus was Jocasta and Laius’s exposed son: he killed his father and married his mother, fulfilling the prophecies. Jocasta hangs herself; Oedipus blinds himself, begs for exile, and leaves Creon responsible for the household and for Antigone and Ismene. Blind and exiled, Oedipus reaches the sacred grove at Colonus with Antigone. Ismene brings news that his sons, Eteocles and Polyneices, fight over Thebes and that Thebes now needs Oedipus because of oracles. Theseus grants him Athenian protection and accepts his promise that his burial will benefit the land. Creon attempts to force Oedipus back and seizes Antigone and Ismene, but Theseus rescues them. Polyneices seeks Oedipus’s support against Eteocles; Oedipus curses both sons. As thunder signals his end, Oedipus gives Theseus a secret protective gift and dies mysteriously, leaving his daughters in Theseus’s care. They resolve to return to Thebes to address their brothers’ feud. In Thebes, Antigone defies Creon’s order that Polyneices remain unburied while Eteocles receives honor. She buries Polyneices according to Heaven’s unwritten laws, is arrested, and openly accepts responsibility. Creon condemns her despite Haemon’s appeal to heed public opinion and mercy. Teiresias warns that the gods reject Thebes because Polyneices is unburied and predicts Creon will lose his child. Creon relents too late: Antigone dies in her tomb, Haemon kills himself, and Eurydice kills herself while cursing Creon. Creon recognizes that his own decisions have brought devastation, completing the continuing suffering of the Labdacidae.

OEDIPUS THE KING body

Oedipus learns from Creon that Apollo’s oracle commands Thebes to purge the pollution caused by the murder of Laius, either by banishing the murderer or shedding blood for blood. Oedipus vows to find Laius’s murderers and publicly curses anyone who conceals or shelters them. When Teiresias refuses to speak, Oedipus accuses him of conspiring with Creon, and Teiresias declares that Oedipus himself is the murderer and lives unknowingly in infamy with his nearest kin. Thebes remains afflicted by plague, while the Theban elders appeal to the gods for relief.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus urges Oedipus and Teiresias to stop quarreling and consider how to fulfill the oracle. Teiresias declares that Oedipus does not know his lineage and is unknowingly an enemy to his living and dead kin. He prophesies that Oedipus will be driven into exile, lose his sight, and discover that he is the murderer of Laius, the brother and husband of his mother, and the father and half-brother of his children. Oedipus angrily rejects Teiresias, who then leaves after delivering the prophecy.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus describes the unnamed doer of a murder as pursued by Apollo and the Fates after a command from Parnassus to discover him. It expresses uncertainty about the master seer’s words and says it knows of no old or recent quarrel between the Labdacidan house and the ruler, Polybus’ son. The Chorus honors Zeus and Apollo but questions whether a mortal seer has proved superior to others in knowledge. It also struggles to accept that a crime has been committed by Oedipus, who once saved the State from the winged songstress.

CREON. body

Creon defends himself against Oedipus’s accusation that he conspired with the seer to seize the throne, arguing that he prefers influence and security to kingship; Jocasta and the Chorus stop the quarrel, and Oedipus reluctantly lets him go. Jocasta recounts that Laius was supposedly killed by robbers at a crossroads and that his infant son was exposed, while Oedipus realizes that he may have killed Laius during an encounter at branching roads. Oedipus explains that Apollo’s prophecy said he would kill his father and marry his mother, and he orders the surviving herdsman brought to Thebes to confirm whether Laius was killed by one man or by robbers. The Chorus prays for reverence toward divine law and laments that faith in Apollo and the oracle has weakened.

[Enter JOCASTA.] body

Jocasta tells Oedipus that Polybus has died naturally, apparently disproving the oracle that Oedipus would kill his father. The Corinthian Messenger then reveals that Polybus was not Oedipus’s biological father and that Oedipus was given to him as an infant by Laius’s Herdsman. The Herdsman admits that the child was Laius’s son, born to Jocasta, and was exposed because of a prophecy that he would kill his father. Oedipus realizes that he has fulfilled the prophecies through parricide and marriage to his mother, while Jocasta leaves in anguish.

[Enter SECOND MESSENGER.] body

The Second Messenger reports that Jocasta has died by hanging and that Oedipus blinded himself after discovering the truth about his marriage and his crimes. Oedipus laments that he murdered Laius, married his mother, and brought ruin upon the house of Labdacus, and he begs to be removed from Thebes. Creon assumes guardianship, agrees to care for Jocasta’s burial and Oedipus’s daughters, but says the gods must be consulted about exile. Antigone and Ismene are brought to Oedipus, who mourns the suffering and disgrace they may face, while the Chorus warns that no mortal should be called happy before the end of life.

OEDIPUS AT COLONUS body

Oedipus, blind and exiled from Thebes, arrives at Colonus with his daughter Antigone and rests within the sacred grove of the Eumenides. A Stranger identifies the place as holy ground dedicated to the Eumenides and Poseidon, and goes to summon the authorities while Oedipus invokes Apollo’s oracle and asks for protection. The Chorus discovers Oedipus, learns his identity, and orders him to leave, but Oedipus argues that Athens should honor its reputation for hospitality and that his presence can benefit the land. The Chorus agrees to await the decision of Theseus, and Antigone then sees her sister Ismene approaching.

[Enter ISMENE] body

Ismene visits Oedipus and Antigone, bringing news that Oedipus's sons are fighting over the Theban throne. She reports that oracles say Thebes will depend on Oedipus, prompting Creon to seek to keep him near Theban territory. Oedipus condemns his sons for failing to help during his banishment and wishes their conflict to destroy them both. The Chorus instructs Oedipus and his daughters to perform ritual offerings to the deities whose grove he has profaned.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus asks Oedipus to recount the cruel suffering that has become widely known. Oedipus explains that the State bound him, unknowingly, in an incestuous marriage with the woman who was both his wife and mother, and that their daughters were also his sisters. He admits that he killed a man who would otherwise have killed him, but maintains that the killing was unintentional and just. Theseus, sovereign of Athens and son of Aegeus, then approaches in response to Oedipus's summons.

[Enter THESEUS] body

Theseus welcomes Oedipus and Antigone, pledging that Athens will protect Oedipus from those who would force him back to Thebes. Oedipus offers his dead body as a gift, explaining that his buried corpse will give power to the land that shelters it and threaten his sons. Theseus accepts Oedipus as a citizen and allows him to remain where he is, while the chorus praises Colonus and its sacred natural beauty. Antigone then warns that Creon is approaching with his company.

[Enter CREON with attendants] body

Creon comes from Thebes to persuade Oedipus to return, claiming that the Thebans and especially Creon mourn his suffering. Oedipus rejects Creon's offer, accusing him of seeking to place Oedipus on Thebes' borders rather than bring him home, and Creon orders his guards to seize Antigone and Oedipus's other daughter. The Chorus of Colonus resists, and Theseus arrives, orders the maidens rescued, and threatens to hold Creon accountable under Athenian law. Oedipus defends himself against Creon's accusations of parricide and incest, insisting that he acted unknowingly and under divine compulsion.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus longs to witness the battle in which Theseus and his warriors confront the flying foe and rescue the two captive maidens. It imagines the conflict near Pythian shore, the torch-lit bay, or the glen west of white Oea. The Chorus calls on Zeus, Athene, Apollo, and the Huntress to help Athens and bring victory. At the end, an escort is said to be approaching with the maidens.

[Enter ANTIGONE and ISMENE with THESEUS] body

Antigone and Ismene are brought back safely to Oedipus by Theseus, who has fulfilled his promise. Polyneices comes as a suppliant, explains his claim against Eteocles and his expedition against Thebes, and asks Oedipus for support, but Oedipus curses both sons. Polyneices refuses Antigone’s plea to turn back and asks that he and his allies receive burial if he dies. Thunder and lightning announce Oedipus’s approaching death; he calls Theseus, promises him a secret that will protect the land, and is led toward the place where he will die.

[Enter MESSENGER] body

The Messenger reports that Oedipus has died in a mysterious, painless manner near the sacred threshold, after entrusting his daughters to Theseus. Oedipus disappears from sight while only Theseus remains to witness what happens, and the exact manner of his death is known only to Theseus. Antigone and Ismene mourn him and wish to see his burial place, but Theseus tells them that Oedipus forbade anyone to approach the hidden tomb. Theseus promises to care for the daughters, and they decide to return to Thebes to try to end the feud between their brothers.

ANTIGONE body

Antigone tells Ismene that Creon has ordered Polyneices to remain unburied and resolves to bury him despite the death penalty. Ismene refuses to help, arguing that they are women who must obey the stronger ruler, while Antigone invokes the eternal laws of Heaven. Creon announces that Eteocles will receive heroic burial but Polyneices will be left as carrion, and he threatens the guards when Polyneices’ corpse is found covered with dust. The Chorus praises humanity’s accomplishments while warning that civic and divine laws must be respected, then sees Antigone being brought before the king.

[Enter GUARD bringing ANTIGONE] body

The Guard brings Antigone before Creon after catching her burying the corpse in defiance of Creon’s interdict. Antigone admits the deed and says that the unwritten laws of Heaven, not Creon’s human law, required her to bury her dead brother. Creon condemns Antigone to death and also treats Ismene as guilty, though Antigone denies that Ismene helped her. Ismene tries to share Antigone’s fate, while the Chorus reflects on the inherited suffering of the Labdacidae and announces Haemon’s angry arrival.

[Enter HAEMON] body

Haemon confronts Creon, urging him to listen to the people and yield to wiser counsel, but Creon refuses and orders Antigone to die. Haemon declares that Antigone’s death will bring another death and leaves in anger. Antigone is taken away to be sealed alive in a rock-hewn tomb, where she mourns her lost marriage, family, and future. The Chorus recalls others whom fate and divine power overcame, while questioning Antigone’s defiance of authority.

[Enter TEIRESIAS and BOY] body

Teiresias warns Creon that the gods reject Thebes because Polyneices remains unburied, and he prophesies that Creon will suffer the loss of his own child. Creon initially accuses Teiresias and other advisers of seeking money, but after the Chorus urges him to yield, he goes to free Antigone and bury Polyneices. Creon reaches Antigone’s tomb too late: Antigone is dead, and Haemon kills himself beside her after attacking his father. Eurydice then kills herself, cursing Creon, who recognizes his guilt and is left crushed by grief and Fate.
