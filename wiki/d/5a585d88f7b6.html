This document is a long paper in the Proceedings of the 2024 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies, Volume 1, describing ARES, an Automated RAG Evaluation System. Jon Saad-Falcon, Omar Khattab, Christopher Potts, and Matei Zaharia present ARES as a framework for evaluating retrieval-augmented generation systems, which retrieve passages and use a language model to generate responses for applications such as question answering, fact-checking, and customer support. ARES focuses on three measures: context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance. It unfolds in three stages: generating synthetic query–passage–answer data from in-domain passages; fine-tuning separate lightweight language-model judges for the three classification tasks; and using prediction-powered inference with a small human preference validation set to correct prediction errors, estimate system performance, and provide 95% confidence intervals. The system requires an in-domain passage set, a few in-domain query–answer examples, and roughly 150 or more human-annotated validation examples. The paper positions ARES among RAG and LLM-evaluation work, especially against EXAM and RAGAS. Unlike EXAM, it does not require sub-questions, and reported results find it stronger than RAGAS’s heuristic prompts. Experiments use FLAN-T5 XXL for synthetic queries and answers, DeBERTa-v3-Large as the judge, and tasks from KILT and SuperGLUE, including Natural Questions, HotpotQA, FEVER, Wizards of Wikipedia, MultiRC, and ReCoRD. The authors create mock RAG systems with known 70%–90% success rates and assess ranking quality with Kendall rank correlation. Results report that ARES generally exceeds RAGAS and GPT-3.5 in ranking and scoring accuracy, while PPI reduces human-annotation needs by 78% relative to an annotation-based baseline. On existing RAG systems, it reaches average Kendall’s tau values of 0.91 for context relevance and 0.97 for answer relevance; on AIS, it distinguishes faithful from non-attributed answers. It generalizes across KILT and SuperGLUE shifts but performs poorly on larger shifts involving languages, code, and extraction tasks. The conclusion proposes stronger judges and synthetic data, while limitations include reliance on 150–300 potentially domain-expert annotations, substantial GPU memory and time, and English-only evaluation.

Introduction body

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become a prominent approach for building user-facing NLP applications. These applications include question answering (QA), fact-checking, and customer support. A RAG system typically uses a retriever to find relevant passages and a downstream language model (LM) to generate a response, with design choices involving retrieval models, document chunking, and language-model prompting or fine-tuning.

RAG combines language models with retrieval systems to provide domain-specific knowledge, factual grounding, and source citations. LLM-based evaluation techniques are used to assess LLM systems and generation quality, including through methods such as AutoCalibrate. For knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, LLMs have been studied for attribution and factuality, while LongEval, Hagrid, and ALCE provide related resources. ARES .. 3.3 body

ARES evaluates and ranks RAG systems in three stages: synthetic data generation, LLM judge training, and prediction-powered inference. It generates query–passage–answer triples and positive and negative examples from in-domain passages, then fine-tunes separate judges for context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance. ARES applies these judges to RAG outputs and combines their predictions with a human preference validation set using prediction-powered inference. The method produces performance estimates and 95% confidence intervals for comparing RAG systems.

Experiments / 4.1 .. 4.3 body

The unit describes how ARES is evaluated as a system for ranking retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. It uses FLAN-T5 XXL to generate synthetic queries and answers, DeBERTa-v3-Large as a fine-tuned judge, and several benchmarks from KILT and SuperGLUE, including Natural Questions, HotpotQA, FEVER, Wizards of Wikipedia, MultiRC, and ReCoRD. The authors construct mock RAG systems with known success rates from 70% to 90% on context relevance and answer relevance, while excluding answer faithfulness on these datasets because hallucinated answers lack human annotations. They measure ranking quality with the Kendall rank correlation coefficient and regard values above 0.9 as successful.

Results & Analysis / 5.1 .. 5.4 body

This unit evaluates ARES against RAGAS and GPT-3.5 for ranking RAG systems and scoring context relevance, answer relevance, and answer faithfulness. ARES generally achieves higher Kendall’s tau and accuracy than the baselines, uses PPI to improve predictions and provide confidence intervals, and requires fewer human annotations than sampled annotation methods. On AIS, ARES distinguishes faithful from non-attributed answers in Wizards of Wikipedia and CNN/DM, while on existing RAG systems it achieves average Kendall’s tau values of 0.91 for context relevance and 0.97 for answer relevance. The unit also finds that ARES generalizes across KILT and SuperGLUE domain shifts but performs poorly under larger shifts involving languages, code, and extraction tasks.

Conclusion body

The conclusion presents ARES, an automated evaluation framework for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). ARES fine-tunes lightweight LLM judges on synthetically generated queries and answers and evaluates RAG systems by separately scoring context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance. Across eight datasets from KILT, SuperGLUE, and AIS, ARES accurately scored and ranked RAG systems and outperformed RAGAS. The authors suggest exploring GPT-4 for human labeling, more robust synthetic datasets, logits for improved PPI confidence intervals, and more sophisticated fine-tuned judges.

Limitations body

ARES relies on roughly 150–300 annotations from a human preference validation set, with more annotations improving the system. These annotations may require familiarity with the RAG system’s domain, especially in specialized areas such as law, medicine, and finance. ARES uses GPU-based hardware to run DeBERTa-v3-Large and FLAN-T5-XXL, which require about 32GB of memory and several hours for fine-tuning or generation. The evaluation datasets are all in English, and future work should examine ARES in other languages using different LLMs.
