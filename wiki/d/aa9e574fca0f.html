Child's Health Primer for Primary Classes

Jane Andrews · 1885 · 24 sections

Abstract written by a model

Child’s Health Primer for Primary Classes, Pathfinder Physiology No. 1, is a primary-school textbook by Jane Andrews on anatomy, physiology, and hygiene. Endorsed by the Scientific Department of the Women’s Christian Temperance Union of the United States, it especially emphasizes the claimed harmful effects of alcoholic drinks, stimulants, narcotics, and tobacco. It uses short lessons, examples, stories, and review questions, with pronunciation guidance in its front matter. The book begins with bones, joints, the spine, ribs, posture, footwear, and warnings about tobacco during growth. It then explains muscles and tendons, exercise and idleness, and the brain, spinal cord, and nerves that direct movement and sensation. These early chapters argue that sleep and rest protect the nervous system while alcohol and tobacco injure it. A central sequence explains alcohol’s production and nature: fruit juice ferments into wine or cider; barley is made into beer; and distillation produces stronger liquors such as brandy, rum, whiskey, and gin. Alcohol is repeatedly presented as a poison that can create desire, drunkenness, weakness, and failure. Tobacco is traced from the farmer’s crop to cigars and snuff, with nicotine described as a strong poison. Opium and soothing-syrup are likewise condemned as narcotics that harm nerves and babies. The later chapters survey organs and bodily processes: teeth, stomach, gastric juice, food, water, salt, lime, digestion, blood, heart, lungs, skin, and the senses. They promote simple food, clean water, exercise, fresh and ventilated air, cleanliness, and care of the eyes and ears. Alcohol and tobacco are linked throughout to injury of digestion, blood, heart, lungs, senses, muscles, nerves, and clear thinking. A discussion of body heat argues that alcohol only gives temporary warmth while increasing heat loss, citing Arctic explorers and the Greely party. The book closes by treating alcohol and tobacco as economic as well as bodily harms. It says money spent on beer, wine, whiskey, and tobacco is wasted and associates liquor with sickness, poverty, crime, policemen, and prisons.

front matter front matter

Child's Health Primer is a textbook for primary classes. It is identified as Pathfinder Physiology No. 1 and concerns anatomy, physiology, and hygiene. The book gives special reference to the effects of alcoholic drinks, stimulants, and narcotics upon the human system. It is indorsed by the Scientific Department of the Women's Christian Temperance Union of the United States.

Child's Health Primer for Primary Classes .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. front matter

The Child's Health Primer introduces primary-school pupils to physiology and hygiene, especially the effects of alcohol, other narcotics, and tobacco on the human body. This unit explains joints, bones, the spine, ribs, and care of the body, including posture and footwear. It teaches children that tobacco is harmful during growth, with special warnings about cigarettes and chewing tobacco. The unit concludes with review questions about the lessons.

CHAPTER II. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. front matter

The unit explains that muscles move the joints by tightening and relaxing, much like stretched rubber straps move a door. Muscles are attached to bones by strong cords called tendons, and different body parts have stronger muscles according to the work they do. Exercise and active work strengthen muscles, while idleness weakens them. Alcohol can change part of the muscles into fat, reducing their strength, so drinking beer does not make a person stronger.

CHAPTER III. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. front matter

The unit explains how the brain, spinal cord, nerves, and muscles communicate to control movement, breathing, feeling, and other body functions. It describes how nerves carry messages to the brain and how the brain sends orders back to muscles, including when a finger touches a hot stove. It teaches that the brain and nerves need sleep, rest, and good care. It warns that alcohol, liquor, and tobacco injure or deaden the brain and nerves and can make people careless, unhealthy, or poorer scholars.

_] CHAPTER IV. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. front matter

This unit explains how fruit juices change when their sugar turns into alcohol and a gas. It describes how grapes are pressed into grape-juice, which can become wine, and how apple-juice can become cider. It teaches that alcohol is a poisonous liquid that can harm the body and mind and may lead a person to become a drunkard. It also warns that wine and cider are unsafe drinks because they contain alcohol.

] CHAPTER V. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains how the brewer makes beer from barley by sprouting the grain so its starch changes into sugar, then adding water and yeast. The yeast helps change the sugar into gas and alcohol, and hops may give the beer a bitter taste. It teaches that barley could be good food, but beer contains alcohol, which is described as a poison. Two boys are compared: one drinks alcoholic beverages and the other abstains, and the abstaining boy is presented as more likely to succeed and be trusted.

] CHAPTER VI. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains distilling by showing how boiling water becomes steam and then returns to water when cooled. It teaches that alcohol turns to vapor more easily than water turns to steam, so alcohol can be collected first from liquids containing it, though repeated distillation is needed to make it pure. Brandy, rum, whiskey, and gin are distilled from wine, cider, and liquors made from corn, rye, or barley. The unit warns that distilled liquor contains much alcohol, while people who drink large amounts of weaker beverages may also become drunkards.

CHAPTER VII. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains that alcohol looks like water but burns with a blue, smokeless flame that gives much heat. Alcohol preserves dead bodies, as shown by a tree-toad that remains unchanged after years in a bottle of alcohol. Beer, wine, and cider contain a little alcohol, while brandy, rum, whiskey, and gin are nearly one half alcohol. Alcohol can create a growing desire for alcoholic drinks, leading some drinkers to become drunkards.

CHAPTER VIII. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

A farmer replaced corn, wheat, and potatoes with tobacco, whose leaves were dried, pressed into cakes, rolled into cigars, or ground into snuff. Tobacco contains nicotine, a strong poison, and working with it can make people pale and sickly. Boys often become sick when they first try smoking, but may continue until they get used to it. Smoking does not usually kill a man immediately because he does not swallow all the nicotine, but taking small amounts of poison repeatedly is unsafe and not truly helpful.

CHAPTER IX. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains that narcotics are substances that can put the nerves to sleep, naming alcohol, tobacco, and opium. It says that opium is a poison made from poppy juice and used in medicines. Soothing-syrup containing opium may quiet babies by injuring their tender nerves and poisoning their bodies. Children are warned never to give soothing-syrup to babies instead of caring for them patiently.

CHAPTER X. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

This unit explains that organs are body parts with special work, focusing especially on the teeth, stomach, and organs in the chest and abdomen. It describes how the teeth cut and grind food, while the stomach and gastric juice change food into a fluid that can nourish every part of the body. It also gives instructions for caring for the teeth and stomach and explains how alcohol and tobacco harm digestion, the mouth, and other people. Doctors learned about the stomach by observing food placed into a soldier's stomach through an opening left by a wound.

notes notes

The unit concerns food and the organs involved in preparing it. It states that the liver partly prepares food. Some other organs also help prepare the food.

] CHAPTER XI. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The chapter explains what the body needs from food to grow, remain strong, and stay well. It describes the importance of pure water, lime for bones, and salt in the diet. It identifies flesh-making foods for muscles and fat-making foods for warmth and comfort. It also explains how sugar and starch make fat and warns that candy may contain harmful substances and cannot provide complete nourishment.

CHAPTER XII. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains how dinner is prepared, chewed, swallowed, digested, and carried through the body. Cooking makes meat, vegetables, and bread easier for the mouth and stomach to work on, while ripe fruit does not need fire-cooking. Saliva changes starch into sugar, and the stomach mixes the food with gastric juice before it enters the intestines and blood. The blood carries nourishment throughout the body to make and mend bones, muscles, skin, hair, eyes, and brain.

CHAPTER XIII. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The chapter explains that people should eat good, simple food, exercise in the open air, and breathe pure air to grow and keep strong. It teaches that alcohol, including beer and cider containing alcohol, does not give real strength and may make people feel strong only temporarily. A captain found that sailors worked better and felt stronger without rum when they were given plentiful food and hot coffee. The chapter concludes that strength of mind is the best kind of strength and that alcohol and tobacco weaken clear thinking.

CHAPTER XIV. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains that the heart is a strong muscular organ in the chest that pumps blood through arteries and veins. The blood carries food from the stomach to every part of the body and must be especially pure for the brain. Exercise in fresh air strengthens the heart and warms the body, while alcohol can poison the blood, affect the brain, and harm the heart. The review questions concern the heart, blood circulation, exercise, food, alcohol, and related organs and tissues.

CHAPTER XV. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

This chapter explains how the lungs help purify the blood by taking in fresh air and sending out impure air. It describes the need for the lungs to have room to expand and for people to keep rooms well ventilated. It teaches that breathing through the nose helps keep dust and impurities from reaching the lungs. It also asks readers to apply what they have learned about alcohol’s effects on muscles and nerves to the lungs.

CHAPTER XVI. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The chapter explains how the skin of the body removes waste matter through perspiration tubes and pores. It describes the care needed to keep the skin, clothing, and bed-clothes clean and well aired. It also reviews the work of the stomach, blood, heart, lungs, and skin in keeping the body healthy. These processes continue day and night without conscious thought because nerves send messages to the muscles.

CHAPTER XVII. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains the five senses, with special attention to sight and hearing. It describes how the eyes, pupils, nerves of sight, ears, and nerve of hearing work, and teaches care of the eyes and ears. Stories about boys who looked at the sun, a boy who made himself cross-eyed, and the boy's father illustrate the dangers of injuring these organs. It also explains that alcohol can weaken the work of all the senses.

CHAPTER XVIII. / WHAT MAKES US WARM? .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains that body heat is produced by work inside the body, carried by the blood, and lost through the skin and breath. Exercise increases blood flow and warmth, while clothing helps retain existing heat rather than producing it. Alcohol creates a temporary feeling of warmth by bringing hot blood to the skin, but it increases heat loss and dulls awareness of cold. Accounts of Arctic explorers and the Greely party show that alcohol and tobacco did not help people endure severe cold, hunger, and freezing.

CHAPTER XIX. / COST OF ALCOHOL. .. REVIEW QUESTIONS. body

The unit explains that spending money on alcohol or liquor is wasteful because it brings harm rather than useful benefits. It describes how beer, wine, and whiskey can quickly consume a dollar, and says that avoiding liquor could reduce sickness, poverty, crime, and the need for policemen and prisons. It also explains that buying tobacco burns up money while harming the smoker. The unit states that six hundred million dollars are spent on tobacco in the country each year.
