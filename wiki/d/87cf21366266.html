Abstract written by a model

This ACL 2024 Findings paper, “RetrievalQA: Assessing Adaptive Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Short-form Open-Domain Question Answering,” is by Zihan Zhang, Meng Fang, and Ling Chen. It studies adaptive retrieval-augmented generation (ARAG): having a large language model decide whether a question needs external retrieval, rather than retrieving for every query. The authors argue that indiscriminate retrieval can hurt performance and raise inference costs. The paper introduces RetrievalQA, a benchmark for this decision problem. Its 1,271 retained short-form questions target new world and long-tail knowledge that models cannot reliably answer from their parameters and therefore require external information. Questions are drawn from RealTimeQA, FreshQA, PopQA, TriviaQA, and ToolQA, then filtered using GPT-4 closed-book answers; 1,514 questions answerable from GPT-2 parametric knowledge are also added to avoid favoring systems that retrieve too often. Sanity checks indicate that the retrieval-required questions are very difficult for models without external knowledge. Experiments compare standard RAG, calibration-based approaches, model-based approaches, Self-RAG, vanilla prompting, and the authors’ method, using retrieval and match accuracy. Retrieval generally improves QA, but Self-RAG is highly dependent on threshold tuning, and vanilla prompting does not consistently improve retrieval decisions as model size grows. Models more readily recognize retrieval needs for new world knowledge than for long-tail knowledge. To address these issues, the authors propose Time-Aware Adaptive REtrieval (TA-ARE), a calibration-free, training-free prompting method. TA-ARE supplies current-date instructions and in-context demonstrations to help models judge whether retrieval is needed. It improves retrieval accuracy by an average of 14.9% and QA accuracy by 6.7% across baselines. Ablations show that time awareness and relevant examples matter, with four demonstrations, two “Yes” and two “No”, performing best. The paper notes limits in dataset sourcing and GPT-4 filtering, its focus on short-form rather than long-form generation, possible noisy or inadequate retrieved documents, and the absence of prompt tuning. The dataset uses public sources, excludes personally identifiable information, and is accompanied by online code and data. The work was supported by the Australian Research Council.

Abstract body

The work examines adaptive retrieval-augmented generation (ARAG), which determines whether a query requires retrieval rather than retrieving indiscriminately. It introduces RetrievalQA, a benchmark of 1,271 short-form questions covering new world and long-tail knowledge that is absent from large language models and therefore requires external information. The authors find that calibration-based methods depend heavily on threshold tuning and that vanilla prompting does not reliably guide retrieval decisions. They propose Time-Aware Adaptive REtrieval (TA-ARE), which helps large language models assess retrieval necessity without calibration or additional training.

Introduction body

The unit concerns retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which augments large language models with retrieved relevant information. RAG has become increasingly popular for knowledge-intensive tasks, including open-domain question-answering. However, standard RAG methods retrieve information indiscriminately, regardless of the input query, which can reduce task performance and increase inference costs.

opening body

The unit investigates whether large language models can make reliable, calibration-free adaptive retrieval decisions. It introduces RetrievalQA, a short-form question-answering dataset covering new-world and long-tail knowledge that is absent from the models’ parameters, requiring models to retrieve external information when necessary. The authors benchmark calibration-based and model-based approaches and find that Self-RAG requires threshold tuning, while vanilla prompting does not reliably guide retrieval decisions. They propose Time-Aware Adaptive REtrieval (TA-ARE), which uses in-context learning to improve adaptive retrieval without calibration or additional training.

Dataset Construction body

The dataset construction aims to evaluate whether LLMs can answer questions only by consulting external resources, focusing on new world knowledge and long-tail knowledge. The authors collect questions from RealTimeQA, FreshQA, PopQA, TriviaQA, and ToolQA, then use GPT-4 closed-book answers to filter out questions with any shared tokens with the ground truth. After filtering, they retain 1,271 of 9,336 questions and add 1,514 questions answerable using GPT-2's parametric knowledge to reduce bias toward methods that retrieve more often. Sanity checks show that RetrievalQA is extremely difficult for various LLM sizes without external knowledge.

RetrievalQA Challenges Adaptive RAG / 3.1 .. 3.4 body

This unit formalizes Adaptive RAG for open-domain QA tasks and evaluates adaptive retrieval approaches on RetrievalQA. It compares standard RAG, calibration-based methods, model-based methods, Self-RAG, Vanilla prompting, and TA-ARE using retrieval accuracy and match accuracy. The experiments show that retrieval generally improves QA performance, Self-RAG depends strongly on threshold tuning, and Vanilla prompting does not reliably scale with model size. The analysis finds that LLMs recognize the need for retrieval more effectively for new world knowledge questions than for long-tail knowledge questions.

Improving Adaptive RAG Prompting / Method .. 4.3 / Conclusion body

This unit presents TA-ARE, a time-aware adaptive retrieval method that uses current-date instructions and in-context demonstrations to improve retrieval decisions without calibration or additional training. It reports that TA-ARE improves retrieval and question-answering accuracy across baselines, with an average gain of 14.9% in retrieval and 6.7% in QA accuracy. Ablation studies find that time awareness and relevant demonstrations help models decide when retrieval is necessary, with four demonstrations, two [Yes] and two [No] examples, performing best. The paper introduces RetrievalQA as a dataset for evaluating adaptive RAG on short-form open-domain question answering.

Limitations body

The limitations concern the dataset, retrieval process, task scope, and prompting methods used in the work. The dataset is mainly collected from existing sources, and GPT-4 is used to filter questions, though some filtering errors may remain despite preliminary human checking. The study focuses on short-form QA and does not assess long-form generation tasks, while acknowledging that retrieved documents may be noisy or insufficient. The authors also do not perform prompt tuning, although better prompts may further improve performance.

Ethical Statement body

The RetrievalQA dataset was created to evaluate LLMs’ self-awareness ability to decide when to retrieve external resources. It was constructed using only publicly available data sources, including five publicly available datasets whose licenses were rigorously vetted for compliance. The creators examined the data for privacy concerns and violations and did not collect personally identifiable information. They state that the research followed legal and ethical standards and adhered to the terms of use and policies of OpenAI and Meta.
