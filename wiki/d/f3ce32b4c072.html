CLERC: A Dataset for U. S. Legal Case Retrieval and Retrieval-Augmented Analysis Generation

Abe Bohan Hou · 2025 · 16 sections
"CLERC: A Dataset for U. S. Legal Case Retrieval and Retrieval-Augmented Analysis Generation" by Abe Hou, Orion Weller, Guanghui Qin, Eugene Yang, Dawn Lawrie, Nils Holzenberger, Andrew Blair-Stanek, Benjamin Van Durme (2025), licensed CC BY. Shown here as extracted text, with facts drawn from it. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.findings-naacl.441

The document presents CLERC (Case Law Evaluation and Retrieval Corpus), a dataset built with legal professionals from U.S. federal case law in CAP through September 21, 2021, to support systems that assist legal writing. It contains 25.5 million instances and targets three connected tasks: legal document retrieval, passage retrieval, and retrieval-augmented legal analysis generation. The central practical setting is lawyers drafting motions and briefs by finding relevant precedent, citing it, and synthesizing cited authority into analysis supporting a reasoning goal. CLERC is prepared in forms including CLERC/doc, CLERC/passage, and CLERC/generation. Documents are concatenated, passages chunked, and analytical paragraphs selected. Retrieval queries are created from 300-word contexts by removing central citations or all citations, with direct and indirect query types distinguished. Generation inputs combine prior case context with salient passages from cited cases. An example query concerns summary judgment and cites Fed.R.Civ.P. 56(c), Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. The document places CLERC among prior work on legal retrieval and generation, long-context generation, and long-document retrieval, arguing that existing benchmarks do not sufficiently test BERT-based and long-context models on clearly defined legal retrieval tasks. Experiments show substantial shortcomings. BM25 surpasses zero-shot late-interaction and bi-encoder retrieval models, while fine-tuned DPR models, especially a LegalBERT-based model, perform better. Still, zero-shot IR reaches only 48.3% recall@1000, and performance varies with query type, length, and domain shift. Repeated legal terms and non-central citations can distract retrievers. For generation, supplying texts of cited cases improves citation coverage, precision, and hallucination-related measures, but outputs remain factually and analytically limited. GPT-4o obtains the highest ROUGE F-scores while hallucinating the most. The document further argues that citation metrics alone can be misleading, illustrated with Llama-3-8B, and proposes adapting FActScore for legal factual recall. The work concludes that CLERC is an initial interdisciplinary step toward legal-assistance systems, but current systems, including fine-tuned ones, are not yet suitable for these tasks. It notes possible OCR errors in CAP data and warns that historical case law can carry racism, sexism, and other harmful ideologies that legal intelligence systems may propagate.

Abstract body

The abstract concerns CLERC, a dataset created with legal professionals to support systems that assist with legal writing. It focuses on two backbone tasks: information retrieval and retrieval-augmented generation. CLERC evaluates whether models can find relevant citations for legal analysis and compile cited text and prior context into a coherent analysis supporting a reasoning goal. Benchmark results show that current approaches struggle: GPT-4o achieves the highest ROUGE F-scores but hallucinates the most, while zero-shot IR models reach only 48.3% recall@1000.

Introduction body

The text introduces CLERC (Case Law Evaluation and Retrieval Corpus), a dataset designed to support case retrieval and legal analysis generation using U.S. case law data. It describes how lawyers and legal professionals use relevant precedents when drafting legal documents such as motions and briefs. CLERC contains 25.5 million instances and supports legal information retrieval, passage retrieval, and retrieval-augmented analysis generation. Evaluations show that current information retrieval models struggle to retrieve relevant documents and that large language models frequently hallucinate.

4 .. 4.2.2 body

This unit reports experiments with CLERC on legal citation retrieval and legal analysis generation. In retrieval, BM25 outperforms zero-shot late-interaction and bi-encoder models, while fine-tuned DPR models, especially the LegalBERT-based model, perform substantially better. Retrieval is affected by query type, query length, and domain shift between standard retrieval data and legal text. In generation, providing the texts of cited cases improves citation coverage, precision, and hallucination-related scores, although generated analyses still have serious factual and analytical limitations.

5 / 5.1 .. 5.2 body

This unit examines why retrieval and generation models struggle on the CLERC retrieval task and related legal analysis evaluation. BM25 and other retrieval models can be distracted by frequently repeated legal terms and retrieve cases containing non-central citations. The unit also shows that high citation-metric scores do not guarantee useful analytical generation, using an example from Llama-3-8B. It proposes adapting FActScore to legal text to evaluate factual recall more effectively.

6 body

Case law data is presented as a high-quality resource for building intelligence systems that assist legal professionals. Through an interdisciplinary effort with legal professionals, the authors build CLERC, a dataset for training and evaluating models on long-context case retrieval and analysis generation tasks. The work aims to encourage further discoveries in AI built with legal text data.

7 body

The work is an initial step toward developing systems that can aid legal professionals. It finds that current systems, including fine-tuned systems, are not currently well-suited to helping legal professionals with these tasks. The case data was gathered through OCR by CAP and may contain small errors, although the analysis found it to be generally high quality.

Ethical Considerations body

The work curates a dataset based on a corpus of historical U.S. federal case law. It warns that past case law may reflect racism, sexism, and other ideologies considered controversial or undesirable for society and people's well-being. It cautions that legal intelligence systems built on case law data may propagate historical biases and ideologies, and it encourages the NLP community to address these risks.
