Part of Balfour, Frances Campbell, Lady, 1858–1931

An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall

Dinah Maria Craik · 1884 · 20 sections

An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall is a first-person travel narrative, written more than a year after the journey, recounting fifteen “innocent days” in September 1881. The narrator travels with two young female companions, affectionately called her “chickens,” seeking Cornwall’s people, coasts, Land’s End, and the landscapes and legends, especially those of King Arthur, that had fascinated her since childhood. Leaving Exeter, they reach Falmouth and make the Lizard their first main base. With the kind, dependable driver Charles, and later boatman John Curgenven, they visit Goonhilly Down, Kennack Sands, Poltesco, Cadgwith, Mullion Cove, Kynance, Housel Cove, Church Cove, and the Lizard Lights. Their experiences mix bathing, sketching, walks, carriage journeys, boat expeditions, churches, village gatherings, and visits to caves and dangerous rocky shores. John and Charles supply local knowledge and personal warmth: Charles tells of his wife’s and baby’s deaths, while John recounts wrecks, rescues, coast-guard work, and Arthurian associations. Storms interrupt the holiday, confining the party indoors, but clear days restore their pleasure in the sea, moonlight, and Cornish hospitality. Throughout, the narrator reflects on grief, mortality, belief, courage, and the possible permanence of human affection and life. After nine days around the Lizard, they travel through Helstone and Marazion, visit St. Michael’s Mount, Penzance, Castle-an-Dinas, St. Ives, Logan Rock, and finally Land’s End. Land’s End, long anticipated as the central goal of the journey, fulfills their hopes despite rain preventing fuller exploration. Their final coastal day is spent at Whitesand Bay and Sennen before an overnight drive back toward Falmouth. The last stage carries them into Arthurian country: Camelford, Slaughter Bridge, Delabole, Trevena, Tintagel, Bossinney Cove, Boscastle, and Porth Hern. The travelers repeatedly imagine Arthur, Guinevere, Mordred, Tristram, Iseult, Uther Pendragon, and Ygrayne against the cliffs, ruins, moors, and sea. Their final boat trip into a frightening sea-cave provides an enduring image of Cornwall’s wild coast. In the envoi, the narrator calls the book a simple memorial of a happy shared time, preserving the brightness of the old hen and her chickens’ journey.

An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall / ] DAY THE FIRST I believe in holidays. body

The narrator describes planning a restrained Cornwall tour with two female companions, whom she calls her “chickens,” emphasizing obedience, light luggage, health, and good spirits. She explains her long-standing desire to see Cornwall, its people, and especially Land's End, inspired by childhood stories and an interest in Cornish courage and justice. The party leaves Exeter on 1 September 1881, travels through Plymouth and other Cornish districts to Falmouth, and chooses the quiet Green Bank hotel over the fashionable Falmouth Hotel. At Falmouth, they arrange lodgings and transport for the Lizard, buy provisions, and end the first day confident that their journey is proceeding well.

The narrator and her two young companions travel by carriage from Falmouth to the Lizard with Charles as their driver. Charles proves dependable and kind, but becomes sorrowful when the narrator learns that his wife died after their baby died. They pass through Trelowarren and across Goonhilly Down, where they see Cornish heath and serpentine stone. At the Lizard, the young companions explore a cove while the narrator remains above because the descent is too difficult for her.

The narrator and companions take a difficult evening boat trip from the Lizard with Charles and the boatman John Curgenven, observing the rough sea, rocks, coastline, and wildlife. They consider rowing to Kynance Cove but turn back as darkness approaches, witnessing the sunset, moonrise, and Lizard Lights. John describes Pistol Meadow, coastal shipwrecks, coast-guard work, lifeboat rescues, and local legends connected with King Arthur. After landing, Charles and John guide the narrator along narrow Cornish hedge walls, and the party safely returns home.

] DAY THE THIRD "And a beautiful day it is, ladies, though it won't do for Kynance. body

Charles proposes an ambitious day trip for the women travelers from Lizard Town to Kennack Sands, Poltesco, and Cadgwith, arranging to return them home for dinner and take them to Mullion afterward. The women visit Kennack Sands, admire its shore and flowers, tour the serpentine works at Poltesco, and continue to Cadgwith and the Devil's Frying-pan. They learn about the dangers of bathing and the Atlantic waves, the character of the Cornish fishing communities, and the severe hardships endured on the coast during winter.

The narrator and her companions travel with Charles and his horse from the Lizard Downs through Mullion to Mullion Cove, where they explore the sea-caves before returning to the Old Inn. Mary's brother receives them at the Old Inn while Mary Mundy is away in Helstone, but Mary returns in time to provide them with a substantial tea. At Mullion Cove, the narrator sees the serpentine rocks, the sea, an artist painting in a cave, and visitors wading through the rising tide. Charles later drives the horse toward Falmouth, while the narrator and her companions spend the night at the Old Inn.

DAY THE FOURTH Sunday, September 4th--and we had started on September 1st; was it possible we had only been travelling four days? body

The narrator and her companions spend Sunday, September 4th, exploring the cliffs, church, churchyard, and sea-pools around Lizard and Landewednack. They attend the church of Landewednack, observe its congregation and historic graves, and enjoy a sunset followed by moonlight. During the night, the weather changes abruptly: a violent Lizard storm brings torrential rain and fierce wind. The narrator concludes that the weather has broken, threatening the harvest festival and the companions' holiday in Cornwall.

DAY THE FIFTH "Hope for the best, and be prepared for the worst," had been the motto of our journey. body

The travelers spend a very wet day indoors at the Lizard, making a fire, reading, sewing, knitting, and decorating their ugly room with artwork. They debate whether King Arthur existed and whether it is better to believe in an impossible ideal man than to accept a low ideal. Charles visits after bringing a party to the Lizard in the storm, and the travelers later wade into Lizard Town to shop. As the weather clears slightly, they reflect on Cornish coast-life, the coming harvest festival at Landewednack, and the mysteries of the laws of the universe and sorrow.

DAY THE SIXTH And a day absolutely divine! body

On DAY THE SIXTH, the narrator and companions enjoy a perfect autumn morning at Housel Cove, bathing in the Atlantic and idling on the shore. They spend the afternoon sketching near Kynance before attending the rectory tea, where rich and poor Cornish folk meet courteously and a village wind band plays badly. The evening church service is crowded and beautifully performed, followed by a walk through the moonlit countryside. The day ends with the wind band playing again near the hotels, while the party flees toward the familiar glow of the Lizard Lights.

DAY THE SEVENTH John Curgenven had said last night, with his air of tender patronising, half regal, half paternal, which we declared always reminded us of King Arthur--"Ladies, whenever you settle to go to Kynance, I'll take you. body

John Curgenven guides the narrator and the girls partway across the downs toward Kynance Cove, warning them about the tide and dangerous rocks. At the Cove, the girls bathe, visit the Bellows, and climb Asparagus Island, while the narrator watches and speaks with an elderly clergyman whose daughters are nearby. The advancing tide forces the visitors to leave, and the group returns over the downs before walking to Lizard Point to watch the sunset. News of sickness and death casts a temporary sadness over the evening, though the narrator finds comfort in the moonlit landscape.

" DAY THE EIGHTH And seven days were all we could allow ourselves at the Lizard, if we meant to see the rest of Cornwall. body

The traveling women spend their eighth and final day at the Lizard, bathing at Housel Cove, viewing the coast, and visiting the Lizard Lights. They learn about launce-fishing, hear the lighthouse attendant's accounts of shipwrecks, and observe a perfect solar rainbow. They arrange a journey to Marazion with Charles, adding a planned boat excursion to sea-caves near Church Cove. The narrator ends the day reflecting on human mortality, death, and the possibility of an imperishable afterlife.

" DAY THE NINTH And our last at the Lizard, which a week ago had been to us a mere word or dot in a map; now we carried away from it a living human interest in everything and everybody. body

On their last day at the Lizard, the narrator and her children cross Church Cove in John Curgenven's boat to visit Raven's Ugo and Dolor Ugo, while John recounts the rescue of survivors from the emigrant ship Brest. After a difficult landing and a delay caused by a leak in the boat, they travel through Cornwall to Helstone, visiting familiar places and receiving generous hospitality. In Helstone they meet an unnamed Cornish engineer, who warmly welcomes the family connected with his former employers and shares his home, inventions, garden produce, and memories.

The travelers pass through the country around Helstone toward Marazion, missing several local curiosities, including Loe Pool and Nonsloe House. They reach Marazion after sunset, stay at a welcoming inn, and explore the bay at low tide. Charles arranges a Norwegian boatman to take them bathing and suggests a moonlit row around St. Michael's Mount. The Mount appears especially grand and mysterious by moonlight, inspiring thoughts of its legends and history.

] DAY THE TENTH I cannot advise Marazion as a bathing place. body

The travelers leave Marazion’s unsuitable beach, visit St. Michael’s Mount, and then travel through Penzance and the Cornish countryside toward St. Ives. At St. Michael’s Mount they see its fortress, residence, chapel, antiquarian treasures, and fishing village. Charles drives them to Castle-an-Dinas, where they view the lonely moor associated with King Arthur, before continuing to St. Ives. In St. Ives they meet a kindly local shopkeeper, inspect the town, and then hurry back toward Penzance, concluding that the man going to St. Ives was the least fortunate figure in the nursery rhyme.

DAY THE ELEVENTH The last thing before retiring, we had glanced out on a gloomy sea, a starless sky, pitch darkness, broken only by those moving lights on St. body

The narrator and traveling party travel from Penzance to Land's End on a quiet Sunday, stopping to see the Logan Rock before reaching the coast. At Land's End they eat at the First and Last Inn, explore the cliffs with a sailor guide, and gaze across the sea toward the Atlantic and the submerged land of Lyonesse. Rain prevents them from properly visiting Whitesand Bay and other sights, so they return home, grateful that the long-imagined journey has fulfilled their hopes.

DAY THE TWELFTH Monday morning. body

The narrator and her companions spend their final day in Cornwall at Whitesand Bay, choosing it over Botallack Mine and other attractions. They collect tiny shells, speak with people at Sennen, and take a final look at Land's End before returning to Penzance. At Marazion they learn that Princess Frederica of Hanover and the Baron Von Pawel-Rammingen are visiting St. Michael's Mount. Charles prepares to drive them from Penzance to Falmouth overnight, arriving by daylight.

DAY THE THIRTEENTH Into King Arthurs land--Tintagel his birth-place, and Camelford, where he fought his last battle--the legendary region of which one may believe as much or as little as one pleases--we were going to-day. body

The travelers journey through Cornwall toward Tintagel and Camelford, visiting places associated with King Arthur and the Arthurian legend. They recount Arthur’s birth, kingship, marriage to Queen Guinevere, conflict with Mordred, wounding at Camelford, and departure with three queens. They visit Slaughter Bridge near Camelford, imagine Dozmare Pool, pass the Delabole quarries, and stay in Trevena before viewing Tintagel Castle and its cliffs. A cloud resembling a boat prompts them to imagine Arthur and the three queens, until a joke about Arthur’s luggage ends the sentiment.

DAYS FOURTEENTH, FIFTEENTH, AND SIXTEENTH-- And all Arthurian days, so I will condense them into one chapter, and not spin out the hours that were flying so fast. body

The narrator and her companions spend their last days in Cornwall visiting Tintagel, Bossinney Cove, Boscastle, and the sea-caves beyond Tintagel. They reflect on Arthurian legends, including the stories of Tristram and Iseult, Uther Pendragon, Ygrayne, and King Arthur, and consider the contrast between barbaric ancient society and the ideal of Arthurian chivalry. At Tintagel they explore ancient ruins, a spring, a chapel, and views associated with Arthurian history, while at Bossinney Cove they meet an old man collecting sand with eleven donkeys. Their final boat trip from Porth Hern takes them into a terrifying sea-cave, leaving the narrator with a vivid memory of the wild coast and its legendary past.

] L'ENVOI Written more than a year after. body

L'ENVOI was written more than a year after the events it describes, when the old hen and her chickens were safely at home. The writer finishes the history of fifteen innocent days during which we three took an Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall. The writer suggests that some readers may find little in the account, while others may see more, but regards it as a simple memorial of a good time whose brightness may endure into the future.
