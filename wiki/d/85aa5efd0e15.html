Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~ · 74 sections

This is a travel and antiquarian account of Greece, organized as a journey through Attica, Corinthia, Laconia, Messenia, and Elis. It describes harbours, roads, towns, sanctuaries, temples, statues, paintings, tombs, rivers, customs, festivals, games, local myths, and political memories, continually joining visible monuments to stories of gods, heroes, artists, rulers, and wars. It begins in Attica, above all Athens: its approaches, Acropolis, Parthenon, courts, theatre, Painted Chamber, temples, graves, and surrounding places such as Marathon, Eleusis, Megara, Salamis, and Oropus. Athene, Theseus, Demeter, Triptolemus, Apollo, and Zeus dominate its sacred traditions; Themistocles, Pericles, Plato, Demosthenes, Adrian, and artists including Phidias, Praxiteles, and Euphranor figure in its civic memory. Interwoven historical digressions trace Alexander’s successors, Ptolemy, Antigonus, Lysimachus, and Pyrrhus, ending in Pyrrhus’s death at Argos. The route then enters Corinth and Sicyon, recounting Corinth’s destruction and rebuilding, its Isthmian sites, rulers, and myths, before moving through Phlius, Nemea, Mycenæ, Tiryns, Argos, Epidaurus, Ægina, Trœzen, and Hermione. Major themes are the Argive royal traditions, the return of the Heraclidæ, Theseus, and the healing sanctuary of Æsculapius at Epidaurus. In Laconia, the account combines Sparta’s legendary origins, kings, Lycurgan institutions, wars, and decline with a detailed survey of Spartan monuments and rituals, including the contests of youths and the cult of Orthian Artemis. It extends through Amyclæ, Therapne, Gythium, Cythera, Tænarum, and other coastal places. Messenia centers on resistance to Lacedæmon. The wars of Euphaes, Aristodemus, and especially Aristomenes lead from Amphea and Ithome to the fall of Eira, exile, settlement at Zancle, and eventual restoration of Messene by Epaminondas and the Thebans. The final books reach Elis and Olympia. They recount Elis’s history and the revival, contests, officials, rites, cheating penalties, monuments, temples, and treasures of the Olympian games. Olympian Zeus, Pelops, Hercules, athletes such as Polydamas, Euthymus, Diagoras, and Theagenes, and the many statues and offerings at Altis dominate the close, which ends with Elis, Cyllene, and the boundary with Achaia.

Description of Greece / BOOK I.--ATTICA. body

Pausanias describes the harbours, monuments, temples, walls, paintings, and myths of Attica, especially the approaches to Athens and the city’s public buildings. He recounts the roles of Themistocles, Theseus, Conon, and other Athenians, and identifies works by artists including Praxiteles, Phidias, and Euphranor. He also digresses on the Galati or Celts, their invasion of Greece, their defeat near Thermopylæ and Delphi, and their later settlement in Asia Minor. The account ends with the tribal heroes of Athens and the benefactions of Emperor Adrian.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit records the early actions of Ptolemy and the origins of his rule over Egypt, as well as the rise and fall of Antigonus. Ptolemy seized Egypt, Syria, and Phœnicia, resisted Perdiccas, formed alliances against Antigonus, and defended Egypt against invasion. Antigonus and Demetrius failed to capture Egypt and Rhodes, and Antigonus later lost his power and died. The text also discusses Ptolemy’s alleged descent from Philip and the distribution of Alexander’s empire into separate kingdoms.

CHAPTER VII. body

The unit describes Ptolemy I Soter’s family conflicts, his wars involving Magas of Cyrene and Antiochus I, and the succession and conduct of later Ptolemies. It recounts Attalus I’s rise, Philetærus’s revolt, Demosthenes’ exile, and the statues and historical figures commemorated by the Athenians. It traces Lysimachus’s wars, marriage to Arsinoe, conflict with Agathocles, and eventual death against Seleucus I. It then follows Pyrrhus of Epirus from his ancestry and struggles in Epirus and Macedonia to his campaigns in Italy and Sicily against the Romans and Carthaginians.

CHAPTER XIII. body

Pyrrhus withdrew from Italy to Epirus by spreading a report that aid from Macedonia and Asia Minor was coming, then defeated Antigonus and took Upper Macedonia and Thessaly. He later invaded the Peloponnese at Cleonymus’s urging, defeated the Lacedæmonians, nearly captured Sparta, and entered Argos while pursuing fugitives. Pyrrhus was killed in Argos by a tile thrown during the fighting, though the Argives said that Demeter caused his death in the form of a woman. His death marked the end of the glory of Epirus.

CHAPTER XIV. body

The unit describes temples, statues, paintings, monuments, and traditions in Athens, including those connected with Demeter, Triptolemus, Athene, Theseus, Zeus, and other gods and heroes. It recounts competing Argive and Athenian traditions about Triptolemus and the teaching of agriculture, as well as stories of Theseus, the Amazons, the Persian wars, and the recovery of Theseus's bones. The Painted Chamber commemorates Athenian victories at Œnoe and Marathon and displays scenes from Greek mythology and history. The account concludes with the precinct and temple of Olympian Zeus, including monuments erected by Adrian and traditions concerning Deucalion and the flood.

CHAPTER XIX. body

The unit describes temples, statues, rivers, monuments, and paintings in Athens, including the Lyceum, the theatre of Dionysus, the temple of Æsculapius, and the Acropolis. It recounts myths involving Theseus, Dionysus, Hippolytus, Phædra, Ægeus, Odysseus, and other Greek figures. It also narrates Sulla’s capture and punishment of Athens after Aristion allied the city with Mithridates against the Romans. The unit praises Euripides, Sophocles, and Æschylus as major dramatists and records various artistic works and historical traditions preserved in Athens.

CHAPTER XXIV. body

The unit describes artworks and religious customs in Athens, including representations of Athene, Zeus, Poseidon, and mythological figures. The Parthenon contains scenes of Athene's birth and her contest with Poseidon for Attica, as well as a chryselephantine statue of Athene. It also recounts Athenian rites involving Zeus the Guardian of the city and explains the worship of Apollo as the Averter of Locusts. The text emphasizes the Athenians' exceptional zeal for religion and describes several votive offerings and statues.

CHAPTER XXV. body

The unit describes monuments and dedications in the Acropolis at Athens, including statues and paintings honoring Pericles, Xanthippus, Olympiodorus, and others, as well as sanctuaries of Athene. It recounts the decline of Greek independence after the battle of Chaeronea, the Macedonian occupation of Athens, the revolt led by Leosthenes, and Olympiodorus’s later liberation of the city. It also explains the Erechtheum, the ancient worship of Athene, and several Athenian legends involving Poseidon, Erechtheus, Theseus, and the Marathonian bull.

CHAPTER XXVIII. body

The unit describes Athenian statues, temples, courts, monuments, tombs, and sacred places, especially around the Acropolis, the Areopagus, and the Academy. It explains traditions concerning Athene, Apollo, Pan, the Venerable Ones, and various heroes, as well as the origins and functions of Athenian law-courts. It records the tombs and memorials of Athenian soldiers, generals, statesmen, philosophers, and artists, including Thrasybulus, Pericles, Plato, and Lycurgus. It also recounts historical conflicts involving the Persians, Lacedæmonians, Bœotians, Macedonians, and others.

CHAPTER XXXI. body

The unit describes the townships, mountains, monuments, temples, and traditions of Attica, including Marathon, Brauron, Rhamnus, Oropus, and Salamis. It recounts the Persian defeat at Marathon and the honours paid to the Athenian dead, Echetlæus, and Hercules. It describes Nemesis at Rhamnus, Amphiaraus’s temple and dream oracle at Oropus, and the religious sites and traditions associated with Salamis and Ajax. It also records traditions concerning islands, heroes, ancient kings, and notable tombs and bodies.

The unit describes the grove of the hero Lacius and nearby religious sites, tombs, and monuments along the road near the Cephisus. It recounts how Demeter gave Phytalus a fig tree after being received into his house, and how Theseus was purified at an altar to Milichian Zeus after killing the progeny of Phytalus. It also mentions the temple of Cyamites, whose name may refer either to the first planter of beans or to a hero. Near Athens, a temple originally dedicated to Apollo later contained statues of Demeter, Proserpine, Athene, and Apollo.

CHAPTER XXXVIII. body

The unit describes the sacred Rheti, Eleusis, its traditions concerning Demeter, Proserpine, Eumolpus, Triptolemus, and the Eleusinian Mysteries, as well as the boundary with Bœotia. It recounts the battle between the Eleusinians and Athenians, after which Eleusis became politically Athenian while retaining control of its Mysteries. It then traces the history, monuments, traditions, and rival accounts of Megara, including its relations with Pandion, Nisus, Thebes, and Alcathous. The account also discusses Theseus's exploits and several heroic tombs and sanctuaries around Eleusis and Megara.

CHAPTER XLII. body

The unit describes the sanctuaries, tombs, monuments, and traditions of Megara and its surrounding towns. Alcathous is associated with the building of Megara’s wall, the temples and tombs of his family, and the killing of his son Callipolis. Apollo is said to have helped Alcathous build the wall, leaving his harp on a stone that produces a harp-like sound when struck. Theseus appears in traditions involving Periboea, Timalcus, and the punishment of Sciron.

BOOK II.--CORINTH. body

The unit describes Corinth, its mythic origins, Roman destruction and rebuilding, major sanctuaries, monuments, harbours, and traditions. It recounts the Isthmus of Corinth, the Isthmian games, and the temples and statues associated with Poseidon, Aphrodite, Bellerophon, Artemis, Apollo, and Athene. It also traces Corinthian rulers and traditions concerning Medea, Sisyphus, the Bacchidae, and the Dorian conquest. The account concludes with Acrocorinth, its sanctuaries, fountain, and the nearby Teneatic gate and Tenea.

And as you go from Corinth along the coast in the direction of Sicyon body

The text describes a burnt temple near Corinth on the road toward Sicyon, identified by some as a temple of Apollo and by others as a temple of Olympian Zeus. It recounts the Sicyonians’ traditions about Ægialeus, the founding of Ægialea, and the succession of their early kings. It also relates that Demeter saved Plemnæus’s son Orthopolis after Plemnæus’s other children died at birth. Orthopolis’s descendants included Chrysorthe, Coronus, Corax, and Lamedon.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit recounts the mythic and historical succession of rulers in Sicyon and the origins of its name, including Epopeus's conflict with Thebes over Antiope and the later Dorian takeover. It describes Sicyon's monuments, tombs, sanctuaries, festivals, religious images, and the earthquake that greatly reduced its population and artistic treasures. It also narrates Aratus's liberation of Sicyon, the city's entry into the Achæan league, and the league's wars with Cleomenes and the Macedonians. The final chapters describe the Battle of Sellasia, the poisoning of Aratus by Philip, and the sanctuaries of Hercules, Æsculapius, Aphrodite, Apollo, and Artemis.

CHAPTER XI. body

Epopeus is associated with a former shrine of Athene and with temples and altars near Sicyon, while Adrastus is credited with building temples and altars to several deities. The account describes sanctuaries of Demeter, Artemis, Apollo, Hera, and the Eumenides around Sicyon, as well as the road from Sicyon to Titane. Titan is said to have first dwelt at Titane and to have been regarded as the Sun’s brother because of his knowledge of the seasons. At Titane, Alexanor built a temple of Æsculapius, whose precinct contains statues and sacred objects connected with Æsculapius, Hygiea, and other figures.

CHAPTER XII. body

The unit describes the traditions, sanctuaries, and history of Phliasia and Phlius, including the origins of Aras, Aræthyrea, Phlias, and the mysteries at Celeæ. It recounts the arrival of the Dorians under Rhegnidas, the resistance of Hippasus, and the later fame of Pythagoras. It then traces the roads and sacred places around Cleonæ, Nemea, Argos, Mycenæ, Tiryns, and the temple of Hera. The account includes traditions concerning Perseus, Acrisius, Zeus, Demeter, Hebe, Dysaules, and Hera.

CHAPTER XVIII. body

The passage describes landmarks on the road from Mycenæ to Argos, including the hero-chapel of Perseus, the tomb of Thyestes, Mysia, the river Inachus, and the gate of Ilithyia. It recounts how the Argives came to be divided into three kingdoms and traces the succession of rulers until Orestes united Argos and Sparta. It then describes the return of the Heraclidæ to the Peloponnese, their claim to Argos and Messenia, and the displacement of Tisamenus and other ruling families. Tisamenus and his army went to Achaia, while other descendants of Neleus went to Athens.

CHAPTER XIX. body

Temenus made Deiphontes his general and associate, married him to his daughter Hyrnetho, and was killed by his sons, after which the Argives gradually reduced and then abolished the kingly power. The unit describes Argive temples, tombs, statues, votive offerings, and local traditions concerning Danaus, Hypermnestra, Apollo, Zeus, and other figures. It recounts conflicts involving the Argives, the Lacedæmonians, Cleomenes, Perseus, and Poseidon, including the defense of Argos led by Telesilla and the flooding of the Argive land. It also gives competing accounts of Medusa and the Gorgon and discusses the origins and memorials of several Argive heroes and institutions.

CHAPTER XXIII. body

Along the Hollow Way in Argos are temples and tombs associated with Dionysus, Amphiaraus, Bato, Eriphyle, and Æsculapius. The Argives are said to have found a statue of Dionysus in a cave after being shipwrecked at Caphareus, and they carried it home to worship. The unit also describes the temple of Cretan Dionysus, where Ariadne was reportedly buried, and discusses disputed Argive claims concerning the Palladium, Deianira, and Helenus. Pausanias rejects the claims that Deianira and Helenus were buried at Argos and that the Palladium remained there.

CHAPTER XXIV. body

The unit describes Larissa and its temples, statues, monuments, games, and nearby roads from Argos to Tegea, Mantinea, Lyrceia, Orneæ, Tiryns, and Epidaurus. It recounts Argive traditions concerning the Danaids, Lynceus, Hyrnetho, and the conflicts involving Argos, Tiryns, and neighboring communities. Epidaurus is presented as the birthplace and principal center of the worship of Æsculapius, whose healing powers and parentage are explained through several traditions. The sanctuary of Æsculapius is described in detail, including its temple, statue, healing records, theatre, sacred buildings, and customs concerning births and deaths.

CHAPTER XXIX. body

The unit describes the monuments, myths, and history of Epidaurus, Ægina, and Troezen. It recounts the origins and descendants of Æacus, the power and later displacement of the Æginetans, and the worship of deities including Hecate, Aphæa, and Artemis. It also records Troezen’s legendary kings and colonists, its temples and altars, and the stories of Theseus, Pittheus, and Orestes.

CHAPTER XXXII. body

The unit describes sanctuaries, myths, customs, and landmarks in and around Trœzen, including the precincts of Hippolytus, temples of Athene, Poseidon, Aphrodite, and other gods, and places associated with Theseus. It recounts traditions concerning Hippolytus and Phædra, Theseus’s birth and marriage, Poseidon’s exchange of sacred places with Apollo, and the origins of several local rites. It also describes the islands near Trœzen, the town of Methana and its warm baths and protective ritual against the South West Wind, and the old and new towns of Hermione. The unit further discusses the exile and death of Demosthenes and argues that he did not receive money from Harpalus.

CHAPTER XXXV. body

Hermione contains temples and sacred places dedicated to Dionysus, Artemis, Poseidon, Vesta, Apollo, Fortune, Demeter, Clymenus, Ares, and Ilithyia. Its most notable sanctuary is the temple of Demeter Chthonia on Pron, where the annual Chthonia festival includes processions and the slaughter of four heifers by four old women. The account then follows roads and sanctuaries around Mases, Asine, Lerna, Temenium, Nauplia, and the Argive borderlands, recounting traditions about Hercules, Pluto, Proserpine, Dionysus, and the hydra. It also describes conflicts involving the Argives, Lacedæmonians, and the people of Asine and Thyrea.

BOOK III.--LACONIA. body

The unit recounts the legendary origins of Laconia and Sparta, the succession of its royal houses, and the expansion of Lacedæmonian power over neighboring communities. It describes the first Messenian War, the Messenian revolt and expulsion, conflicts with Argos and Tegea, and the development of Lycurgan legislation. It then follows Cleomenes, Dorieus, Leonidas, and Leonidas’ 300 through wars involving Argos, Athens, and Xerxes’ invasion at Thermopylæ. The later narrative covers Pausanias, Lysander, Thebes, and Agesipolis, including the battle of Platæa and wars in Bœotia, Argolis, and Chalcidice.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit recounts successions and disputes among the Spartan kings, including the deposition and restoration of Leonidas and the final reign of Cleomenes son of Leonidas. It describes Spartan conflicts with Argos, Messenia, Elis, Athens, and Thebes, including the Peloponnesian War and the later Corinthian War. It also follows Agesilaus’ expedition into Asia Minor against Persia, his victories over Tissaphernes, and his eventual return to Greece, where he defeated the Thebans and their allies at Coronea.

CHAPTER X. body

Agesilaus of the Lacedæmonians aided the Ætolians, later fought in Egypt, and died on his return, receiving exceptional burial honours. During the reign of his son Archidamus, the Phocians seized the temple at Delphi; Archidamus aided them but prevented their planned massacre and enslavement of the Delphians. The text then describes Sparta, also called Lacedæmon, including its market-place, temples, monuments, roads, and local traditions.

CHAPTER XIV. body

The unit describes monuments, temples, tombs, and customs in Sparta, including the tombs of Leonidas and Pausanias. It explains the Dromus, where young men practise running, and Platanistas, where they engage in ritualized fights. The Spartan youths sacrifice to Enyalius before fighting, and their contests involve wrestling, biting, tearing, and pushing opponents into the water. The passage also mentions Lycurgus, who legislated for the state and for the youths’ fights.

CHAPTER XV. body

The unit describes temples, hero-chapels, statues, tombs, and cult practices in Sparta, on the Spartan citadel, and along the road to Amyclæ. It recounts traditions concerning Hercules, Lycurgus, Artemis, Athene, Aphrodite, Apollo, Poseidon, and other gods and heroes. It gives particular attention to the Orthian Artemis cult, including its foreign image, violent origins, and ritual flogging, and to Bathycles’ elaborate throne for Amyclæan Apollo. It also catalogs the mythological scenes represented on that throne and explains several Spartan monuments and dedications.

CHAPTER XIX. body

The unit describes Amyclæ, Therapne, Sparta, Gythium, and other places in Laconia, including their temples, statues, fountains, tombs, and local traditions. It concerns Apollo and Hyacinthus at Amyclæ, Menelaus and Helen at Therapne and Gythium, and Achilles in the story of Leonymus of Croton and the island Leuce. It also records the history and customs of the Eleutherolacones, the Lacedæmonians, and the Dorians. Various myths explain the origins of place names, sanctuaries, festivals, and monuments.

CHAPTER XXIII. body

The unit describes coastal places in and around Laconia, including Cythera, Bœæ, Epidaurus Limera, Zarax, Brasiæ, Las, Tænarum, Thalamæ, Leuctra, Cardamyle, and Gerenia. It records temples, statues, springs, harbours, local customs, and traditions concerning gods and heroes such as Aphrodite, Apollo, Æsculapius, Dionysus, Achilles, Castor, Pollux, Nestor, and Machaon. It recounts the destruction of Delos by Menophanes under Mithridates and the punishments that followed, as well as legends about the origins of several towns and sacred places. The text also discusses Homeric traditions, including disputes about whether Achilles sought Helen in marriage and accounts of Achilles, Patroclus, Pyrrhus, and Machaon.

BOOK IV.--MESSENIA. body

The unit describes the origins, rulers, religious traditions, and early history of Messenia, including its settlement by Polycaon and Messene and the establishment of Andania. It traces the succession from Perieres, Aphareus, and Neleus through the Dorian takeover under Cresphontes and the restoration of Æpytus. It recounts the disputes between the Messenians and Lacedæmonians, especially the cases of Teleclus, Polychares, and Euæphnus. The conflict became open war when the Lacedæmonians captured Amphea under Alcamenes during the reign of Euphaes.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit introduces the Messenian war and explains that Aristomenes will be discussed later, while comparing the accounts of Myron of Priene and Rhianus of Bene. After the capture of Amphea, the Messenians prepare for war under Euphaes, fight the Lacedæmonians in two major battles, and withdraw to Ithome after suffering financial losses, desertions, and pestilence. A Delphic oracle requires the sacrifice of a pure virgin from the family of the Æpytidæ, leading to the death of Aristodemus’s daughter and a disputed interpretation of the oracle. Euphaes dies after the second battle, and Aristodemus is elected king as the war continues with raids and support from Arcadian allies.

CHAPTER XI. body

In the fifth year of Aristodemus’s reign, the Messenians and their Arcadian, Argive, and Sicyonian allies defeated the Lacedæmonians and Corinthians, after Aristodemus used light-armed troops to harass and surround the Spartan force. The Lacedæmonians and Messenians then consulted Delphi, whose ambiguous prophecies concerned guile, hidden things, and the tripods of Zeus of Ithome; Spartan stratagems failed, while Œbalus secretly placed clay tripods at the altar. Aristodemus later killed himself after ominous portents and the loss of hope, and the Messenians evacuated Ithome after twenty years of war, submitting to Spartan rule and tribute. Thirty-nine years later the Messenians rose again under Aristomenes, fought the battle of Deræ, and prepared to meet the Lacedæmonians and their allies at Boar’s Memorial.

CHAPTER XVI. body

Aristomenes repeatedly defeats the Lacedæmonians, raids Laconia, survives capture in Ceadas, and continues leading the Messenian resistance from Eira. The Lacedæmonians bribe Aristocrates, whose betrayal causes the Messenians and Arcadians to lose the battle at the Great Trench, after which the Messenians retreat to Eira. Eira is besieged for eleven years, while Aristomenes conducts raids and resists the blockade. Theoclus interprets an oracle as foretelling the end of Messene, and Eira ultimately falls after a herdsman reveals that its defenses have been abandoned during a storm.

CHAPTER XXI. body

Emperamus led the Spartans into Eira, whose capture ended the Messenian resistance after three days and nights of fighting. The surviving Messenians escaped under Aristomenes, while Theoclus died attacking the Lacedæmonians, and the Arcadians received the refugees at Mount Lycæus. Aristocrates betrayed Aristomenes’ plan to capture Sparta and was stoned to death. The exiled Messenians later settled in Sicily after Anaxilas helped them conquer Zancle and rename it Messene.

CHAPTER XXIV. body

Aristomenes arranged marriages for his daughters, died before carrying out plans for vengeance against the Lacedæmonians, and was honored by Damagetus and the people of Rhodes. The Messenians who had become Helots revolted, established themselves at Ithome, later received Naupactus from the Athenians, and fought the Acarnanians at Œniadæ before escaping to Naupactus. After the victory at Leuctra, Epaminondas and the Thebans recalled the exiled Messenians, recovered the Messenian Mysteries, and restored Messene and other Messenian towns. The restored Messenians later resisted the Lacedæmonians, received aid from Philip and the Macedonians, and captured Elis by stratagem.

CHAPTER XXIX. body

The unit recounts Demetrius, son of Philip, and the Macedonians' failed attempt to capture Messene, whose inhabitants repelled them with help from the citadel and the women of the town. It traces the Messenians' eventual entry into the Achæan League, their reception of the Arcadians after Cleomenes captured Megalopolis, and later conflicts involving Sparta, the Achæans, Philopœmen, and Dinocrates. It then describes Messenia, including Abia, Pharæ, Thuria, Messene, the Carnasian grove, and local temples, monuments, rivers, and traditions. The unit also records traditions about Aristomenes, Zeus at Ithome, and the shield of Aristomenes at the battle of Leuctra.

CHAPTER XXXIV. body

The unit describes the coast and inland places from Messene through Messenia, including the Pamisus, Corone, Asine, Mothone, Coryphasium, Pylos, and Cyparissiae. It records local traditions about temples, springs, founders, heroic figures, and the origins of the Dryopes and the people of Asine. It also recounts how Illyrian raiders deceived and captured many inhabitants of Mothone. The account ends by describing Pylos, its associations with Neleus and Nestor, the island of Sphacteria, and the Neda as the boundary between Messenia and Elis.

BOOK V.--ELIS. body

The unit recounts the origins, royal succession, colonization, and political history of Elis, including its connections with Ætolia, Arcadia, Achaia, and the Dorians. It describes conflicts involving Augeas, Hercules, Phyleus, Pisa, Pylos, Argives, and Corinth, as well as the traditions surrounding the Isthmian and Olympian games. Oxylus led the Dorian return to the Peloponnese and established a mixed Epean and Ætolian population in Elis. The account ends with Iphitus’s revival of the Olympian games and Elis’s later alliances and wars with the Lacedæmonians, Macedonians, and other Greek peoples.

CHAPTER V. body

Aristotimus briefly rules Elis before Chilon, Hellanicus, Lampis, and Cylon depose and kill him. The account describes Elis and Triphylia, including the flax of Elis, Lepreus, the rivers Aniger and Acidas, and the cave of the Nymphs of the Aniger. It also recounts Scillus, Xenophon’s residence there, and the punishment associated with Mount Typæum for women entering the Olympian contests. At Olympia, Pausanias describes the Alpheus and traditions concerning the origins and early history of the Olympian Games.

CHAPTER VIII. body

The unit recounts the origins, development, additions, and discontinuations of contests at Olympia, naming figures such as Clymenus, Endymion, Pelops, Hercules, Iphitus, and numerous victors. It explains changes to the Olympic programme, the scheduling of events, and the number and organization of the umpires of the games. It also describes the Altis, the temple and statue of Zeus at Olympia, their dedications and sculptures, and the artists associated with them. The temple’s decoration includes the chariot race between Pelops and Œnomaus, the fight between the Lapithæ and Centaurs, and many of the Labours of Hercules.

CHAPTER XI. body

The unit describes the gold-and-ivory statue of Olympian Zeus at Olympia, including its throne, paintings, ornaments, and the traditions surrounding its creation and preservation. It records the offerings and monuments in the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, along with the grove of Pelops, the great altar, and numerous other altars. It explains Elis's sacrificial customs, including the use of white poplar wood, the role of the Alpheus, and restrictions connected with sacrifices to Pelops. It also recounts traditions about Hercules, Pelops, the Olympic contests, and the protective or prophetic significance of various rites and objects.

CHAPTER XV. body

The unit describes the altars, buildings, rituals, and priestly offices within and around Altis at Olympia, including the Town Hall of the people of Elis and the continuing fire on its hearth. It recounts the temple of Hera, the Hera Festival, the sixteen matrons who administer it, and the maiden races held there. It also describes the statues and mythological scenes in Hera’s temple, especially the chest of Cypselus, which depicts episodes involving Pelops, Hercules, and other heroes.

CHAPTER XVIII. body

The unit describes mythological scenes and inscriptions on the chest, including figures such as Hercules, Zeus, Atlas, Medea, and the gods and heroes of the Trojan and Theban stories; its maker is conjectured to have been Eumelus the Corinthian. It then recounts votive offerings and monuments at Olympia, Altis, and the temples, including the pillar of Oenomaus, Philip’s House, and statues made from fines imposed on dishonest athletes. Pausanias details several cases of bribery, lateness, cheating, and cowardice in the Olympic games, and explains how the resulting fines funded statues of Zeus. The account also connects the chest’s military scenes with Cypselus and his ancestors, whose origins lay beyond Sicyon.

CHAPTER XXII. body

The unit describes numerous statues and votive offerings at Olympia, especially statues of Zeus erected by cities and individuals, together with their inscriptions, artists, and historical contexts. It records the statue dedicated by the Greeks who fought at Platæa, the 30-year peace between the Lacedæmonians and Athenians, and the oath-taking statue of Zeus in the Council Chamber. It also recounts the shipwreck of the Messenians’ chorus in the Sicilian Strait and the brazen effigies erected at Olympia in memory of the drowned boys, choir-master, and piper. Other offerings include representations of Hercules and the Achæan heroes, including works attributed to Onatas the son of Micon.

CHAPTER XXVI. body

The Dorian Messenians received Naupactus from the Athenians and erected a Victory monument at Olympia, claiming that it commemorated their participation with the Athenians in the action at Sphacteria. Olympia contained many offerings associated with Micythus, including works by Argive Glaucus and Dionysius, as well as offerings by Phormis and others. Phormis, a Mænalian who served Gelon and Hiero in Sicily, dedicated horses, charioteers, and other offerings at Olympia. The text also describes offerings and marvels in the Altis, including Phormis’s unusually alluring bronze horse and a trophy made from Lacedæmonian spoils.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II. body

Pausanias describes statues and other votive offerings at Olympia, especially those commemorating athletes, horsemen, trainers, rulers, and military figures. He records the victories, origins, artists, inscriptions, and unusual histories of many Olympic contestants, including Lichas, Damiscus, Dicon, Sostratus, and Lysander. He also discusses Elis's control of the games, disputes over athletic records, the Messenians' return to success after their restoration, and the shifting loyalties of the Samians and other Ionians after the Battle of Aegospotami.

CHAPTER V. body

The unit describes the statue of Polydamas by Lysippus and recounts the extraordinary strength and exploits of Polydamas, son of Nicias, who was from Scotusa. Polydamas slew a lion, seized a bull, stopped a chariot, and killed three Persian Immortals in single combat at the court of Darius. Scotusa was destroyed by Alexander the king of the Pheræans, and its surviving people later suffered further losses and abandoned the town. Polydamas eventually died when he tried to hold up the collapsing roof of a cave.

CHAPTER VI. body

The unit describes statues and athletic victories at Olympia, especially those of Euthymus, Diagoras and his family, and other Greek athletes. Euthymus defeated the boxer Theagenes in one Olympiad, later won three Olympic boxing crowns, and defeated the Hero of Temesa, after which he married the maiden offered to the Hero. Diagoras of Rhodes and his descendants achieved exceptional success in boxing and the pancratium, while Dorieus also became involved in conflicts between Rhodes, Sparta, Athens, and the great king. The text also recounts legends connected with Temesa, Pisirodus, and Glaucus of Carystus.

CHAPTER XI. body

The unit describes Theagenes of Thasos, his legendary strength, athletic victories, and later honors. After his death, a statue of Theagenes killed one of his enemies, was thrown into the sea, and was eventually recovered and restored when a famine afflicted Thasos. Delphi instructed the Thasians to restore those they had exiled, and the Pythian Priestess later told them they had forgotten Theagenes. The Thasians then sacrificed to the statue as to a god, and Theagenes received honors as a healer.

CHAPTER XII. body

The unit describes monuments at Olympia commemorating victories associated with Hiero the son of Dinomenes, Hiero the son of Hierocles, and other figures. Hiero the son of Hierocles became tyrant of Syracuse, shifted allegiance from Carthage to Rome during the war for Sicily, and was assassinated by Dinomenes. The passage also records statues and victories of Areus, the son of Acrotatus, Aratus the son of Clinias, and several athletes. It discusses inscriptions identifying victors and corrects the claim that Tritaea was an Arcadian town.

CHAPTER XIII. body

The unit describes numerous victors and monuments at Olympia, including Astylus of Croton, Hermogenes Xanthius, Polites, Leonidas of Rhodes, Milo, Clitomachus of Thebes, and Caprus of Elis. It records athletic achievements in running, wrestling, boxing, the pancratium, the pentathlum, horse-racing, and chariot-racing, together with the sculptors and dedicators of many statues. It also discusses unusual athletic feats, disputed victory records, the careers and deaths of athletes, and the political or military associations of several figures. The account repeatedly compares Olympic victories with successes at the Pythian, Nemean, and Isthmian games.

CHAPTER XVII. body

The unit describes monuments, statues, votive offerings, and treasuries in and around Altis at Olympia, including those honoring athletes, rulers, and notable figures such as Gorgias of Leontini and Anaximenes of Lampsacus. It recounts the treasuries of several Greek cities and the objects preserved in them, including the treasury of the Sicyonians. It also describes Mount Cronos, the temple of Ilithyia and Sosipolis, the Hippodamium, and the arrangements for the Olympian games. Finally, it explains the horse-race starting-place, the horse races, and the various traditions concerning Taraxippus, the terror of horses.

CHAPTER XXI. body

The unit describes landmarks, temples, rivers, tombs, and athletic facilities around Olympia, Pisa, and Elis. It recounts the destruction of Pisa by Elis after repeated disputes over control of the Olympian games, and it identifies monuments associated with Pelops, Hippodamia, Oinomaus, and her suitors. It also details the gymnasium, market-place, temples, porticoes, and religious practices of Elis. Several local traditions explain the names and histories of rivers, sanctuaries, statues, and towns.

CHAPTER XXVI. body

The unit describes Elis, where the old theatre between the marketplace and the temple of Mene is identified with the theatre and temple of Dionysus. The people of Elis honor Dionysus above all other gods and celebrate the Thyia, during which sealed empty flagons are said to become full of wine. It also discusses Cyllene, its temples, and its harbor, as well as the Seres, the Ser insect, and the production of their thread. Finally, it gives the boundary between Elis and Achaia as the river Larisus in the present day and the promontory Araxus in ancient times.
