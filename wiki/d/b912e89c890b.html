The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · 414 BC · 11 sections

Abstract written by a model

The document comprises an introductory note and Euripides’ The Trojan Women, a tragedy centered on the suffering of Troy’s conquered women after the city’s fall. The note calls the play an intense, harrowing witness to misery after conquest, linked in time, though not directly in subject, to Athens’ conquest of Melos. It presents Euripides as opposing strong established power and later leaving amid strife for Macedon, where he wrote the Bacchae and died. The play opens with Poseidon and Pallas Athena agreeing to punish the victorious Greeks for destroying Troy and violating Athena’s holy place: storms and a disastrous return await them. Amid Troy’s ruins, Hecuba mourns Priam, her children, and the city while captive women await enslavement. Talthybius announces the lots: Cassandra goes to Agamemnon, Andromache to Pyrrhus, Polyxena remains at Achilles’ tomb, and Hecuba belongs to Odysseus. Cassandra enters in prophetic frenzy, foretelling Agamemnon’s household’s destruction and Odysseus’s suffering. Andromache arrives with Astyanax, grieving Hector, Polyxena, Troy, and slavery. Talthybius reveals the Greeks’ decree that Astyanax, Hector’s son, must be thrown from the walls lest he become a threat. After soldiers take him, Andromache is sent to the ships. Menelaus then comes for Helen, intending punishment. Helen blames Aphrodite and divine enchantment; Hecuba charges her with freely choosing Paris and causing Troy’s ruin. Menelaus rejects her defense but sends her to Greece alive. Finally, Talthybius returns with Astyanax’s body. Hecuba gives him funeral rites, laying him on Hector’s shield. As the Greeks burn Troy, Hecuba and the other women lament their dead city and depart into slavery.

INTRODUCTORY NOTE body

The introductory note presents Euripides’ Troaedes as an intense, harrowing study of the misery that follows conquest, centered on the conquered women rather than on the conquerors or the dead. It connects the play’s production in the spring after 416 B.C. with Athens’ conquest of Melos, while emphasizing that the play is not a direct political allusion to Melos. The note describes the Troaedes as both a work of art and a prophecy or bearing of witness, expressing pity for mankind and opposition to the strong and established powers. It also traces Euripides’ later life through an atmosphere of strife and hatred until his departure for Macedon, where he wrote the Bacchae and died.

THE TROJAN WOMEN body

Poseidon and Pallas Athena agree to punish the Greeks for the destruction of Troy and the violation of Athena's holy place. Poseidon promises to raise storms against the Greek fleet after it sails, while Athena seeks a disastrous homecoming for the Greeks. Hecuba awakens among the ruins, mourns Troy, Priam, her children, and the other dead, and summons the captive Trojan women. The women fear the lots that will send them as slaves to Greek lands, and a herald approaches from the Greek ships with news of their fates.

[TALTHYBIUS, _followed by some Soldiers, enters from the left_. body

Talthybius tells Hecuba how the lots have assigned the Trojan women to Greek masters. Cassandra has been chosen as Agamemnon's bride, Andromache has been taken by Pyrrhus, Polyxena is kept at Achilles' tomb, and Hecuba is assigned to Odysseus. Cassandra enters with torches, prophesies the destruction of Agamemnon's house and Odysseus's future suffering, and leaves for the Greek fleet. Hecuba collapses in grief, while the chorus recalls the sack of Troy and the enslavement and loss brought by the Greek victory.

[_As the song ceases a chariot is seen approaching from the town, laden body

Andromache arrives among the Greek spoils with her son Astyanax and mourns Hector, Polyxena, Troy, and her own enslavement. Talthybius tells her that the Greeks have decreed Astyanax's death because he might grow into a threat as Hector's son, and that he will be thrown from Troy's walls. Andromache grieves over her child before soldiers take him away, while she is driven toward the ships and her new master. Hecuba and the Chorus lament Troy's destruction, attributing its ruin to war, love, divine power, and the consequences of Paris's actions.

[_As the song ceases, the King_ MENELAUS _enters, richly armed and body

Menelaus arrives at the ruined Troy to take Helen, intending that she be punished for abandoning him and causing the war. Helen argues that Aphrodite's judgment and divine enchantment made her leave Sparta with Paris, while Hecuba accuses her of choosing Paris and causing Troy's destruction. Menelaus rejects Helen's defense but spares her immediate execution, ordering soldiers to escort her to the shore and sail her to Greece on another ship. The Trojan women mourn their city, dead husbands, and children as the captives face exile across the sea.

[TALTHYBIUS, _followed by one or two Soldiers and bearing the child_ body

Talthybius brings Hecuba the dead Astyanax, explaining that Andromache asked for burial rites and that the child was hurled from Ilion's tower. Hecuba mourns Astyanax, wraps him in garments, performs funeral rites, and places him upon Hector's shield for burial. The Greeks set Troy on fire, and Talthybius orders the Trojan women to prepare to depart as captives, with Hecuba assigned to Odysseus. As Troy collapses, Hecuba and the Trojan women lament their dead, their ruined city, and the fate that sends them into slavery.
