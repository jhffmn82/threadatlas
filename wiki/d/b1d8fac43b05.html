The Good and The Bad: Exploring Privacy Issues in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Shenglai Zeng, Jiankun Zhang, Pengfei He, Yue Xing, Yiding Liu, Han Xu, Jie Ren, Shuaiqiang Wang, Dawei Yin, Yi Chang, Jiliang Tang · 2024 · 14 sections
"The Good and The Bad: Exploring Privacy Issues in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)" by Shenglai Zeng, Jiankun Zhang, Pengfei He, Yiding Liu, Yue Xing, Xu Han, Jie Ren, Chang Yi, Shuaiqiang Wang, Dawei Yin, Jiliang Tang (2024), licensed CC BY. Shown here as extracted text, with facts drawn from it. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.findings-acl.267

This paper studies privacy in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where a retriever selects documents from an external database and a large language model (LLM) uses them to answer queries. It examines two connected risks: extraction of private retrieval-database records and exposure of LLM training data. The authors use a black-box threat model and introduce composite structured prompting attacks, including targeted and untargeted extraction prompts, alongside targeted and prefix attacks for training-data leakage. After situating RAG as a system introduced by Lewis et al. (2020) that can improve relevance, reduce hallucinations, and update knowledge without retraining, the paper notes that LLMs may memorize pre-training data and that RAG adds a second sensitive source: retrieved context. For the first research question, it tests Enron Email and HealthcareMagic-101 with Llama-7b-chat, Llama-13b-chat, and GPT-3.5-turbo. The models can reproduce retrieved text and sensitive information; in a medical-dialogue study, Llama2-7b-Chat and GPT-3.5-turbo produce verbatim or highly similar retrieval records at rates near 50%. Command design strongly affects leakage, whereas retrieving more documents yields only limited additional leakage. Re-ranking offers little protection; summarization lowers untargeted leakage but may fail against targeted attacks; and distance thresholds impose a privacy–utility tradeoff. For the second question, the paper compares GPT-Neo-1.3B with no retrieval, noise or prompts, and several retrieval datasets using Enron_Mail examples. Retrieval substantially reduces extraction of personal identifying information and reconstruction of memorized training text. W3C-Email retrieval instead causes more retrieval content to be generated, supporting the view that retrieved context shifts generation away from training data while creating its own extraction risk. The paper concludes that RAG can leak private retrieval records but may mitigate training-data memorization. It focuses on inference-stage RAG, notes that other retrieval-based language-model architectures need study, identifies differential privacy as a possible protection for retrieval data, and leaves using RAG to safeguard training data as an open question.

Abstract body

The work studies privacy risks in retrieval-augmented generation and its effects on large language models. It presents empirical studies and novel attack methods showing that RAG systems can leak information from a private retrieval database. It also finds that retrieval-augmented generation can mitigate leakage of large language models’ training data. These findings provide insights for privacy protection of retrieval-augmented LLMs and for builders of large language models and RAG systems.

1 / Introduction body

This unit explains how retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines retrieval from an external database with large language model (LLM) generation, and examines privacy risks in both sources of information. It asks whether private data can be extracted from the retrieval database and whether retrieval data affects LLM memorization. The authors propose a composite structured prompting attack and report that Llama2-7b-Chat and GPT-3.5-turbo can output verbatim or highly similar retrieval records at rates near 50% in their medical dialogue study. They also find that retrieval augmentation can substantially reduce the tendency of LLMs to output memorized training data.

2 / Related Work / 2.1 / Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) .. 2.2 / Privacy Risk of Large Language Models body

Lewis et al. (2020) introduced retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which combines a dataset, retriever, and large language model to improve generation accuracy and relevance. RAG can mitigate hallucinations and supports flexible updates without retraining or fine-tuning the entire system, enabling applications such as personal chatbots and medical diagnostic assistants. Research on large language models shows that they can memorize and reveal information from pre-training corpora, with risks influenced by model size, data duplication, and prompt length. Studies also examine privacy risks in fine-tuning data and retrieval-based kNN-LM, although kNN-LM differs from RAG in architecture and mechanism.

This unit describes how Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems combine a large language model M, a retrieval dataset D, and a retriever R to answer a user query q using retrieved documents. It defines a black-box attack in which an attacker uses API queries to investigate privacy leakage on retrieval data through targeted and untargeted extraction prompts. It also evaluates LLM training-data privacy leakage by comparing training-data exposure with and without retrieval augmentation, using targeted attacks and prefix attacks.

4 / RQ1: Can we extract private data from / 4.1 / Evaluation Setup .. 4.5 / Potential Mitigation body

This unit evaluates privacy leakage from the external retrieval database in RAG using targeted and untargeted attacks. Experiments use the Enron Email dataset and HealthcareMagic-101 dataset with Llama-7b-chat, Llama-13b-chat, and GPT-3.5-turbo, finding that models can reproduce retrieved text and extract sensitive information. Ablations show that command design affects leakage, while increasing the number of retrieved documents produces only limited additional leakage. Re-ranking has little mitigation effect, summarization reduces untargeted leakage but can remain ineffective for targeted attacks, and distance thresholds create a privacy-utility tradeoff.

5 / RQ2: Can retrieval data affect the / 5.1 / Evaluation setup .. 5.4 / Discussions & Practical Implications body

This unit examines whether retrieval data affects the memorization and reproduction of training-set information by large language models in retrieval-augmented generation. Using GPT-Neo-1.3B and Enron_Mail examples, the study compares targeted and prefix attacks with no retrieval, noise or prompts, and several retrieval datasets. Retrieval data substantially reduces extraction of personal identifying information and reconstruction of memorized text, although W3C-Email retrieval data leads the model to output more retrieval data instead of training data. The authors suggest that retrieval shifts generation toward the retrieved context and may reduce privacy leakage from training data while creating risks for information extraction from retrieval data itself.

6 / Conclusions body

The paper investigates privacy risks associated with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for large language models (LLMs). It evaluates retrieval data extraction using proposed attack methods and explores defense techniques that can mitigate these risks. The paper finds that integrating retrieval data can substantially reduce LLMs’ tendency to output memorized training data, suggesting that RAG could help mitigate training data leakage. These findings provide insights into privacy concerns relevant to the use of retrieval-augmented LLMs in real-world applications.

7 / Limitations body

The unit concerns limitations in the study of retrieval augmentation, which focused primarily on inference-stage applications rather than pre-training or fine-tuning. It identifies privacy risks in commonly employed retrieval-augmented generation systems and notes that other retrieval-based language models have different components and architectures requiring further investigation. It also presents differential privacy as a potential strategy for protecting retrieval data and identifies the use of retrieval-augmented generation systems to safeguard training data as an open research question.
