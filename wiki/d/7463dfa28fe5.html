Abstract written by a model

This is the 1814 English edition of G. Booth’s translation of The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian: a surviving portion of Diodorus’s forty-book universal history, supplemented by fragments and excerpts, with Quintus Curtius and Arrianus intended to fill a gap in Book XVII. Its preface presents universal history as moral and political instruction and describes Diodorus’s thirty years of travel, research, and use of records at Rome. The surviving narrative begins with competing accounts of the world’s formation and humanity’s gradual development of language, fire, shelter, and arts. It first treats “barbarian” nations, especially Egypt: Egyptian claims to antiquity; Osiris and Isis’s civilizing rule and Osiris’s murder by Typhon; the Nile, Egyptian geography, kings, monuments, law, social orders, medicine, animal worship, burial, and alleged influence on Greek religion and institutions. Book II moves through Assyria under Ninus and Semiramis, Babylon, the fall of Sardanapalus, Chaldean learning, India, Scythia, Arabia, and fabulous islands. Book III surveys Ethiopia, harsh gold-mining labor, coastal and inland peoples, animals, Libya, Amazons, Atlantides, and rival genealogies of gods. Books IV and V shift to Greek myth, recounting Bacchus, Hercules, the Argonauts, Theseus, Thebes, Crete, Sicily, and numerous islands, as well as Britain, Celtica, Iberia, and other western peoples. Five following books are stated to be lost. Book XI resumes with Xerxes’s invasion of Greece: Leonidas’s stand at Thermopylae, naval fighting at Artemisium and Salamis, Gelon’s victory at Himera, and Greek victories at Plataea and Mycale. It follows the rise of Athenian power through Themistocles, Aristides, Cimon, Pericles, and wars involving Sparta, Persia, and Sicily. Book XII develops the Peloponnesian War, its causes, plague, shifting campaigns, and Athenian politics, then narrates the catastrophic Sicilian expedition: Alcibiades’s flight, Nicias and Demosthenes’s defeat, and harsh Syracusan treatment of prisoners. The final portions trace later Athenian-Spartan conflict, Alcibiades’s return and fall, Carthaginian conquests in Sicily, Dionysius’s rise as ruler of Syracuse, Aegos Potamos, and Athens’s surrender after twenty-seven years of war. Book XIV then covers the Thirty at Athens and its restoration by Thrasybulus, Cyrus’s failed challenge to Artaxerxes and the Greek retreat, Dionysius’s wars with Carthage, Agesilaus and the weakening of Sparta, and ends with the peace negotiated through Antalcidas, Dionysius’s capture of Rhegium, and the Gauls’ sack of Rome.

The unit introduces the 1814 English edition of The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, translated by G. Booth. It explains that Diodorus Siculus wrote a forty-book general history from the beginning of the world to his own time, of which fifteen books survive. The edition includes the surviving books, fragments collected by Photius and others, excerpts published by H. Valesius, and a supplement from Quintus Curtius and Arrianus intended to fill a gap in the seventeenth book. The translator also explains why the first five books were newly translated despite an earlier English version.

THE PREFACE. front matter

The preface praises universal histories for teaching political judgment, preserving the memory of virtuous actions, condemning wrongdoing, and benefiting all mankind. The author explains that earlier historians often treated only particular nations or periods, omitted barbarian affairs or ancient mythologies, or left their works incomplete. He presents the work as a unified history of the world, composed over thirty years through extensive travel and research, including records gathered at Rome. The work consists of forty books covering mythological antiquity through the beginning of the Gallic war, spanning 1,138 years after the Trojan war's preceding period.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK L body

Diodorus Siculus introduces differing opinions about the origin of mankind and the formation of the world. One account holds that heaven and earth were once a single mass, from which the ordered world and living creatures gradually emerged. Mankind first lived primitively, using herbs and natural fruits, but developed language, shelters, fire, and useful arts through necessity and experience. Diodorus then explains that he will recount what is reported about the ancient nations, while admitting that the first kings and the relative antiquity of nations cannot be known with certainty.

Diodorus first discusses the barbarians, especially the Egyptians, before turning to the history of the Grecians. The Egyptians are said to have originated the first humans and many living creatures because of Egypt's climate and the fertility of the Nile, and they identify the sun, moon, and five elemental qualities with various gods. The account then describes Osiris and Isis, their reign and civilizing works, the founding or disputed founding of Thebes, and the discoveries associated with agriculture, bread, wine, language, and the arts. Finally, Osiris begins a world expedition with Isis's advisers, Hercules, Apollo, Anubis, Pan, and others to teach cultivation and improve human life.

The unit presents Egyptian priests' and Egyptians' accounts of Osiris, Isis, their worship, and the antiquity of Egyptian history, contrasting them with Greek traditions. It claims that Orpheus transferred Egyptian accounts of Osiris and Bacchus into Greece, associating Dionysius with the Greek story of Semele, Cadmus, and Jupiter. The unit also discusses Egyptian plants, animals such as the crocodile and river horse, and the Nile’s annual inundation. It presents the inundation as the basis of Egypt’s fertile soil, abundant agriculture, and prosperity.

The unit examines the Nile, its headwaters, course, and annual overflowing, reviewing and criticizing explanations offered by ancient writers. Herodotus, Ephorus, Anaxagoras, Democritus, the philosophers of Memphis, and Agartharchides of Cnidus are among those whose theories are discussed. The account rejects explanations based on the Etesian winds, Ethiopian snow, Egypt’s underground waters, and an opposite inhabited zone. CHAP. XV. body

Lysander assembled and supplied the Lacedaemonian fleet, captured Lampsacus, and defeated the Athenian fleet at Aegos Potamos. Conon escaped to Evagoras, while Philocles was captured and executed at Lampsacus. The Lacedaemonians and Peloponnesians then blockaded Athens by land and sea, forcing the Athenians to surrender and accept the demolition of their fortifications, a ten-ship limit, and subjection to the Lacedaemonians. CHAP. XVI. body

Amilcar the Carthaginian sacked Agrigentum, devastated the country around Gela and Camarina, and besieged Gela. Dionysius brought an army to relieve Gela, but his forces were defeated and he withdrew, later ordering the inhabitants of Camarina to remove to Syracuse. The resulting anger led the Italians to leave his camp and the Syracusans to revolt, though Dionysius quickly returned to Syracuse and suppressed the rebellion. Amilcar then made peace with Dionysius, leaving several Sicilian cities under Carthaginian power or tribute, and the first Carthaginian war against Dionysius ended.

After the Peloponnesian War, Athens obtained peace with the Lacedaemonians on condition that it demolish its walls and govern according to its own laws. Under pressure from Lysander, the Athenians abolished their democracy and appointed the Thirty, who established an oligarchic tyranny, executed Theramenes, confiscated property, and drove many citizens into exile. The Lacedaemonians supported the Thirty and ordered other Greek cities to return Athenian fugitives. In Sicily, Dionysius fortified Syracuse, but the Syracusans revolted and besieged him until Campanian forces enabled him to defeat and disperse them.

CHAP. III. body

After the Peloponnesian War, the Lacedaemonians imposed oligarchies and tribute on many Greek cities and restored Lysander as high admiral. Dionysius strengthened his tyranny at Syracuse, while Alcibiades was killed after Pharnabazus revealed his plans to Artaxerxes. Clearchus established a brutal tyranny in Byzantium, was defeated by the Lacedaemonians at Porus, and later became a general for Cyrus. The Thebans subdued the Oropians, the Lacedaemonians invaded the Elei under Pausanias, and Dionysius planned war against Carthage while fortifying Epipolae.

CHAP. VI. body

Thrasybulus and the Athenian exiles overthrew the Thirty Tyrants, took the Piraeus, and helped restore Athens to its ancient laws, while opponents were allowed to withdraw to Eleusis. Sparta intervened through Pausanias, reconciling the exiles and citizens, while Lysander had been summoned by the new Athenian decemvirs. In Asia, Tissaphernes threatened the Greek cities, prompting Sparta to send Thimbron and later Dercyllidas against the Persian king; Pharnabazus and Tissaphernes meanwhile helped organize a Persian fleet under Conon. CHAP. VII. body

Dionysius made peace with the Rhegians and Messanians, then prepared a large-scale war against the Carthaginians by building ships, weapons, armor, artillery engines, and military facilities. He secured support from many Sicilian cities, married Doris of Locris and Aristomache of Syracuse, and persuaded the Syracusans to approve war against Carthage. Dionysius declared that the Carthaginians should restore the Greek cities they had subdued and then marched against Motya, while most Sicilian cities submitted to him. The Carthaginians, weakened by a plague, sent a night raid that destroyed most of the ships in Syracuse's harbor, while Dionysius continued the siege of Motya.

CHAP. IX, body

CHAP. XL body

Thrasybulus led an Athenian fleet to Ionia, the Chersonesus, Lesbos, and Rhodes, but later died after an attack by the Aspendians. The Carthaginians under Mago invaded Sicily with a large army, but Dionysius used Agyrus and the Agyrinenses to isolate them and then concluded peace with Carthage. Rhodes defected from Athens and accepted Lacedaemonian assistance. CHAP. XU. body

Dionysius of Syracuse led expeditions into Italy, first suffering a naval defeat near Rhegium and later defeating the Greek forces at the battle at the river Helorus before besieging Rhegium. The Lucanians defeated the Thurians after the Thurians invaded Lucania without waiting for their confederates, but Leptines rescued about a thousand survivors and helped establish peace. Dionysius besieged Caulonia, destroyed the city, and transported its inhabitants to Syracuse. CHAP. XIII. body

The Lacedaemonians, exhausted by war, made peace with Artaxerxes through Antalcidas, on terms requiring the Greek cities in Asia to submit and the other Grecians to govern themselves.
