Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · 168 sections

Abstract written by a model

This document is a continuous section of a geographical and antiquarian description, covering Achaia, Arcadia, Bœotia, and Phocis. It combines regional origins, migrations, wars, civic change, landscapes, monuments, cults, myths, and local marvels, moving town by town along roads, rivers, mountains, and coasts. It begins in Achaia with the succession of Ægialians, Ionians, and Achæans, then follows Ionian migration to Attica and Asia Minor, including Miletus, Ephesus, Samos, Chios, and Smyrna. Achaia’s later history centers on the Achæan League: its rise, conflicts with Sparta and Macedonia, alliance with Rome, internal quarrels over Sparta and exiles, and ruin through leaders including Callicrates, Menalcidas, Diæus, and Critolaus. Roman victories by Metellus and Mummius culminate in Corinth’s destruction and Greece’s subjection. The narrative then surveys Achaean places, especially Patræ, Ægium, Helice, Ægira, and Pellene, preserving their festivals, temples, disasters, heroes, and foundations. Arcadia follows, beginning with Pelasgus, Lycaon, Arcas, and their descendants. Its account ranges through Mantinea, Orchomenus, Pheneus, Stymphelus, Clitor, Psophis, Thelpusa, Megalopolis, Lycosura, Phigalia, Pallantium, and Tegea. Major themes are Arcadian claims of great antiquity; cults of Zeus Lycæus, Despœna, Demeter, Artemis, Poseidon, and Hermes; strange waters such as the Styx and Ladon; and the rise and decline of cities. Megalopolis is founded with Theban aid after Leuctra but later devastated by Cleomenes. Philopœmen emerges as a final Greek public benefactor, fighting Spartan rulers, reforming the Achæans, resisting Roman pressure, and dying after capture in Messenia. Bœotia centers on Platæa and Thebes. Platæa’s Persian-war memory, Hera’s Dædala festival, and repeated destruction are set beside Thebes’ mythical royal history and political fortunes. Epaminondas’ victory at Leuctra, foundation of Megalopolis, restoration of Messene, and death at Mantinea mark Theban greatness, followed by Thebes’ destruction by Alexander and partial restoration. The book also explores Helicon, the Muses, Hesiod, Orpheus, Orchomenus, Trophonius’s oracle, and many sanctuaries. Phocis culminates at Delphi. After Phocian wars, the Sacred War, Macedonian intervention, and resistance to the Galati, the text describes Delphi’s oracle, temples, games, Amphictyonic Council, treasures, inscriptions, and votive offerings. It ends with Polygnotus’s paintings of Troy and Hades, then a survey of Phocian and Ozolian Locrian towns, ending at Amphissa, Myonia, Œanthea, and Naupactus.

Description of Greece, Volume II / BOOK VII.--ACHAIA. / CHAPTER I. body

The country between Elis and Sicyonia was called Achaia in Pausanias's day, but was formerly known as Ægialus and inhabited by Ægialians or Ionian Ægialians. Xuthus's sons Achæus and Ion became connected with Thessaly and Ægialus, while Ion ruled the Ægialians, founded Helice, and gave them the additional name Ionians. After the Dorians expelled the Achæans from Argos and Lacedæmon, the Achæans under Tisamenus fought the Ionians, defeated them, and settled in Ægialus. The Ionians later migrated to Attica, where the Athenians and their king Melanthus welcomed them.

notes notes

Ægialus is identified as the Greek word for sea-shore. The note compares this interpretation with the names Pomerania and Glamorganshire. The Iliad, book II, line 575, is cited in connection with the discussion.

CHAPTER II. notes

Medon and Nileus, sons of Codrus, disputed the Athenian kingship, but the oracle at Delphi assigned it to Medon, so Nileus and other sons of Codrus led a colony to Asia Minor. The expedition included Ionians, Thebans, Minyæ, Phocians, Abantes, and others, who founded or occupied several coastal cities, including Miletus, Ephesus, Priene, and Myus. Nileus and his forces conquered Miletus, killing its male inhabitants except those who escaped and marrying their women and daughters. Androclus drove the Leleges and Lydians from Ephesus, while the inhabitants of Myus later abandoned their city after the Mæander formed a marsh that bred immense numbers of mosquitoes.

CHAPTER III. notes

The Colophonians regard the temple and oracle of Apollo at Claros as ancient, tracing their origins to Cretans under Rhacius and later to Manto, Mopsus, and Ionian settlers. Colophon, Lebedus, Teos, Erythræ, Clazomenæ, and Phocæa are described through traditions of Greek migration, interaction with the Carians, and later changes under rulers such as Lysimachus and Alexander. The account also explains the depopulation of Colophon and Lebedus, the settlement of Ephesus, and the origins of the Ionian communities in Asia Minor.

CHAPTER IV. notes

The unit describes Samos and Chios as Ionian cities and recounts the traditions concerning their rulers, colonists, and earlier inhabitants. Procles led Ionian colonists to Samos, but Androclus and the Ephesians later expelled the Samians, some of whom settled at Samothrace and Anæa before recovering Samos. It also discusses the temple of Hera in Samos, the artists Smilis and Dædalus, and traditions about Hera’s birth and statue. For Chios, Ion the Tragedian records stories of Poseidon and Chios, while later rulers fought the Carians and Abantes and joined the Ionians in sacrifices to the Pan-Ionic league.

CHAPTER V. notes

The unit describes Smyrna's history, including its capture from the Æolians, its admission to the Pan-Ionic league, and its relocation to the site of modern Smyrna after Alexander the son of Philip received a dream. It surveys Ionia's fertile land, major temples, baths, rivers, groves, caves, and other notable places. It recounts the traditions surrounding the temple of Hercules at Erythræ, its wooden raft, and the fisherman Phormio. It also records local traditions connected with the river Meles, Homer, and other Ionian wonders.

CHAPTER VI. notes

After the Ionians departed, the Achæans divided their land among twelve towns, including Patræ, and were ruled by several kings and powerful families. The Achæans participated in the Trojan War and later fought in some Greek wars, but they did not join the Greek forces at Thermopylæ, Salamis, or Platæa during the invasion of Greece by Xerxes and the Medes. They later allied with Patræ and the Athenians against the Lacedæmonians and took part in the war at Chæronea against Philip and the Macedonians, though they did not fight at Lamia. After the Galati crossed into Asia Minor, no Greek state clearly remained dominant because Lacedæmon, Thebes, and the Athenians had each been weakened.

CHAPTER VII. notes

The Achæans formed the Achæan League at Ægium, and the Sicyonians and other Peloponnesians later joined it as its power grew. The Lacedæmonians opposed the Achæans, with Agis capturing Pellene briefly and Cleomenes later defeating the Achæans before making and breaking peace with them and Antigonus. Antigonus governed the Macedonians as regent for Philip, the son of Demetrius, who later took power and established garrisons at Corinth, Chalcis, and Magnesia while attacking the Athenians and Ætolians. Rome intervened against Philip by sending an army under Otilius, who captured and destroyed Hestiæa and Anticyra, leading the Senate to replace him with Flaminius.

CHAPTER VIII. notes

Flaminius defeated the Macedonian garrison at Eretria, besieged Corinth, and urged the Achæans to join the Romans. The Achæans eventually joined the siege, and Corinth joined the Achæan League after being freed from the Macedonian garrison. The Achæans later supported the Romans against Philip, the Ætolians, and Antiochus, while imposing severe measures on Sparta after Nabis was overthrown. Philip suffered a decisive defeat at Cynoscephalæ and was forced by the Roman peace terms to withdraw his garrisons from the Greek towns he had seized.

CHAPTER IX. notes

Metellus and his colleagues urged the Achæans to treat the Lacedæmonians more kindly, but the Achæans refused to hear them without a decree from the Roman Senate. After Metellus reported against the Achæans, Areus and Alcibiades also denounced them before the Senate, prompting further Roman intervention. Appius and later a larger Roman delegation restored exiled Lacedæmonians, remitted certain fines, and altered the jurisdiction of capital cases while leaving Sparta in the Achæan League. The restored Lacedæmonians then encouraged Messenian and Achæan exiles to appeal to Rome, and the Senate ordered their restoration, which deeply embittered the Achæans.

CHAPTER X. notes

The text presents betrayal of one’s country and fellow citizens for gain as a recurring cause of destruction in Greece, culminating in the subjection of the Achæans to the Romans through Callicrates. Perseus the son of Philip violated his agreement with the Romans by attacking Abrupolis, king of the Sapæans, whose people were Roman allies, leading to war and the overthrow of Perseus and the Macedonian Empire. After the war, Roman Senators accused prominent Achæans of aiding Perseus, and Callicrates helped secure the dispatch of more than 1,000 Achæans to Rome for trial. The Romans imprisoned them in various towns in Etruria and released only about 300 or fewer 17 years later.

CHAPTER XI. notes

The Romans sent Gallus to Greece to arbitrate the disputes between the Lacedæmonians and the Argives, and authorized him to release towns from the Achæan League. The Athenians plundered Oropus, and the Roman Senate ordered a fine that was later reduced from 500 talents to 100, though the Athenians did not pay it. The Oropians secured an agreement involving an Athenian garrison and hostages, but an outrage by members of the garrison led them to demand compliance. Menalcidas and Callicrates persuaded the Achæans to assist the Oropians, but the Athenians plundered Oropus again and withdrew their garrison before the Achaean army arrived.

CHAPTER XII. notes

The Oropians paid Menalcidas, who withheld Callicrates's share, prompting Callicrates to prosecute him before the Achæans. Menalcidas bribed Diæus of Megalopolis with three talents to secure his acquittal, and Diæus then misrepresented the Roman Senate's instructions concerning Spartan jurisdiction. Disputes over those instructions led to war between the Achæans and the Lacedæmonians, while prominent Spartans went into exile and were condemned in their absence. The Roman Senate said it would send ambassadors to arbitrate, but their delayed arrival allowed Diæus and Menalcidas to deceive the Achæans and Lacedæmonians respectively.

CHAPTER XIII. notes

The Achæans prepared to renew their war with the Lacedæmonians under Damocritus, while Metellus and the Romans defeated Andriscus in Macedonia and ordered the Achæans to await Roman arbitrators. Damocritus defeated the Lacedæmonians near Sparta but failed to pursue them, was fined 50 talents, and fled when he could not pay. Diæus succeeded him, agreed to delay open warfare, but strengthened the Achæans by securing towns around Sparta and placing garrisons in them. Menalcidas then persuaded the Lacedæmonians to violate the truce and sack Iasus, after which he was accused and committed suicide by poison.

CHAPTER XIV. notes

Orestes and other Roman envoys came to Greece to arbitrate the disputes between the Lacedæmonians and the Achæans, conveying the Roman Senate’s view that several communities should not be forced into the Achæan League. The Achæans responded by attacking and imprisoning Lacedæmonians at Corinth, while sending ambassadors toward Rome. Critolaus then deceived the Roman commissioners, became General of the Achæans, and persuaded them to undertake war against Sparta and Rome. Pytheas and the Thebans further encouraged the conflict, although the Thebans had recently been fined for invasions and ravages.

CHAPTER XV. notes

The Romans declared the Achæans guilty of treason and ordered Mummius to lead naval and land forces against them, while Metellus hurried to defeat them first. Critolaus rejected Metellus’ proclamation, was defeated near Scarphea, and disappeared, while Arcadian survivors were later cut to pieces at Chæronea. Diæus took command of the Achæan army, freed slaves for military service, and sent Alcamenes with 4,000 men to Megara, but Alcamenes fled to Corinth when the Romans approached. Metellus occupied Thebes and Megara and then offered the Achæans peace and harmony, but Diæus’ folly frustrated his plan.

CHAPTER XVI. notes

Mummius took command of the Roman army, defeated the Achæans, and captured and burned Corinth after the Achæan defeat. Diæus fled to Megalopolis, killed his wife, and took poison, while many Achæans and Corinthians escaped or were killed. Mummius destroyed the walls and arms of cities that had fought Rome, enslaved captives, and carried off Corinthian treasures. Rome then reorganized Greece, imposed taxes and political restrictions, and later remitted fines and restored some Greek institutions.

CHAPTER XVII. notes

Greece suffered severe weakness and subjugation, later briefly receiving freedom from Nero before Vespasian restored taxation and Roman control. Achaia’s growth was halted by the folly of its rulers, and Dyme was plundered by Sulpicius before Augustus assigned it to Patræ. Dyme contained traditions and monuments associated with Athene, the Dindymene Mother, Attes, Sostratus, and the runner Œbotas. Œbotas’s imprecation was said to prevent Achæans from winning at Olympia until they honored him, after which Sostratus of Pellene won the boys’ race.

CHAPTER XVIII. notes

Olenus was an originally small Achaean town near the river Pirus that was later abandoned, and its people moved to Piræ and Euryteæ. Patræ grew from the towns of Aroe, Anthea, and Mesatis, was enlarged and renamed by Patreus, and later received inhabitants from other towns under Augustus. Patræ possessed the imported statue of Laphrian Artemis and celebrated Laphria with a procession and a large animal sacrifice. Ætolia and Calydon were depopulated under Augustus, while their statues and people were transferred elsewhere.

CHAPTER XIX. notes

The Ionians of Aroe, Anthea, and Mesatis worshipped Artemis Triclaria and once practiced an annual human sacrifice because Comætho and Melanippus had violated her temple through their love. After the capture of Ilium, Eurypylus son of Euæmon received a chest containing a statue of Dionysus, lost his mind upon seeing it, and was directed by the oracle at Delphi to dedicate the chest where a strange sacrifice was being offered. He arrived at Aroe during the sacrifice, and the people recognized the oracle’s meaning; Eurypylus recovered, the sacrifice ended, and the river was renamed Milichus. The people of Patras identify this Eurypylus as the son of Euæmon and sacrifice to him annually during the festival of Dionysus.

CHAPTER XX. notes

Æsymnetes is the god inside a chest at Patræ, whose worship is overseen by nine men and nine women chosen for their worth. During his festival, the priest takes the chest outside the temple, and the lads of the district perform rites involving crowns of corn, bathing in the Milichus, crowns of ivy, and a visit to his temple. Patræ also contains temples and statues of Artemis, Athene, Apollo, the Dindymene Mother, Zeus, Hera, Nemesis, and Aphrodite, as well as the Odeum and several tombs. The account explains the origins and rituals associated with Artemis Limnatis and describes monuments connected with Preugenes and Patreus.

CHAPTER XXI. notes

The unit describes cults, temples, statues, and oracles at Patræ, including those dedicated to Dionysus, Poseidon, Aphrodite, Demeter, Serapis, and Æsculapius. It recounts how Coresus, a priest of Dionysus at Calydon, sacrificed himself to save Callirhoe after Dionysus caused the Calydonians to become insane. It explains that Callirhoe later killed herself, and that a well near Calydon was named after her. It also describes the oracle at Dodona, the healing oracle at Patræ, and local beliefs about Poseidon and other gods.

CHAPTER XXII. notes

Pharæ is an inland town of Achaia reckoned with Patræ since the time of Augustus, with a market-place containing a statue and oracle of Hermes. The people of Pharæ venerate Hermes’ well, its sacred fish, and thirty square stones associated with different gods, and they maintain groves and altars for Castor and Pollux. Tritea is another inland town reckoned with Patræ, known for a painted tomb, temples to the Greatest Gods and Athene, and traditions about its foundation. The coast between Patræ and Ægium includes Rhium, Panormus, the wall of Athene, Erineus, and several rivers and sanctuaries.

CHAPTER XXIII. notes

The account describes the ruins of Argyra, the rivers Selemnus and Bolinæus, and traditions about their origins and powers. Selemnus was said to have been a handsome shepherd loved by the sea-nymph Argyra, until Aphrodite transformed him into a river and granted him forgetfulness of her. At Ægium, the narrator describes temples and statues of Ilithyia, Æsculapius, Athene, Hera, Dionysus, Zeus Soter, and the Argive gods. He also records a dispute with a Sidonian about whether Æsculapius represented health-giving air and whether Apollo was his father.

CHAPTER XXIV. notes

Ægium contains temples and statues honoring several gods, including Zeus Homagyrius, and remains the site of the general meeting of the Achæans. Helice, once an important city with a holy temple of Poseidon of Helice, was destroyed after the Achæans drove suppliants from the temple and killed them. An earthquake and simultaneous sea inundation overwhelmed Helice, leaving its ruins difficult to see beneath or near the sea. The text also describes signs preceding earthquakes and distinguishes several kinds by their effects.

CHAPTER XXV. notes

The unit describes the punishment said to follow outrages against suppliants, citing Dodona, the Areopagus, the Eumenides, the Lacedæmonians, and the Athenians. It recounts the destruction of Helice during the archonship of Asteus and the subsequent occupation of its territory by the people of Ægium. It then describes Cerynea, including its connection with refugees from Mycenæ and its temple of the Eumenides, followed by accounts of Bura, the Buraicus oracle of Hercules, the river Crathis, and the nearby sanctuary of Earth.

CHAPTER XXVI. notes

Ægira, formerly called Hyperesia, is described through its temples, statues, origin story, and surrounding roads and settlements. The Hyperesians used torches fastened to goats’ horns to deter an invasion by the Sicyonians, and afterward associated their city’s new name with the goats and Artemis the Huntress. Phelloe lies inland from Ægira, while Pellene is the neighboring Achaean town and Aristonautæ its seaport. The unit also discusses Donussa, a town subject to Sicyon, and competing traditions about local names and origins.

CHAPTER XXVII. notes

Pellene is a town built on the more level parts of a steep hill, with numerous temples, shrines, a gymnasium, and civic structures. Its religious sites include those dedicated to Hermes, Athene, Artemis, Dionysus, Apollo Theoxenius, Ilithyia, Poseidon, and Mysian Demeter at nearby Mysæum. Promachus of Pellene is honored for his athletic victories and military deeds, while Chæron is neglected because he helped establish tyranny in Pellene. Nearby sites include Mysæum, the healing temple of Æsculapius called Cyros, and the rivers Crius and Sythas.

BOOK VIII.--ARCADIA. / CHAPTER I. notes

Arcadia is described as the inland division of the Peloponnese, inhabited near Argolis by the people of Tegea and Mantinea and shut off entirely from the sea. The Arcadians say that Pelasgus was their first settler and that he became their king. Pelasgus introduced huts, garments made from pig hides, and guidance about which plants and fruits were safe to eat, especially acorns. During his reign, Arcadia was said to be called Pelasgia.

CHAPTER II. notes

Lycaon, son of Pelasgus, founded Lycosura on Mount Lycæus, called Zeus Lycæus, and established the festival called the Lycæa. Unlike Cecrops, who offered cakes and sacrificed nothing living, Lycaon sacrificed a baby to Zeus Lycæus and was said to become a wolf. The account presents this transformation as an old and credible Arcadian tradition, while also reporting that later wolf transformations could be reversed after ten months without eating meat. Niobe was likewise said to have become a stone and to weep on Mount Sipylus.

CHAPTER III. notes

The third generation after Pelasgus expanded Arcadia in population and cities, as the sons of Lycaon founded numerous towns. Nyctimus, Lycaon’s eldest son, succeeded to his power, while Œnotrus received money and men from him and sailed to Italy, becoming king of Œnotria. Œnotrus’s voyage was described as the first colony to depart from Greece. Lycaon’s daughter Callisto was associated with Zeus, transformed into a she-bear by Hera, and placed among the stars as the Great Bear.

CHAPTER IV. notes

After Nyctimus died, Arcas succeeded him as king, introduced agriculture and crafts, and changed the names Pelasgia and Pelasgi to Arcadia and Arcadians. Arcas had three legitimate sons by the Dryad Nymph Erato and divided the country among them, while his descendants founded cities and gave their names to regions and landmarks. The kingdom later passed to Aleus, who built the temple of Athene Alea at Tegea and had four children, including Lycurgus. Lycurgus became king as the eldest son of Aleus, killed Areithous by guile, and outlived both of his sons.

CHAPTER V. notes

The unit traces the succession of Arcadian kings from Echemus through the descendants of Cypselus and recounts their relations with the Dorians, Lacedæmonians, and Messenians. Agapenor led the Arcadians to Ilium, was carried to Cyprus after its capture, and founded Paphos, while later rulers transferred the royal seat from Tegea to Trapezus and faced conflicts with Lacedæmon. The Arcadians supported Aristodemus and the Messenians in their war against the Lacedæmonians. Aristocrates committed sacrilege against Artemis Hymnia, and his descendant Aristocrates later betrayed the Messenians at the Great Trench; this led to the deposition of all the descendants of Cypselus from the Arcadian sovereignty.

CHAPTER VI. notes

The Arcadians describe their major historical wars and alliances, including their changing relations with the Lacedæmonians and their membership in the Achæan League. They remained neutral against Philip and the Macedonians and did not fight the Galati at Thermopylæ, according to their own account. The text then describes the passes leading into Arcadia from Argolis, especially those near Mantinea, and identifies nearby water sources, sanctuaries, and rivers.

CHAPTER VII. notes

The district of Mantinea is reached by crossing Artemisium, and its plain Argum is described as unfruitful because rainwater disappears into the ground and later reappears at Dine. Ruins near the plain mark the camp of Philip, son of Amyntas, who came to Arcadia to win over the Arcadians and separate them from the other Greeks. Philip is praised for his military valour but condemned for breaking oaths, violating truces, and dishonouring good faith. His death at the age of forty-six fulfilled a Delphic oracle, and the text connects the early deaths of Alexander and Philip’s family with the end of Macedonian supremacy.

CHAPTER VIII. notes

The unit describes the traditions, founding, and history of Mantinea. Mantineus is said to have founded it, while Antinoe later led its transfer to another site under the guidance of a serpent, giving the nearby river the name Ophis. Mantinea was conquered and partly destroyed by Agesipolis after he diverted the Ophis through its unbaked-brick walls, but it was later restored by the Thebans. The Mantineans shifted alliances repeatedly, joining the Lacedæmonians, the Achaean League, or other powers at different times, and the city’s name was changed to Antigonea before Adrian restored Mantinea.

CHAPTER IX. notes

The unit describes the temples, tombs, monuments, and religious practices of the Mantineans and the city of Mantinea. It mentions temples and statues dedicated to deities including Æsculapius, Leto, Hera, Aphrodite Symmachia, Athene Alea, and Antinous. It recounts the transfer of Arcas’s remains to Mantinea and identifies tombs and monuments associated with Arcas, Gryllus the son of Xenophon, Antinoe, and Podares. It also records Mantinean participation in the seafight off Actium and honors awarded for bravery in the battle against Epaminondas and the Thebans.

CHAPTER X. notes

The unit describes notable places on the roads from Mantinea through Arcadia, especially the temple of Poseidon Hippius near the city. It recounts the temple’s ancient construction, the impious entry of Æpytus, and the tradition that seawater springs from within it. It also relates how the Mantineans and other Arcadians defeated the Lacedæmonians and Agis in battle, with Poseidon reportedly helping them. A stone trophy was erected to Poseidon for the victory, and the lineage of Leocydes is connected with an ancient stag tradition.

CHAPTER XI. notes

The unit describes places near Mantinea, including Pelagos, the tombs of the daughters of Pelias, and the supposed tomb of Areithous. It recounts the battle at Mantinea, where Epaminondas was mortally wounded and later died, with Mantinean, Spartan, Athenian, and Theban accounts differing about who wounded him. It also explains how an oracle at Delphi misled Epaminondas through the ambiguous name Pelagos, and compares this with the misleading place-names in the stories of Hannibal the Carthaginian and the Athenians.

CHAPTER XII. notes

Near Mantinea are the temple of Zeus Charmo, the plain of Alcimedon, and the boundary with Megalopolis. Alcimedon, one of the Heroes, lived in a cave on Mount Ostracina, where Hercules rescued Alcimedon's daughter Phialo and her exposed infant son. Two roads lead from Mantinea to Orchomenus, passing the alleged tomb of Penelope, the ruins of old Mantinea, and the well of Alalcomenea. Another road to Orchomenus passes Mount Anchisia and the tomb of Anchises, who was said to have died and been buried there.

CHAPTER XIII. notes

The unit describes Orchomenus, including its ancient hilltop walls and marketplace, its later settlement, local water source, temples, Artemis statue, and unidentified war tombs. It explains the distinctive lifelong separation of the priestess and priest of Hymnian Artemis and mentions the goddess’s annual festival. The surrounding landscape includes Mount Trachys, a marshy plain, roads to Caphya, Stymphelus, and Pheneus, and the tomb of Aristocrates, who violated the priestess of Artemis Hymnia. It also identifies Amilus, formerly a town, and Caryæ at the end of a ravine leading toward Pheneus.

CHAPTER XIV. notes

Pheneus lies below Caryæ and was said to have been destroyed by a deluge, with water pits and a canal associated with Hercules. The people of Pheneus attributed their founding to the autochthon Pheneus and preserved traditions about Odysseus, including his horses, a temple to Artemis Heurippe, and a statue of Poseidon Hippius. The unit also describes the tomb and hero-cult of Iphicles, the worship of Hermes, and the tomb and annual nighttime honors of Myrtilus. Pausanias questions whether the bronze statue of Poseidon could really have been an offering of Odysseus and rejects the claim that the Myrtoan Sea was named after Myrtilus.

CHAPTER XV. notes

Pheneus has a temple of Eleusinian Demeter where rites said to have been brought by Naus, great-grandson of Eumolpus, are celebrated in the manner of Eleusis. Near the temple is Petroma, two stones containing writings about the mysteries, which are read to initiates during the annual great rites. Beyond Pheneus are the ruins of a temple of Pythian Apollo, where the people still sacrifice to Apollo and Artemis and identify tombs associated with Hercules’ expedition against Elis. Mount Crathis contains the source of the river Crathis, which flows into the sea near Aegae and gave its name to a river in Italy.

CHAPTER XVI. notes

East of Pheneus lies the promontory of Geronteum, which marks the boundary between Pheneus and Stymphelus. Near Geronteum are Tricrena, with three wells sacred to Hermes, and Sepia, where Æpytus son of Elatus died from a serpent bite and was buried. The serpents at Sepia are reduced by the annual snow and severe cold. The tomb of Æpytus is a modest earthen mound with a stone coping, which Pausanias was curious to see because Homer mentioned it.

CHAPTER XVII. notes

Cyllene is described as the highest mountain in Arcadia and as the site of ruins of a temple of Cyllenian Hermes. The mountain and the god are said to have received their names from Cyllen, son of Elatus, while the statue of Cyllenian Hermes is made of juniper wood and is about eight feet high. Nearby Chelydorea marks the boundary between Pheneus and Pellene, and Nonacris lies near a cliff from which water called the Styx falls. Cyllene is also noted for its all-white blackbirds.

CHAPTER XVIII. notes

Hesiod, Linus, and Epimenides describe Styx’s parentage and relationships, while Homer presents Styx in several poetic contexts, including as a river of Hades. Near Nonacris, water identified as the Styx drips from a cliff, flows into the Crathis, and is described as deadly and corrosive to many materials. Beyond Nonacris are the mountains Aroania, where the daughters of Prœtus reportedly hid after becoming mad. Melampus brought them to Lusi and cured them through sacrifices and purifications in the temple of Artemis, after which Artemis was called Hemerasia by the people of Clitor.

CHAPTER XIX. notes

Cynætha is an Arcadian town about 40 stades from the temple of Artemis, with a market-place containing altars and a statue of the Emperor Adrian. Its most memorable feature is a temple of Dionysus, where the inhabitants celebrate the god in winter by sacrificing a bull. A nearby well called Alyssus cures people bitten by mad dogs or otherwise injured when they drink its cold water. The road west from Pheneus toward Clitor follows the canal dug by Hercules and passes through Lycuria, the boundary between Pheneus and Clitor.

CHAPTER XX. notes

The text describes the springs of the river Ladon, which are said to be formed when water from the marsh at Pheneus reappears after passing beneath the mountains. It praises the Ladon for the beauty of its stream and connects it with the story of Daphne. According to traditions in Arcadia and Elis, Leucippus, son of Œnomaus, disguised himself as a woman to pursue Daphne, who disliked men. When Daphne and the other maidens bathed in the Ladon, they discovered Leucippus's sex and killed him, while poets attribute his death to Apollo's jealousy.

CHAPTER XXI. notes

The road from the springs of Ladon to the town of Clitor is 60 stades and follows the Aroanius through a narrow path. Near Clitor, the river Clitor joins the Aroanius about 7 stades from the town. The Aroanius contains several kinds of fish, including variegated fish said to sound like thrushes. Clitor has temples to Demeter, Æsculapius, and Ilithyia, as well as other sanctuaries nearby.

CHAPTER XXII. notes

Stymphelus lies at the boundary between Pheneus and Stymphelus and, though no longer ranked as Arcadian, is said to be Arcadian by race. Its spring forms the river Stymphelus in winter, and the river disappears underground before emerging in Argolis as the Erasinus. Stymphelus is associated with man-eating birds called Stymphelides, whom Hercules was said either to have killed with arrows or frightened away with rattles. The town also has an ancient temple of Stymphelian Artemis, and a later disruption of the river was interpreted as punishment for neglecting her festival.

CHAPTER XXIII. notes

The unit describes Alea, Caphyæ, and the surrounding roads, waterways, sanctuaries, and settlements in Arcadia. It records traditions about Cepheus, Menelaus, and the origin of Caphyæ, including Menelaus’ plane-tree and well. It also explains how Artemis of Condylea became known as Apanchomene after children were stoned and a disorder afflicted the women of Caphyæ. The route continues past Nasi and Ladon through Soron to the boundary of Clitor and Psophis at Siræ.

CHAPTER XXIV. notes

The unit describes the competing traditions about the founding and naming of Psophis, including its connection with Phegia, Hercules, and Erymanthus. It recounts the Erymanthian boar hunt, the ruined temple of Aphrodite Erycina, and the burial place of Alcmæon at Psophis. Alcmæon’s exile, marriages, pursuit of the necklace of Eriphyle, and treacherous death are linked to Delphi, Achelous, and the origins of the Acarnanians. The account concludes by questioning the tradition that Aglaus of Psophis enjoyed uninterrupted happiness and reflecting on human fortune and misfortune.

CHAPTER XXV. notes

The road from Psophis to Thelpusa passes Tropæa, Aphrodisium, the boundary pillar, and several sites associated with rivers and temples. Thelpusa lies on a lofty ridge by the Ladon and contains temples of Æsculapius, the twelve gods, and Eleusinian Demeter. The text describes Demeter’s pursuit by Poseidon, her transformation into a mare, and her later worship as Erinys and Lusia at Onceum. It also recounts traditions about Arion, the divine horse, and Æsculapius’s exposure and upbringing near Thelpusa.

CHAPTER XXVI. notes

Heræa, founded by Heræus son of Lycaon, lies along the Alpheus and contains race-courses, baths, temples, and other religious buildings. Its athlete Damaretus was the first to win at Olympia in the race in heavy armour. The route from Heræa toward Elis crosses the Ladon and the Erymanthus, whose boundary status is disputed between Arcadian and Eleian accounts. Aliphera, named for Alipherus son of Lycaon, lies beyond the Alpheus and has notable temples and a bronze statue of Athene, while nearby Melæneæ is deserted and Buphagium marks the sources of the Buphagus.

CHAPTER XXVII. notes

Megalopolis was founded in Arcadia soon after the Lacedæmonians’ defeat at Leuctra, with Epaminondas the Theban encouraging the Arcadians to unite and sending 1,000 Thebans for their protection. The city resisted Lacedæmonian attacks, defeated an army led by Acrotatus, and later joined the Achæan League under the leadership of Lydiades. Cleomenes eventually captured Megalopolis in peacetime, killed many inhabitants, drove out others, and destroyed the city, while Philopœmen saved many of its men and boys and led them with the women to Messenia. The unit also identifies the boundary between Megalopolis and Heræa as the source of the river Buphagus.

CHAPTER XXVIII. notes

Gortys is described as a former town and present-day village with a temple of Æsculapius and statues by Scopas. Its river, called Lusius locally and Gortynius elsewhere, is said to be exceptionally cold and to rise at Thisoa before joining the Alpheus. Nearby Teuthis, formerly a town, is associated with the Arcadian leader Teuthis, who wounded Athene and was later punished by a wasting disease. A monument near the road from Gortys to Megalopolis commemorates those who died in the battle against Cleomenes.

CHAPTER XXIX. notes

After crossing the Alpheus, the traveler reaches Trapezus and then Bathos, where rites are held for the Great Goddesses every third year. The Arcadians say that the battle between the giants and the gods took place at Bathos, where they sacrifice to thunder, lightning, and storms. The text discusses Homer’s references to giants and argues that they were mortal rather than divine and did not have dragons’ feet. It also describes a giant corpse found in the diverted channel of the Orontes, which an oracle identified as Orontes of Indian race, before describing Basilis and Thocnia near the Alpheus.

CHAPTER XXX. notes

The river Helisson rises in a village of the same name, passes through Dipæa, Lycæatæ, and Megalopolis, divides Megalopolis, and joins the Alpheus. Megalopolis contains a market-place with precincts, temples, porticoes, public offices, statues, and a council chamber. The unit describes the precinct and temple of Lycæan Zeus, along with monuments to Polybius and other figures. Polybius is honored for his historical writing, service to Rome, and role in helping Greek cities of the Achæan League establish constitutions and laws.

CHAPTER XXXI. notes

The west end of the portico contains precincts and temples dedicated to the Great Goddesses, Demeter, Proserpine, Aphrodite, and other gods. Damophon made several statues there, including those of the Great Goddesses, Hermes, and Aphrodite. The worship of the Great Goddesses at Megalopolis is said to have been instituted by Callignotus, Mentas, Sosigenes, and Polus as an imitation of the Eleusinian Mysteries. The temple of Proserpine admits women at all times but men only once a year, while a large temple houses the rites of the Great Goddesses.

CHAPTER XXXII. notes

The unit describes the part of the city beyond the river, including a remarkable theatre with a perennial spring and the foundations of the Thersilium, a council-chamber built for 10,000 Arcadians. It records temples, statues, altars, and ruins associated with Apollo, Hermes, Aphrodite, Ares, Dionysus, Hercules, Artemis, and Asclepius. It also describes a racecourse, a hill with sanctuaries, and unusually large bones said to have belonged to one of the giants summoned by Hopladamus to aid Rhea.

CHAPTER XXXIII. notes

Megalopolis, founded with the zeal of the Arcadians and the good wishes of Greece, has lost its ancient prestige and felicity and is now mostly ruins. The deity and fortune are said to bring changes, reducing strong and prosperous places to weakness and ruin while favoring newer cities such as Alexandria and Seleucia. Examples include Mycenæ, Nineveh, Thebes, Babylon, Tiryns, Delos, and other formerly wealthy cities that have declined. Fortune’s power is also shown by the submergence of Chryse and the formation of the island Hiera, illustrating the instability of human affairs.

CHAPTER XXXIV. notes

The route from Megalopolis to Messene passes the temple of Maniæ, identified with the Eumenides, where Orestes was said to have gone mad after murdering his mother. Nearby places commemorate Orestes’s madness, healing, and cutting of his hair, while local tradition describes his pursuit by the Furies of Clytæmnestra before his trial at the Areopagus. The route then reaches the Alpheus, where the Gatheatas receives the Carnion. Hermæum marks the boundary between Messenia and Megalopolis.

CHAPTER XXXV. notes

The unit describes roads from Megalopolis to Messene, Lacedæmon, and several inland places in Arcadia. It identifies Hermæum as the boundary between Messenia and Megalopolis and records statues and temple ruins associated with Despœna, Demeter, Hermes, Hercules, Artemis, Poseidon, and Callisto. It also reports the Arcadians’ claim that Belemina originally belonged to them, though the account is judged improbable. Several former towns and settlements, including Tricoloni, Zœtia, Paroria, Thyræum, Hypsus, and Phalanthum, are described as deserted or ruined.

CHAPTER XXXVI. notes

Methydrium lies 137 stades from Tricoloni and was built by Orchomenus on a hill between the Malœtas and Mylaon rivers. Its traditions concern Rhea’s protection of Zeus from Cronos at Mount Thaumasium, where Rhea’s Cave is restricted to women sacred to the goddess. A road from Megalopolis leads to Mænalus, passing several temples, tombs, and ancient sites. Mænalus has ruins of a town, temples, athletic facilities, and a mountain believed to be sacred to Pan.

CHAPTER XXXVII. notes

The account describes the temple of Despœna near Acacesium, its porticoes, images, altars, sacred grove, and associated rites. The statues of Despœna and Demeter were made by Damophon from a single block of stone, with Artemis and Anytus standing beside them. The Arcadians worship Despœna above the other gods and perform secret rites in her Hall, where they sacrifice animals by cutting off whichever limb they happen to touch. Nearby are a temple of Pan, an altar to Ares, and temples or statues of Aphrodite, Apollo, and Athene.

CHAPTER XXXVIII. notes

Lycosura is described as the oldest town on earth and as a model for all later town building. Mount Lycæus, also called Olympus or the Sacred Hill by some Arcadians, is associated with the rearing of Zeus and contains sacred sites, rivers, games, and rituals. The Arcadians identify the local Cretea and the Nymphs Thisoa, Neda, and Hagno with Zeus’s upbringing, and they perform rites to bring rain during drought. The mountain also contains an enclosure, altar, and temples connected with Lycæan Zeus, Pan, and Parrhasian Apollo.

CHAPTER XXXIX. notes

Phigalia lies on a mostly precipitous hill and was attacked and captured by the Lacedæmonians, causing the Phigalians to flee. The exiled Phigalians went to Delphi, where the Pythian Priestess declared that they could return only with the aid of 100 picked men from Oresthasium who would die in battle. The men of Oresthasium joined the expedition, fought the Lacedæmonian garrison, and all died after driving out the Spartans, enabling the Phigalians to recover their town. The unit also describes temples and statues at Phigalia, including those of Artemis the Preserver, Hermes, and Dionysus Acratophorus.

CHAPTER XL. notes

Phigalia has a stone statue of Arrhachion the pancratiast in its market-place, and its inscription has been obliterated. Arrhachion won twice at Olympia in the two Olympiads before the 54th, and the people of Elis awarded him a posthumous victory after he died while defeating his last opponent. The text also recounts how Creugas died in a boxing match with Damoxenus after they agreed to strike once in turn. The Argives awarded Creugas the victory at Nemea and erected a statue to him in Argos because Damoxenus violated their agreement.

CHAPTER XLI. notes

The Phigalians maintain a mortuary chapel for 100 picked men from Oresthasium and annually offer them funeral sacrifices as heroes. The rivers Lymax and Neda flow near Phigalia, and the Lymax joins the Neda near hot baths and the temple of Eurynome. The Phigalians identify Eurynome with Artemis, while their Antiquarians identify her as Oceanus’s daughter. On Mount Cotilius stands the temple of Apollo the Helper at Bassae, built by Ictinus, along with a roofless temple of Aphrodite.

CHAPTER XLII. notes

The unit describes the cave on Mount Elaion sacred to Black Demeter and the traditions of the people of Phigalia about Demeter, Poseidon, and Despoena. It recounts how Demeter’s withdrawal caused the fruits of the earth to fail, and how Pan informed Zeus, who sent the Fates to persuade her to end her anger and grief. After an earlier statue of Demeter was destroyed by fire and the goddess’s rites were neglected, famine struck Phigalia, and the Pythian Priestess ordered the people to restore her honours. Onatas of Aegina later made a brazen statue of Demeter, but it had been crushed by falling stones before Pausanias’s visit.

CHAPTER XLIII. notes

Pallantium was a town in Arcadia whose name was associated with the settlement founded near the Tiber by Evander and his Arcadian followers. The elder Antonine made Pallantium a town and granted it freedom and exemption from taxation, while also defeating the Mauri, taking territory from the Brigantes, restoring Cos and Rhodes, and supporting communities through financial and civic works. He was called Pius for honoring the gods and was praised for allowing certain subjects to leave their property to their sons rather than to the Emperor. His son Antonine later fought the Germans and subdued the Sauromatae after they began a war.

CHAPTER XLIV. notes

The account of Arcadia describes roads and landmarks connecting Megalopolis, Pallantium, Tegea, and Asea. The Alpheus and Eurotas flow together for about 20 stades before disappearing and reemerging in different regions. Pallantium contains temples, statues, and the remains of an ancient citadel, while the Mound marks the boundary between the districts of Megalopolis, Tegea, and Pallantium. The route toward Tegea includes the temple of Aphneus and the well of Leuconius.

CHAPTER XLV. notes

Tegea was said to have received its name from Tegeates, son of Lycaon, and its town was founded in its later form by Aleus. The Tegeans were renowned for Ancæus's and Atalanta's exploits against the Calydonian boar, Echemus's victory over Hyllus, and their defeat and capture of Lacedæmonians. Tegea's earlier temple of Athene Alea burned down, and the later temple was built on a larger and more magnificent scale. Scopas the Parian was identified as the architect of the new temple, whose sculptures depicted the Calydonian boar hunt and the combat between Telephus and Achilles.

CHAPTER XLVI. notes

The ancient statue of Athene Alea and the tusks of the Calydonian boar were carried away by Emperor Augustus after his victory over Antony and his allies, including the Arcadians except the Mantineans. The text says Augustus followed a long-established custom of removing votive offerings and statues from conquered nations. The statue of Athene Alea was later placed in Augustus's Forum at Rome, made entirely of ivory by Endœus. One tusk of the boar was said to have been broken off, while the remaining tusk was kept as a votive offering in Cæsar's gardens in the temple of Dionysus.

CHAPTER XLVII. notes

At Tegea, the statue of Athene called Hippia by the Manthurii and Alea by other Greeks stands with statues of Æsculapius and Hygiea by Scopas. The temple of Athene contains ancient votive offerings, an altar attributed to Melampus, and representations of several divine figures, while games called Aleæa and Halotia are held nearby. Tegea also has a temple to Athene Poliatis, said to protect the city through a lock of Medusa’s hair given to Cepheus. The unit further recounts legends concerning Auge and Artemis Hegemone, whose dream-inspired intervention led Chronius to kill Aristomelidas and build a temple at Tegea.

CHAPTER XLVIII. notes

The market-place at Tegea contains temples, statues, pillars, altars, and tombs connected with Aphrodite, Ares, Zeus, Tegeates, Mæra, Ilithyia, Polybius, and Elatus. Iasius is commemorated as the winner of the horserace at Olympia when Hercules established the Olympian games, and the passage explains the prizes and the origin of the palm-crown custom. The women of Tegea defeated the Lacedæmonians during the first invasion of Charillus, and their victory led to the worship of Ares Gynæcothœnas and a separate sacrifice by the women. The passage also records traditions about Auge’s delivery of Telephus at the temple of Ilithyia and his exposure and nursing on Mount Parthenium.

CHAPTER XLIX. notes

The unit describes Philopœmen’s upbringing, character, education, and military career. After Cleomenes captured Megalopolis, Philopœmen led many of its inhabitants safely to Messene and later persuaded them to return with arms rather than accept Cleomenes’ conditions. He fought bravely at the battle of Sellasia, later improved the Achæan cavalry, and defeated the cavalry of Elis and the Ætolians at the river Larisus. The text contrasts Philopœmen’s hot temper with Epaminondas’ mildness and self-control.

CHAPTER L. notes

Philopœmen reformed the arms of the Achæan infantry and, as commander in chief, defeated the Lacedæmonians under Machanidas at the battle of Mantinea, killing Machanidas. He later gained honor among the Greeks, defended Megara against the Thebans, and forced Nabis to withdraw from Messene. Philopœmen also fought Nabis during the Roman war, setting fire to the Lacedæmonian camp at Gythium and defeating the Lacedæmonians in a night attack. Nabis later secured a temporary truce from the Romans but was assassinated by a man suborned by the Ætolians.

CHAPTER LI. notes

Philopœmen compelled the Lacedæmonians to join the Achæan League and later resisted Roman influence while reorganizing Sparta. As general of the Achæans, he exiled about 300 Lacedæmonian leaders, sold about 3000 Helots into slavery, demolished Sparta’s walls, and changed the youths’ training, though the Romans later restored their national training. After opposing concessions to Rome, Philopœmen led a small force into Messenia while ill, was captured, and was poisoned by Dinocrates. Lycortas then invaded Messenia, where the popular party joined him, those responsible for Philopœmen’s murder were killed, and Philopœmen’s remains were taken to Megalopolis.

CHAPTER LII. notes

Greece ceased to produce a stock of distinguished men after Philopœmen, whom the text calls its last public benefactor. Miltiades is identified as the first public benefactor of all Greece, while Leonidas, Themistocles, Conon, Epaminondas, Leosthenes, and Aratus are also described in relation to Greece's defense or recovery. The leaders celebrated for the Peloponnesian War are said to have nearly ruined Greece, which Conon and Epaminondas later helped recover. An inscription at Tegea praises Philopœmen for his wisdom, bravery, victories over Spartan tyrants, and defense of freedom.

CHAPTER LIII. notes

At Tegea, Apollo and Artemis are said to have punished those who neglected Leto while she was pregnant and wandering through Arcadia. Apollo Aguieus and Artemis became associated with the killing of Scephrus by his brother Limon, the famine that followed, and rites performed at Tegea. The chapter also describes Tegean traditions about the migration of Tegeates’ sons to Crete, while noting that Cretan genealogies give different parentage for Cydon, Catreus, and Gortys. It records Tegea’s temples, altars, tribal statues, festivals, and surrounding sanctuaries.

CHAPTER LIV. notes

The unit describes the boundary between the districts of the Lacedæmonians and Tegea, marked by the Alpheus, and traces the river’s unusual course through several regions before reaching the sea. It records roads from Tegea toward Thyrea and Argos, including temples, sacred groves, the supposed tomb of Orestes, and places associated with Pan, Telephus, and Dionysus. The road from Tegea to Argos crosses Mount Parthenium and reaches Hysiæ in Argolis, the boundary between Tegea and Argos. The text concludes by identifying these as divisions of the Peloponnese and listing the towns and notable things in each.

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I. notes

Bœotia is contiguous to Attica, and Platæa to Eleutheræ; the Bœotians are said to take their name from Bœotus. The Platæans joined the Athenians at the battle of Marathon, fought with them after Xerxes's landing, and later helped repel Mardonius at Platæa. During the Peloponnesian war, the Lacedæmonians besieged and captured Platæa, and the Platæans were later restored after returning from Athens. The Thebans captured Platæa a second time three years after Leuctra, destroyed the town except for its temples, and Philip later restored it after his victory at Chæronea.

CHAPTER II. notes

The unit describes the roads, ruins, tombs, monuments, and sanctuaries around Mount Cithæron, Eleutheræ, and Platæa. It discusses traditions concerning Mardonius, Actæon, Pentheus, and Œdipus, including Mardonius’s burial and Actæon’s death. Near Platæa, the Greeks maintain tombs for those who fell against the Medes, an altar and statue of Zeus Eleutherius, and the festival called Eleutheria. Within Platæa, the text describes a hero-chapel to Platæa and the temple of Hera, including statues of Rhea and Hera.

CHAPTER III. notes

Hera, displeased with Zeus, went to Eubœa, so Zeus followed Cithæron's advice and staged a false marriage to Platæa using a dressed wooden statue. Hera discovered the deception, was pleased by the trick, and reconciled with Zeus, after which the Platæans commemorated the event with the festival called Dædala. The festival involved wooden images, processions from the Asopus to Mount Cithæron, and the burning of the images and animal sacrifices on a huge altar. The great festival was celebrated by communities throughout Bœotia, while the Platæans also held a smaller festival.

CHAPTER IV. notes

The Platæans have a temple to Arean Athene built from spoils given to them by the Athenians after the Battle of Marathon, with a statue by Phidias and paintings by Polygnotus and Onatas. Platæa also contains a temple of Eleusinian Demeter, the tomb of Leitus, and the restored fountain Gargaphia, which Mardonius and the Persian cavalry had fouled. Near the route from Platæa to Thebes are the river Oeroe and the ruins of Scolus, while the Asopus remains the boundary between the districts of Platæa and Thebes.

CHAPTER V. notes

The unit recounts the early history of Thebes, from the Ectenes, Hyantes, and Aones through the arrival of Cadmus and the establishment of Cadmea. It traces the Theban royal line through Cadmus, Laius, Oedipus, Polynices, and Eteocles, including the conflicts that led to changes in the city’s rulers. It also discusses Amphion’s musical fame, Oedipus’s patricide and incest, and the later wars between Thebes and Argos.

CHAPTER VI. notes

The Thebans were defeated twice by the Athenians but later defeated them at Delium. They were first friendly with the Lacedæmonians after the Persian departure, then joined the Corinthians against them and achieved a major victory at the battle of Leuctra. The Thebans later fought for ten years in the Sacred War and suffered especially heavily in the Greek defeat at the battle of Chæronea, after which a Macedonian garrison was placed in Thebes. Following Philip's death, the Thebans expelled the garrison, despite omens warning of their coming ruin.

CHAPTER VII. notes

The Thebans were expelled from Thebes by Alexander, fled to Athens, and were restored by Cassander the son of Antipater with help from the Athenians, Messenians, and Arcadians of Megalopolis. Cassander opposed Alexander's family, but his sons later suffered violent deaths and his house was punished. The Thebans rebuilt their walls, then joined Mithridates in war against Rome, repented when Sulla invaded Bœotia, and lost half of Thebais before later recovering it through Roman favor. In the narrator's time, the lower part of Thebes was deserted except for its temples, while the citadel remained inhabited.

CHAPTER VIII. notes

The text describes the ruins of Potniæ, its grove, local rites, the temple of Dionysus Ægobolus, and traditions about a well that makes horses mad. It then identifies the supposed place where Amphiaraus was swallowed by the earth. At Thebes, seven gates remain within the old walls, each with a name and an associated explanation. The account also describes the Thebans' flight to Homole and return through the gate Homolois, and says that Capaneus was struck by lightning at Electris.

CHAPTER IX. notes

The Argives fought the Thebans in a major war that began with a battle at Ismenius, where the Thebans were initially defeated but later destroyed the Peloponnesian army except for Adrastus. The Thebans suffered heavy losses, giving rise to the expression "Cadmean victory." Later, the Epigoni, led by Thersander, marched against Thebes with forces from several regions and defeated the Thebans at Glisas, after which some fled with Laodamas and the rest were subdued by blockade. The epic poem called the Thebais concerns this later war, which was fought over the sons of Œdipus.

CHAPTER X. notes

Near the gates of Thebes are a sepulchre for those who fell fighting Alexander and the Macedonians, and a place associated with Cadmus sowing the dragon’s teeth. The hill sacred to Apollo, the god, and the river beside it are all called Ismenius, and the Temple of Ismenian Apollo contains statues and offerings, including a cedarwood statue of Apollo by Canachus. The Thebans annually choose a well-born, handsome, and strong youth as priest of Ismenian Apollo, who wears a laurel crown and may dedicate a bronze tripod. Above the temple is a spring sacred to Ares and the tomb of Caanthus, whom Apollo killed after Caanthus set fire to the grove while seeking his sister Melia.

CHAPTER XI. notes

The Thebans identify ruins near the Electris gate as the house where Amphitryon lived after fleeing Tiryns, and they show Alcmena’s bridal-bed and the monument of Hercules’ sons by Megara. They say that Hercules, in madness, tried to kill Amphitryon but was stopped when Athene caused a stone called Composer to strike him. The unit describes the temple of Hercules called Champion, its statues, and the story of Daedalus and Icarus. It also mentions the 12 Labours of Hercules carved by Praxiteles and an altar of Apollo Spodius near a gymnasium and racecourse dedicated to Hercules.

CHAPTER XII. notes

The Thebans are said to have begun sacrificing oxen used for labor to Apollo Spodius after once sacrificing an ox from a nearby waggon. They say that Cadmus was guided to the site of Thebes by a marked cow, founded his home there, and established an altar and statue of Athene Onga. The account describes ruins associated with Cadmus, Harmonia, and Semele, along with traditions concerning Dionysus Cadmus and statues at Thebes. It also commemorates Pronomus, who enabled the same flute to be used for different musical measures.

CHAPTER XIII. notes

Epaminondas led the Thebans at Leuctra after the Bœotarchs voted to risk a battle against the Lacedæmonians. The Lacedæmonians, commanded by King Cleombrotus, were defeated after Cleombrotus and many prominent Lacedæmonians fell. The Thebans and their remaining Bœotian forces lost 47 men, while the Lacedæmonians lost more than 1,000.

CHAPTER XIV. notes

After the battle at Leuctra, Epaminondas released the other Peloponnesians but kept the Lacedæmonians shut up until he heard that the Spartans were coming to relieve them. He captured Ceressus, took the Thespians there captive, and then intervened in the Peloponnese by supporting the Argives, restoring Mantinea, and establishing Megalopolis for the Arcadians. Epaminondas continued as Bœotarch beyond the lawful term, marched against Sparta, and began the colonization of Messene while the Theban allies plundered Laconia. He later routed Iphicrates and his Athenians near Lechæum, pursued them toward Athens, returned to Thebes, and was acquitted for remaining in office.

CHAPTER XV. notes

Alexander, ruler in Thessaly, treacherously imprisoned Pelopidas, prompting the Thebans to march against him. When Epaminondas took command near Pylæ, Alexander lost confidence and released Pelopidas. Epaminondas later defeated Lacedæmonians, Achæans, and Athenians, showed mercy to Bœotian deserters, and was killed by an Athenian at Mantinea. He was praised for restoring Messene, helping Megalopolis, and bringing freedom and autonomy to Greece.

CHAPTER XVI. notes

Near the statue of Epaminondas, Thebes has a temple of Ammon with a statue by Calamis and a hymn by Pindar inscribed nearby. The Thebans also have a temple of Fortune, ancient wooden statues of Aphrodite attributed to Harmonia, and a temple of Law-giving Demeter. At the gate called Prœtis, there are a theatre and the temple of Lysian Dionysus, who freed Thebans captured by Thracians. The area also contains ruins of the house of Lycus, the sepulchre of Semele, and monuments to the children of Amphion.

CHAPTER XVII. notes

The temple of Artemis Euclea contains a statue by Scopas and the burials of Antipœnus’s daughters, who sacrificed themselves so Hercules and the Thebans could defeat the Orchomenians. Near the temple are offerings and statues connected with Hercules, Apollo Boedromius, Hermes Agoræus, and Amphitryon. The sepulchre of Zethus and Amphion is associated with a fertility ritual involving earth carried from Tithorea to Antiope’s tomb. Antiope, driven mad after Dirce’s death, was cured and married by Phocus, and the two were buried together; stones near Amphion’s tomb were said to have followed his music.

CHAPTER XVIII. notes

The road from Thebes to Chalcis passes the tomb of Melanippus, three stones said to mark Tydeus’s burial, and the tombs of the children of Œdipus. The Thebans perform funeral sacrifices for several heroes and for the children of Œdipus, during which the flame and smoke are said to divide. They show a cenotaph of Tiresias and the tomb of Hector near the Well of Œdipus, claiming Hector’s remains were brought from Ilium in response to an oracle. The Well of Œdipus is said to be where Œdipus washed away the blood from his father’s murder.

CHAPTER XIX. notes

Teumessus is associated with the hiding of Europa by Zeus, the Teumessian fox, and a temple of Athene Telchinia. The area around Glisas contains tombs, mythic landmarks, and ruins of Harma and Mycalessus, while Mycalessus has a temple of Mycalessian Demeter whose statue receives fruits that remain fresh. At Aulis, near the Euripus, a temple of Artemis commemorates the substitution of a doe for Iphigenia and preserves the remains of a plane-tree mentioned by Homer. The Greeks sacrificed various victims to Artemis after favorable winds appeared, and Aulis is inhabited mainly by potters.

CHAPTER XX. notes

Delium is a place near the sea in the district of Tanagra with statues of Artemis and Leto. The people of Tanagra trace their founder, Pœmander, through a genealogy connected to Apollo and say that Tanagra was his wife, though Corinna gives her a different parentage. Tanagra was also called Graia for a long time because Tanagra lived to an advanced age, but it later regained the name Tanagra. At Tanagra, traditions describe Orion's tomb, Hermes's upbringing, Atlas's meditation, and two accounts of Triton's defeat and death.

CHAPTER XXI. notes

The speaker describes the appearance of Tritons and several unusual animals, including Ethiopian bulls, Indian camels, the elk, and the tiger. Ethiopian bulls are called rhinoceroses because of their nose horns, while the elk is a difficult-to-hunt animal found among the Celts. The speaker identifies the Indian manslayer, or martiora, as a tiger but rejects the Indian claims that it has three rows of teeth and a projectile tail-sting. The speaker also argues that animals vary by climate and land, citing examples from Greece, Libya, India, Arabia, Ethiopia, and Phrygia.

CHAPTER XXII. notes

Tanagra is described as having temples to several gods, including Hermes, and as honoring its gods by keeping houses and temples separate in pure places. Its traditions include Hermes carrying a ram to end a pestilence, Corinna’s victory over Pindar, and distinctive kinds of cocks. In Boeotia, Anthedon lies at the foot of Mount Messapium on the sea and contains temples, tombs, and a place associated with Glaucus. Glaucus was a fisherman who became a marine god after eating a certain grass and was believed to predict the future.

CHAPTER XXIII. notes

The Thebans have the gymnasium of Iolaus, a race-course, and the hero-chapel of Iolaus near the gate Prœtis, while the Hippodrome contains the tomb of Pindar. Pindar was said to have begun writing poetry after bees made honey on his lips as he slept, and he later received honors connected with Apollo and Proserpine. Acræphnium, situated on Mount Ptoum, contains a notable temple and statue of Dionysus, while the nearby temple of Ptoan Apollo once housed an infallible oracle. Beyond Mount Ptoum lies Larymna, a Boeotian coastal city with a temple and statue of Dionysus, a deep harbor, and mountains suitable for wild-boar hunting.

CHAPTER XXIV. notes

The text describes the area around Lake Cephisis, also called Copais, and the settlements along its shores. The river Cephisus rises at Lilæa in Phocis and flows into the lake. Several former towns near the lake were reportedly destroyed by a winter flood, while the lake is known for its fish, especially its eels. The account then follows the route through nearby villages and towns to the river Platanius, which flows into the sea near Halæ.

CHAPTER XXV. notes

The unit describes tombs, monuments, temples, and other sacred places in and around Thebes. Menœceus voluntarily killed himself in accordance with an oracle when Polynices and his army came from Argos, while Polynices and Eteocles are said to have killed one another in single combat, and Antigone dragged Polynices’s corpse to Eteocles’s funeral pile. It also recounts traditions about Pindar’s house, local temples, and Hercules. Most of the concluding account concerns the Cabiri, the Cabirian mysteries, their restoration by Pelarge and Isthmiades, and the violent punishments inflicted on those who profaned the temple of the Cabiri.

CHAPTER XXVI. notes

The text describes the plain of Tenerus, the temple to Hercules surnamed Hippodetes, and the mountain associated with the Sphinx near the temple of the Cabiri. It recounts differing traditions about the Sphinx, including her riddles, piracy, ancestry, and death at the hands of Œdipus. It then describes the ruins of Onchestus and the town of Thespia, including its temples, statues, and a story about a dragon slain through the sacrifice of Cleostratus. Thespia also contains statues of Dionysus, Fortune, Hygiea, Athene the Worker, and Plutus.

CHAPTER XXVII. notes

The Thespians especially honor Eros, whose worship, traditions, and statues are described, including works by Lysippus, Praxiteles, and Menodorus. The statue by Praxiteles was removed to Rome and later destroyed by fire, while Menodorus's imitation stood at Thespia. Thespia also contains temples and monuments dedicated to Aphrodite, Hercules, the Muses, and others. The account discusses differing traditions about Eros's parentage and Hercules's relations with the daughters of Thestius, as well as the possibility that the temple of Hercules was founded for an older Hercules.

CHAPTER XXVIII. notes

Helicon is described as the most fertile and tree-filled mountain in Greece, with purslane bushes that provide excellent food for goats. Its grass and roots are said not to harm humans, and its pastures make snake venom less potent. The text contrasts this with mountainous Phœnicia, where roots supposedly strengthen viper venom, and with Arabia, where vipers living near balsam trees have milder venom because they feed on the trees’ sweet-scented sap. Arabians frighten these sacred vipers away with two poles when gathering balsam sap rather than killing them.

CHAPTER XXIX. notes

Ephialtes and Otus are said to have first sacrificed to the Muses on Helicon, while Ascra was built at the foot of the mountain by Œoclus and the sons of Aloeus. The sons of Aloeus believed the Muses were three, but Pierus later established nine Muses at Thespia, and other traditions describe different genealogies for them. The passage describes the fountain Aganippe, the image of Eupheme, and rites performed for Linus before sacrifices to the Muses. Linus is said to have been a renowned musician slain by Apollo, whose lament became known in Greece and Egypt, though other traditions identify different Linuses.

CHAPTER XXX. notes

The unit describes statues at Helicon and discusses Hesiod, Homer, Orpheus, and Dionysus. It reports several traditions about Orpheus’s powers, death, Eurydice, tomb, and remains, including accounts from Dium, the Macedonians, and Libethra. It also recounts an oracle of Dionysus predicting Libethra’s destruction after Orpheus’s remains were exposed to the Sun. The unit concludes by describing the short Orphic Hymns and their use by the Lycomidae at the Mysteries.

CHAPTER XXXI. notes

At Helicon, visitors see statues, tripods, a grove dedicated to the Muses and Eros, and the spring Hippocrene. The text discusses competing traditions about Hesiod, including his works, divination, and death. It also describes the fountain of Narcissus at Donacon and competing accounts of Narcissus's love and the origin of the narcissus flower.

CHAPTER XXXII. notes

Creusis is described as a haven of the Thespians with a private statue of Dionysus, while the voyage around its coast is difficult. The coastal cities of Thisbe and Tipha are described, including their temples of Hercules and local traditions. Haliartus contains the tomb of Lysander, who died while attacking the city. Lysander is praised for his naval skill and occasional justice but condemned for executions, denying burial to captives, imposing oligarchies, and encouraging Spartan greed.

CHAPTER XXXIII. notes

Haliartus contains Lysander’s tomb, a hero-chapel to Cecrops, the temple of the Praxidicæ, and roofless temples of uncertain dedication. Near it are Mount Tilphusium and the fountain Tilphusa, where Tiresias died after drinking while being taken to Apollo at Delphi. The river Lophis is said to have sprung from the blood of Lophis, the son of a ruler who killed him on the Pythian Priestess’s instructions. At Alalcomenae, the temple of Athene was neglected after Sulla removed its old ivory statue, and the local stream was called Triton.

CHAPTER XXXIV. notes

The text describes the temple of Itonian Athene near Coronea, where the Bœotians hold their general meeting and where Iodama was turned to stone after seeing Athene’s Gorgon-adorned tunic. It recounts features of Coronea, including altars, a temple of Hera, and nearby Mount Libethrium with statues and fountains. It also describes Mount Laphystium and the traditions of Athamas, Phrixus, Helle, and Hercules. The history of Orchomenus is traced through Andreus, Athamas, Haliartus, Coronus, Phrixus, and Eteocles.

CHAPTER XXXV. notes

The Bœotians say that Eteocles was the first to sacrifice to the Graces and established their worship as three, although their names are not remembered. The Lacedæmonians instead say that Lacedæmon appointed two Graces, Cleta and Phaenna, while the Athenians honoured Auxo and Hegemone and later worshipped three Graces. Homer, Hesiod, and other poets give differing accounts of the Graces’ identities, names, and number. Earlier artists represented the Graces dressed, but artists in Pausanias’s time commonly represented them naked.

CHAPTER XXXVI. notes

After Eteocles died without male progeny, Phlegyas succeeded to the kingdom through his mother Chryse, changed the country’s name to Phlegyantis, and established the city of Phlegyas. The people of Phlegyas separated from the other Orchomenians, attracted neighboring peoples, and were destroyed after attacking Delphi, with only a few escaping to Phocis. Chryses, son of Chrysogenia and Poseidon, succeeded the childless Phlegyas, and his son Minyas became renowned for his wealth and built a Treasury. Under Minyas’s son Orchomenus, the town and its inhabitants took the name Orchomenus and Orchomenians, while Hyettus received land there after fleeing Argos.

CHAPTER XXXVII. notes

Orchomenus passed to Clymenus's line after Orchomenus died without a child, but Clymenus was killed by Thebans and succeeded by Erginus. Erginus and his brothers defeated Thebes and imposed a yearly tax, which Hercules later had remitted, causing the Minyæ to suffer reverses and make peace. In old age, Erginus fathered Trophonius and Agamedes, who became renowned builders; Agamedes was killed by Trophonius during a robbery, and Trophonius was swallowed by the earth. Ascalaphus and Ialmenus later ruled the Orchomenians and led them to Troy, while Orchomenus was eventually restored after the Orchomenians were driven out by the Thebans.

CHAPTER XXXVIII. notes

Orchomenus contains temples, sacred stones, the treasury and tomb of Minyas, the tomb of Hesiod, and monuments associated with Actaeon and Hercules. The Pythian Priestess instructed the Orchomenians to recover Hesiod’s bones from Naupactus and later to bury Actaeon’s remains and fasten a brazen image of his spectre to a stone. Hercules was credited by the Thebans with diverting the Cephisus into the Orchomenian plain and blocking its former outlet, though the text questions whether the Orchomenians could not have reversed this work. Chersias described the ancestry of Aspledon and was said to have composed Hesiod’s epitaph.

CHAPTER XXXIX. notes

Lebadea is described as a prosperous town whose grove of Trophonius contains temples, statues, caves, and the sources of the River Hercyna. The account explains the origins of Hercyna and describes Trophonius’s oracle, including the sacrifices, purification, descent into the artificial underground chamber, and methods of receiving answers. Consultors drink from the waters of Lethe and Memory, descend with honey cakes, and are questioned after returning. The narrative also records that the author personally witnessed people consult the oracle and had consulted it himself.

CHAPTER XL. notes

The Bœotians, suffering a two-year drought, were directed by the oracle at Delphi to seek Trophonius at Lebadea, where Saon from Acræphnium discovered the oracle and learned its ritual. The text lists works attributed to Dædalus in Bœotia, Crete, Delos, Argos, and Sicily, and describes the origins and history of Chæronea. Sulla and the Romans erected trophies at Chæronea for victories over Taxilus and the army of Mithridates, while Philip son of Amyntas erected no trophies. Near Chæronea stands the common sepulchre of the Thebans who fell in the battle against Philip, and the people of Chæronea chiefly venerate the sceptre of Zeus, which they call the spear.

CHAPTER XLI. notes

The text discusses works attributed to Hephæstus, especially the sceptre of Agamemnon, which is considered probably the only one of his works still extant. It compares several other objects claimed to be made by Hephæstus, including a brazen bowl at Patara and a chest at Patræ, while questioning the identity of the necklace displayed at Amathus. It relates the necklace’s association with Harmonia and Eriphyle, its dedication at Delphi, and its later seizure by Phocian tyrants. The text also describes Petrachos above Chæronea and the healing unguents made at Chæronea from flowers.

BOOK X.--PHOCIS. / CHAPTER I. notes

Phocis is described as a region near Bœotia, the Peloponnese, and the sea, whose inhabitants fought several wars against the Thessalians. The Phocians first defeated Thessalian cavalry at Hyampolis by burying earthenware pots in the ground, and later won a major battle after receiving an oracle from Delphi and preparing to sacrifice their women, children, and possessions if defeated. Tellias of Elis commanded the Phocians and later devised a successful night attack in which 500 Phocians covered themselves and their armour with plaster to resemble a divine apparition. The Phocians commemorated their victory by sending statues of Tellias, other commanders, and local heroes to Apollo at Delphi.

CHAPTER II. notes

The Phocians, led first by Philomelus, seized the temple at Delphi after being fined by the Amphictyonic Council and recruited a strong mercenary army. The Thebans declared war on them, and the conflict lasted ten years with victories on both sides until the Phocians were defeated near Neon and Philomelus died. Onomarchus then commanded the Phocians, but after Philip the son of Amyntas joined the Thebans and defeated them, Onomarchus was killed by his own soldiers. Phayllus and then Phalæcus succeeded him, but both later died or lost power amid divine retribution and the misuse of sacred money.

CHAPTER III. notes

Philip ended the Phocian or Sacred War in the tenth year after the temple was plundered, during the archonship of Theophilus at Athens and the first year of the 108th Olympiad. Many Phocian towns were destroyed, and the Phocians were deprived of participation in the temple at Delphi and in the general Greek Council, while their votes in the Amphictyonic Council were given to the Macedonians. The Athenians and Thebans later helped rebuild the Phocian towns before the fatal Greek defeat at the Battle of Chæronea. The Phocians subsequently fought against Antipater and the Macedonians at Lamia and Crannon and against the Galati and the Celtic army at Delphi.

CHAPTER IV. notes

Panopeus is a small settlement in Phocis, about 20 stades from Chæronea, whose inhabitants live in narrow dwellings but participate in the Phocian Council. Its traditions connect it with Phlegyans, Prometheus, Tityus, and the dances of the Thyiades. Nearby Daulis is a small but physically strong Phocian town associated with the myths of Tereus, Procne, and Philomela, and with a hero-chapel at Tronis.

CHAPTER V. notes

The unit describes the road from Daulis to Delphi, including the Phocian assembly building and the Cross-roads where Oedipus is said to have killed his father. It recounts traditions about the origins and succession of the oracle of Apollo, involving Earth, Poseidon, Themis, Apollo, Daphnis, Phemonoe, Olen, and others. It also gives several traditions about Delphi’s successive temples, ending with the stone temple built by the Amphictyones after the fourth temple burned down.

CHAPTER VI. notes

The unit recounts several traditions about the origins and names of Parnassus, Lycorea, Delphi, and Pytho. Parnassus is said to have founded the earliest town there, which was destroyed in Deucalion’s flood, after which survivors founded Lycorea. Other traditions connect Lycorea, the Corycian cavern, and Delphi with Apollo and various divine or heroic figures. Pytho was traditionally named for the dragon slain by Apollo, who was said to guard Earth’s oracle, and Phemonoe later delivered an oracle promising Apollo’s protection of the Delphians.

CHAPTER VII. notes

The temple at Delphi was repeatedly plundered, most extensively by the Phocian authorities and the army of the Galati, and finally by Nero, who removed 500 bronze statues dedicated to Apollo. The earliest contest at Delphi was singing a hymn in honour of Apollo, and later the Amphictyones added musical, athletic, and equestrian events. The Pythian games gradually introduced new contests, while singing to the pipes was discontinued because its music was considered gloomy. Victors in the Pythian games received a laurel crown, associated with Apollo's reported love for Daphne, daughter of Ladon.

CHAPTER VIII. notes

The unit describes the origins and changing membership of the general Council of the Greeks, later called the Amphictyonic Council, including the roles of the Phocians, Macedonians, Lacedæmonians, and Nicopolis. It recounts how Augustus altered the distribution of votes and states that the Amphictyones numbered thirty in the narrator’s time. It then describes four temples at Delphi, including the temple of Athene Pronoia, the Massaliotes’ votive offering, and the former golden shield of Crœsus. The unit also discusses the hero Phylacus, the river Plistus, and the origins and properties of the Castalia spring.

CHAPTER IX. notes

Delphi is described as a very hilly town whose large sacred precincts of Apollo contain numerous votive offerings. These include statues and dedications from the Corcyraeans, Tegeans, Lacedæmonians, and others, commemorating victories, legends, and military actions. The text recounts the Corcyraeans’ offering after sacrificing a bull to Poseidon and catching a great shoal of tunny fish. It also describes monuments connected with the battle of Ægos-potamoi and with the conflict between the Lacedæmonians and Argives beyond Thyrea.

CHAPTER X. notes

The unit describes statues at Delphi dedicated from the tenth of the spoils of Marathon, including figures connected with Athens and its tribes. It also records Argive offerings commemorating victories at Oenoe and the expedition against Thebes, as well as offerings connected with restoring the Messenians. The Tarentines are represented by bronze horses and captive women, and the history of Tarentum’s colonization by Phalanthus is recounted. Phalanthus interpreted an oracle from Delphi through the name of his wife Æthra and captured Tarentum.

CHAPTER XI. notes

The unit describes treasuries and votive offerings at Delphi, including those of the Sicyonians, Siphnii, Thebans, Athenians, Syracusans, and Potidaeans. The Cnidians dedicated statues near the Sicyonian treasury, while the people of Lipara dedicated statues after a naval victory over the Tyrrhenians. It recounts how the Siphnii built their treasury from the wealth of Siphnos's gold mines but lost the mines after ceasing their tribute to Delphi. It also describes Athenian offerings and a portico funded by war spoils from victories over Peloponnesians and other Greek allies.

CHAPTER XII. notes

The unit discusses the Herophiles, especially the first and second Herophile, their origins, prophecies, travels, and associations with Apollo. It describes the projecting stone at Delphi where the first Herophile was said to have chanted oracles, and records the second Herophile’s predictions concerning Helen and the fall of Ilium. It also recounts traditions about Herophile’s tomb in the Troad, competing claims about her birthplace, and other prophetically inspired women and men, including Demo, Sabbe, Phaennis, the Peleae, Euclus, Musaeus, Lycus, and Bacis.

CHAPTER XIII. notes

Dropion, son of Deon and king of the Pæonians, sent a brazen head of a Pæonian bison to Delphi. The text describes how Pæonian bisons were captured by driving them onto slippery hides, weakening them with hunger, and then taming them with husked pinenuts. Delphi contained numerous offerings from the Phocians, Thessalians, Macedonians, Cyrenæans, Tarentines, and other Greeks, including groups depicting conflicts involving Apollo and Hercules. After the battle of Platæa, the Greeks dedicated a gold tripod on a bronze dragon, but Phocian leaders later took away the golden part.

CHAPTER XIV. notes

The unit recounts the legend of Tennes, son of Cycnus, whose cutting of his father’s mooring ropes gave rise to the proverb of the Tenedian axe and whose island became known as Tenedos. It describes Greek dedications at Olympia and Delphi after the battles of Artemisium and Salamis, as well as Themistocles’ failed attempt to dedicate Persian spoils to Apollo. It also explains the Delphians’ bronze wolf, made after a wolf led them to sacred treasure stolen by a man and hidden on Mount Parnassus.

CHAPTER XV. notes

At Delphi, several statues and offerings commemorate victories involving Apollo, including victories over the Medes, Athenians, and Galati. Phaennis foretold the Galati's invasion of Asia and the rise of Attalus, whom she described through the image of a bull-horned helper. The Athenians dedicated a bronze palm and gilt statue of Athene for their victory at the Eurymedon, but gold was later removed from the statue. Clitodemus attributed this damage, along with other prodigies, to crows that appeared before the Athenians' expedition to Sicily.

CHAPTER XVI. notes

The Lydian kings’ votive offerings to Apollo have disappeared except for the iron base of the bowl of Alyattes, made by Glaucus of Chios. Delphi contains offerings and statues from the Lacedæmonians, Ætolians, Elyrus, Carystians, and others, including representations connected with local victories and myths. In the seafight between the Liparæans and Tyrrhenians, the Liparæans captured several groups of ships after following the Pythian Priestess’s instructions and dedicated statues of Apollo at Delphi. Echecratides of Larissa dedicated a small Apollo, which the Delphians considered the first votive offering.

CHAPTER XVII. notes

The unit describes Sardinia, its geography, peoples, settlements, and successive colonizations. Libyans, Greeks, Iberes, Thespians, Athenians, and Trojans came to the island, while the Ilians, Corsi, and Balari later occupied its mountainous regions. The Carthaginians conquered most of Sardinia and built Caralis and Sulci, but the Ilians and Corsi resisted because of the protection offered by the mountains. The account also records Sardinia’s winds, climate, animals, plants, and the deadly grass associated with Sardonic laughter.

CHAPTER XVIII. notes

The Achæans captured Phana after learning from a woman that its inhabitants depended on a single measured supply of water, which they fouled. At Delphi, the oracle had instructed them to observe how much water kept the inhabitants alive and how much the town had already drunk. The Ambraciotes dedicated a brazen ass after defeating the Molossi, whose planned night attack was disrupted by an ass pursuing a she-ass and braying. The account also mentions other votive offerings, including statues and representations dedicated after victories or fulfilled vows.

CHAPTER XIX. notes

At Delphi, votive offerings commemorate Scyllis and his daughter Hydna, who helped wreck Xerxes’ fleet, while the temple’s gables and golden arms depict gods, heroes, and victories. The people of Methymna also sent Delphi a bronze image of Phallenian Dionysus after fishermen found a foreign-looking head at sea. The text recounts the Galati invasions of Greece, including their earlier expeditions under Cambaules and Bolgius and their large campaign under Brennus and Acichorius. Brennus persuaded the Galati to invade Greece for its wealth, assembling an army of 152,000 infantry and a cavalry force supported by servants under the custom called Trimarcisia.

CHAPTER XX. notes

The Greeks, though dejected, resolve to fight the Galatians because they believe their survival and future security are at stake. The text compares the Greek forces at Thermopylæ during the Persian invasion with those assembled there against the Galatians, describing the latter contingents in detail. Greek forces destroy the bridges over the Sperchius to hinder the Galatian advance. Brennus responds by sending 10,000 troops across a marshy part of the river, forcing the Greek detachment there to withdraw to the main army.

CHAPTER XXI. notes

Brennus led the Galati across the Sperchius toward Heraclea and Thermopylæ, seeking to force a route into Greece. At Thermopylæ, the Greeks defeated the Galati in a battle where the difficult terrain limited cavalry and the Athenians attacked from triremes. The Galati retreated in confusion, suffering heavy losses, and their dead were left unburied. The Athenians distinguished themselves, especially the young Cydias, who was killed in the fighting.

CHAPTER XXII. notes

Seven days after the battle, Galati attempting to cross Mount Œta near Heraclea were defeated by a garrison commanded by Telesarchus, who was killed. Brennus sent Orestorius and Combutis with 40,000 infantry and about 800 cavalry into Ætolia, where they destroyed Callion and committed atrocities against its inhabitants. The Ætolians, joined by young men, old men, women, and some Achæans from Patræ, retaliated during the Galati retreat, and fewer than half of the 40,800 barbarians returned to Thermopylæ. Brennus then led 40,000 picked men through a pass over Mount Œta, defeated the Phocians guarding it, and forced the Greeks at Thermopylæ to disperse after the Athenians evacuated them by triremes.

CHAPTER XXIII. notes

Brennus led the Galati toward Delphi, where the Greeks, aided especially by the Phocians and Ætolians, defeated them amid earthquakes, storms, lightning, falling rocks, and a panic that caused the barbarians to kill one another. Brennus was wounded, retreated with the surviving Galati, and later committed suicide by drinking neat wine. Acichorius’s division was delayed and harried by the Ætolians, and the retreating barbarians were attacked repeatedly until none reached home from the force that crossed into Greece. The disaster occurred in Phocis during Anaxicrates’ archonship, while the remaining Celts returned to Asia Minor the following year.

CHAPTER XXIV. notes

At Delphi, the Seven Wise Men dedicated the sayings “Know thyself” and “Not too much of anything” to Apollo and inscribed them in the vestibule of the temple at Delphi. The temple also contains a statue of Homer, an oracle concerning Homer, an altar of Poseidon, statues of the Fates, an altar associated with Neoptolemus the son of Achilles, the iron Chair of Pindar, and another gold statue of Apollo. Homer is connected by different traditions with Ios and Cyprus, while Ios preserves tombs said to belong to Homer and Clymene. Outside the temple are the grave of Neoptolemus and the fountain Cassotis, whose water is said to inspire prophetic powers.

CHAPTER XXV. notes

The Lounge is a building at Delphi containing Polygnotus’s paintings, including a scene of the capture of Ilium and the Greeks’ preparations to sail home. The painting shows Menelaus preparing to depart, while Phrontis, his pilot, is remembered as having died near Sunium and Menelaus as having stayed behind for his funeral rites. It also depicts Helen, Æthra, Andromache, Polyxena, Nestor, and many other figures associated with the fall of Ilium and its aftermath. The Trojan women appear dejected, as though they are already captives.

CHAPTER XXVI. notes

Polygnotus’s painting depicts the fall of Troy, including the captives Clymene, Creusa, Aristomache, Xenodice, Deinome, Metioche, Pisis, Cleodice, and Laodice. It shows Epeus knocking down Troy’s walls, the Wooden Horse, several Greek heroes, and Ajax the son of Oileus swearing an oath concerning the violation of Cassandra. Neoptolemus is shown killing Trojan men, while Cassandra clings to the wooden statue of Athene at the altar. The painting also includes Medusa, an old shaven-headed figure or eunuch, and a frightened child.

CHAPTER XXVII. notes

The painting depicts numerous Trojan dead, including Pelis, Eioneus, Admetus, Leocritus, Coroebus, Priam, Axion, Agenor, Echeclus, Sinon, Anchialus, Eresus, and Laomedon. It presents differing accounts by Lescheos and other poets concerning who killed several figures and what happened to Hecuba and Priam. The painting also shows Antenor’s household being spared by the Greeks, with Theano, their children, servants, and baggage depicted sorrowfully. Simonides’ elegiac couplet identifies Polygnotus of Thasos as the painter of these incidents in the capture of Ilium.

CHAPTER XXVIII. notes

The left part of Polygnotus’ painting depicts Odysseus descending to Hades to consult Tiresias about returning home. It shows Acheron, Charon’s boat, Tellis, Cleobœa, a son being strangled by his father, a sacrilegious wretch receiving punishment, Eurynomus, Auge, and Iphimedea. The passage also discusses the Minyad’s account of Theseus and Pirithous, reverence for fathers, and piety to the gods. Eurynomus is described as a demon who eats the flesh of the dead, although he is not mentioned in the Odyssey, the Minyad, or The Return from Ilium.

CHAPTER XXIX. notes

The unit describes figures in Polygnotus’ picture, including Ocnus making rope while a she-ass eats it, Ariadne watching Phædra, and several women associated with myths of the underworld. Dionysus is said to have taken Ariadne from Theseus and to have invaded India and built a bridge over the Euphrates. Odysseus summons the dead while Tiresias approaches, and Theseus and Pirithous sit on an enchanted rock after their attempt to rescue Persephone. The text also discusses the friendship of Theseus and Pirithous, citing Homer and Panyasis.

CHAPTER XXX. notes

Polygnotus depicts the daughters of Pandareus, several Trojan-war figures, and other mythic characters in a painted scene. The daughters, Camiro and Clytie, are shown crowned with flowers and playing with dice, while Pandareus is identified as a Milesian associated with Tantalus in theft and perjury. Phocus and Iaseus are portrayed recalling their friendship through a signet-ring, although Peleus later contrived Phocus’ death. The painting also includes Actæon, Orpheus, Thamyris, Marsyas, and others, with details indicating their stories, attributes, or fates.

CHAPTER XXXI. notes

The painting depicts numerous mythological figures, including enemies of Odysseus, the mournful Hector, Memnon and Sarpedon, Paris and Penthesilea, and figures suffering punishments. Polygnotus portrays Ajax the son of Oileus as shipwrecked, Meleager looking toward him, and several scenes associated with death, mourning, and the underworld. The painting also includes Callisto, Nomia, Pero, Sisyphus, people parodying or appearing to mock the Eleusinian mysteries, and Tantalus enduring punishments described by Homer and Archilochus.

CHAPTER XXXII. notes

Delphi has a handsome theatre, a statue of Dionysus offered by the men of Cnidos, and a stadium made of Mount Parnassus stone that Herodes later embellished with Pentelican marble. The Corycian cavern on Mount Parnassus is sacred to Pan and the Corycian Nymphs, and is described as especially accessible and remarkable, with springs and water dripping from its roof. Tithorea contains the remains of a theatre and market-place, a grove and shrine of Athene, and the tomb of Antiope and Phocus. Near Tithorea is a highly sacred shrine of Isis, where access is granted only to those invited by Isis in dreams and where the Festival of Isis is held twice yearly.

CHAPTER XXXIII. notes

The text describes roads and settlements in Phocis, including Ledon, Lilæa, Charadra, Parapotamii, Amphiclea, Tithronium, and Drymæa. Ledon was deserted because of its weakness, while Lilæa was restored after expelling a Macedonian garrison and contained temples, a theatre, a market-place, and baths. Charadra depended on a stream for water, and the fertile land near the Cephisus was divided mainly into farms. Amphiclea was associated with traditions about a protective snake and with Dionysian prophecy and healing, while Drymæa contained an ancient temple to Law-giving Demeter.

CHAPTER XXXIV. notes

Elatea is described as a major town of Phocis that resisted Cassander and the Macedonians, was subdued by the Romans under Titus Flaminius, and later earned freedom and immunity from taxation by resisting Taxilus and the barbarians from Pontus. Its traditions connect its foundation with Elatus, son of Areas, and its monuments include a market-place, a statue of Elatus, a temple of Æsculapius, a theatre, and an ancient bronze statue of Athene. During the invasion of the Costoboci, Mnesibulus led Elatean defenders against them and was killed after slaying many barbarians. Near Elatea stands the temple of Athene Cranæa, whose priest served for five years and whose armed statue was made by the sons of Polycles.

CHAPTER XXXV. notes

The unit describes Abæ and Hyampolis in Phocis, including their origins, ruins, sanctuaries, water supplies, and civic buildings. Abæ was associated with Apollo and had a temple destroyed first by Xerxes’ army and later by the Thebans during the Phocian War. Hyampolis was founded by Hyantes who fled Thebes and especially worshipped Artemis. The text also describes the road from Chæronea to Phocis and the town of Stiris, whose inhabitants claimed Athenian origins and worshipped Demeter Stiritis.

CHAPTER XXXVI. notes

The road from Stiris to Ambrosus crosses a vine-growing plain where coccus grows and is collected for wool dyeing. Ambrosus lies beneath Mount Parnassus opposite Delphi and has double defensive walls built during the war against Philip and the Macedonians. The road onward leads to Anticyra, whose ancient name was Cyparissus and whose inhabitants were expelled twice for their political loyalties. Anticyra contains sanctuaries, gymnasiums, a conduit, and a tomb associated with the sons of Iphitus.

CHAPTER XXXVII. notes

The unit describes Bulis, its location and temples, and the route between it, Anticyra, and nearby ports. It then discusses the road from Delphi to Cirrha, including Apollo’s Hippodrome and the treeless plain. Cirrha is said to have violated Apollo’s sacred territory, leading the Amphictyones, under Clisthenes with Solon’s advice, to wage war against it. Solon diverted the river Plistus and poisoned its water with hellebore, enabling the Amphictyones to capture Cirrha and make it the seaport of Delphi.

CHAPTER XXXVIII. notes

The unit describes the Ozolian Locrians, the traditions explaining their name, and their principal towns. Amphissa is presented as their largest and most famous town, with tombs, temples, and an imported statue of Athene. Myonia and Œanthea are also described, while Naupactus receives an account of its name, history, sanctuaries, and the healing of Phalysius. Except for Amphissa, these towns were under the Achæans of Patræ by a grant from the Emperor Augustus.
