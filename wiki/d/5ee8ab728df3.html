Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · 18 sections

Abstract written by a model

This unit surveys Aeschylus and seven surviving dramas, stressing dates, lost trilogies, recurring mythic and theological narratives, and the limits imposed by missing sequels and fragments. It follows four plays in particular. The Suppliant Maidens begins with Io’s persecution by Hera, her restoration by Zeus in Egypt, and the descent of Danaus and Aegyptus from her line. Danaus’s daughters flee Egypt rather than marry Aegyptus’s sons and claim Argos as their ancestral home. King Pelasgus hesitates because protection may mean war, but the Argive people unanimously grant sanctuary; when the Egyptian herald attempts to seize the women, Pelasgus enforces the decree. The Danaids thank Argos while fearing the conflict to come. The Persians turns to Xerxes’s invasion of Hellas. Atossa’s dreams and omens precede a messenger’s report that the Persian fleet has been defeated at Salamis and the retreating army devastated. Darius’s ghost condemns Xerxes’s bridge across the Hellespont and his disregard for the gods, warning Persia not to attack Hellas again. Xerxes returns in torn clothes, and he and the elders lament the dead and the collapse of Persian power. The Seven against Thebes depicts Eteocles organizing defenses against seven Argive attackers. As a spy reports each champion and gate, Eteocles assigns six defenders; at the seventh he chooses to confront his brother Polynices himself. Despite the Chorus’s warnings and its account of the inherited curse on Laius’s and Oedipus’s house, the brothers kill one another. After Thebes survives, Eteocles is honored in burial but Polynices is forbidden burial; Antigone vows to bury Polynices, dividing the Chorus. Prometheus Bound presents Zeus’s newly established rule as ruthless. Bound by Hephaestus for giving fire and arts to mortals, Prometheus lists the knowledge he gave humanity and insists that fate will ultimately limit Zeus. Oceanus offers cautious help, while the Oceanides pity Prometheus. Io arrives in her suffering, and Prometheus predicts both her restoration and that her distant descendant will free him. Refusing Hermes’s demand for the secret marriage that will cause Zeus’s fall, Prometheus remains defiant as Zeus buries him in a storm-shattered ravine; the Chorus stays with him.

INTRODUCTION body

The unit surveys Aeschylus and his seven surviving dramas, emphasizing their dates, lost trilogies, themes, and place in Greek drama. It discusses The Suppliant Maidens, The Persians, The Seven against Thebes, and Prometheus Bound, along with the larger legendary or theological narratives to which they belonged. It describes the Danaids' flight to Argos, the Persian defeat at Salamis, the conflict between Eteocles and Polynices, and Prometheus's punishment by Zeus. The introduction also stresses that several plays survive only as fragments of broader dramatic designs whose lost sequels prevent full understanding.

THE SUPPLIANT MAIDENS / DEDICATION body

The dedication presents a gift drawn from the lost world of ancient Greece, evoking its broken art, poetry, myths, and enduring memories. The argument recounts how Io, beloved by Zeus and persecuted by Hera, became a heifer, was watched by Argus, and eventually reached Egypt, where Zeus restored her and she bore Epaphus. From Io’s descendants came Aegyptus and Danaus, whose children became involved in a forced marriage dispute. The daughters of Danaus, horrified by marriage to the sons of Aegyptus, fled with their father to Argos, where the king and citizens sheltered them.

The Suppliant Maidens body

The Chorus of the Daughters of Danaus explains that they fled Egypt to escape forced marriages to the sons of Aegyptus and have come to Argos as suppliants. They invoke Zeus, other gods, and their ancestress Io, claiming Argos as their immemorial ancestral home through Io and Epaphus. Danaus warns them that an approaching armed host may be arriving and instructs them to seek protection at the sacred altar, holding olive-shoots and speaking humbly.

Enter the KING OF ARGOS. body

Pelasgus, king of Argos, questions the Danaids and learns that they claim descent from Io, Epaphus, and Zeus. The Danaids appeal as suppliants against forced marriage to the sons of Aegyptus, threatening to hang themselves from the Argive shrines if they are surrendered. Pelasgus refuses to decide alone and sends Danaus with attendants to display the suppliant branches at the city’s shrines and seek the people’s support. He recognizes that either protecting the Danaids or yielding them may bring grievous consequences, including war between Argos and Aegyptus’ sons.

Re-enter DANAUS. body

Danaus announces that the Argive people have voted unanimously to protect his daughters, allowing them to remain free in Argos and cursing any native who assists in their seizure. The pursuing fleet of Aegyptus’ sons arrives, and the Herald of Aegyptus tries to drag the Danaids from the sanctuary, but the King of Argos intervenes and enforces the city’s decree. The King offers the Danaids protected lodging in the city, while Danaus urges them to preserve their chastity and avoid bringing shame upon their hosts. The Chorus gives thanks to the gods and Argos, but fears that the threatened marriage will lead to war and bloodshed.

THE PERSIANS body

Xerxes leads a vast Persian invasion of Hellas to avenge Darius’ defeat at Marathon, crossing the Hellespont by a bridge and sending forces by land and sea. The Persian fleet is defeated near Salamis, and the army left under Mardonius is later destroyed near Plataea, while Xerxes returns to Susa in distress. Atossa, Xerxes’ mother and Darius’ widow, fears for the empire after dreams and omens of defeat and seeks counsel from the Chorus of Persian Elders. A courier arrives at the Palace of Susa carrying news of the expedition.

Enter a MESSENGER. body

The MESSENGER reports to Atossa and the CHORUS that Xerxes’s Persian fleet was defeated at Salamis, where Greek ships destroyed or captured the advantage of the much larger Persian force. He recounts the deaths of many Persian commanders and the slaughter of the Persian troops sent to an islet near Salamis, while Xerxes flees with the surviving land force. The retreating army suffers hunger, thirst, pursuit, and the freezing of the Strymon, leaving only a remnant to return home. Atossa interprets the disaster as the work of the gods and fate, performs libations, and summons Darius, while the CHORUS mourns the ruin of Persia’s power.

The GHOST OF DARIUS rises. body

The ghost of Darius rises at the summons of Atossa and the Chorus of Persian elders and learns that Xerxes’ invasion has ended in catastrophe at sea and on land. Darius explains that Xerxes’ attempt to bridge the Helle and his disrespect toward the gods brought ruin, and he warns Persia never again to attack Hellas. Atossa leaves to prepare a royal robe and meet her returning son, while Xerxes enters in torn garments and mourns the destruction of his army. Xerxes and the Chorus then perform an extended lament for the Persian dead, including those lost at Salamis.

THE SEVEN AGAINST THEBES body

Eteocles learns from the Spy that seven Argive warriors have sworn either to destroy Cadmus’ city or die before its walls. He orders the defenders to man the gates and confronts the Chorus of Cadmean maidens for spreading panic through their cries and prayers. The Chorus fears that the siege will bring the city’s destruction, enslavement, and the violation of its women. Eteocles resolves to lead six chosen men, making seven defenders for the seven gates.

Enter on one side THE SPY; on the other ETEOCLES and the SIX body

The Spy reports to Eteocles how the seven Argive champions are assigned to the gates of Thebes and describes their threats and shield devices. Eteocles names defenders for the first six gates, answering each boast with a chosen warrior and interpreting the opposing emblems. At the seventh gate, Polynices is set against Eteocles, who decides to fight his brother himself despite the Chorus's warnings. The Chorus connects the coming bloodshed with the curses on Laius's and Oedipus's house, the prophecy of Loxias, and the inherited pollution of the family.

Enter THE SPY. body

THE SPY reports that Cadmus’ city has repelled the attack at six gates, but the seventh-gate battle ended with Eteocles and Polynices killing each other. Antigone and Ismene approach with mourners, and the Chorus laments the brothers, their shared blood, and the curse of Oedipus. A Herald announces that Eteocles will receive honorable burial, while Polynices is to be cast out unburied for the dogs. Antigone vows to bury Polynices despite the city’s decree, and part of the Chorus joins her while the rest carries Eteocles to his grave.

PROMETHEUS BOUND body

Prometheus is bound to a rocky ravine in the Scythian steppe by Hephaestus under orders enforced by Strength and Force, as punishment for giving fire and arts to mortal men. Prometheus declares that he saved mortal men from Zeus’s intended destruction and foretells that Zeus will someday need his knowledge and lose his pride and sway. The Chorus of Oceanides visits him in pity, while Oceanus comes to offer help but leaves after Prometheus warns him against provoking Zeus. The unit presents Zeus as a newly established and ruthless ruler whose tyranny punishes compassion for humanity.

[_Exit OCEANUS._] body

Prometheus describes how he transformed mortals from an ignorant, helpless state by giving them fire-related, practical, medicinal, divinatory, and material knowledge. He says that he taught mortals writing, number, memory, agriculture-related skills, animal harnessing, chariots, ships, medicine, prophecy, sacrifice, and the discovery of metals. Prometheus remains bound and expects long suffering, because Fate and the Furies govern the end of his punishment, while Zeus cannot escape the fate ordained for him. The Chorus laments Prometheus’s suffering but fears opposing Zeus and recalls the love and aid Prometheus gave to mortals.

Enter IO, horned like a cow. body

Io, driven from her home by Zeus’s desire and Hera’s anger, arrives at Prometheus’s rocky prison while tormented by a gadfly and haunted by Argus. Prometheus recounts Io’s past suffering and foretells her long wandering through Europe, Asia, and Africa. He predicts that Zeus will one day restore Io’s reason at Canopus, where she will bear Epaphus, whose descendants will return to Argos. Prometheus also declares that Zeus will eventually be overthrown and that a distant descendant of Io will free him.

[_Exit IO._] body

Prometheus refuses Hermes’s demand to reveal the marriage that he says will bring about Zeus’s fall. Hermes warns that Zeus will bury Prometheus in the rocks, later expose him to an eagle that feeds on his flesh, and grant no release until another deity takes his place in death. The Chorus chooses to remain beside Prometheus despite the warning, while Hermes condemns their decision. Zeus then sends thunder, lightning, storm, and earthquake to rend the ravine and bury Prometheus beneath the ruins.
