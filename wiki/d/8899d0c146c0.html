The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429 · 43 sections

The document is a sequence of tragic dramas centered on royal families, divine prophecy, honor, burial, deception, and the destructive consequences of pride or inherited violence. In Antigone, Antigone defies Creon’s ban on burying Polynices, invoking Heaven’s unwritten laws; Creon entombs her alive, then reverses himself too late after Tiresias’s warning. Antigone, Haemon, and Eurydicè die, and Creon recognizes his ruin. In Aias, Athena’s madness makes Aias slaughter livestock after Achilles’ armour goes to Odysseus. Ashamed, Aias kills himself; Teucer defends his right to burial against Menelaus and Agamemnon, and Odysseus persuades Agamemnon to permit it. In King Oedipus, Oedipus seeks Laius’s murderer to end Thebes’s plague, only to discover that he killed Laius and married his own mother, Jocasta. Jocasta hangs herself, Oedipus blinds himself, and Creon takes power. Electra follows Orestes’ return to avenge Agamemnon: a false report of Orestes’ death deceives Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, after which Orestes kills them. The Trachinian Maidens shows Dêanira trying to regain Heracles’ love with a robe treated by Nessus; it tortures Heracles, leading Dêanira to kill herself and Heracles to order his own pyre. In Philoctetes, Odysseus and Neoptolemus seek the abandoned Philoctetes and Heracles’ bow for Troy. Neoptolemus abandons deceit, returns the bow, and Heracles commands Philoctetes to sail to Troy, promising healing and glory. Oedipus at Colonos concludes Oedipus’s story: blind and exiled, he receives protection from Theseus at Colonos, rejects Creon and his sons, curses Eteocles and Polynices to kill each other, and dies mysteriously after entrusting Athens’ future protection to Theseus.

4. Of the three Theban plays, the Antigone was first composed, front matter

ANTIGONE body

Antigone tells Ismene that Creon has ordered Eteocles buried with full honors but has forbidden burial and mourning for Polynices. Antigone declares that she will bury Polynices despite the order, while Ismene refuses to join her because they are women subject to authority and lack the strength to oppose the citizens. Creon announces his policy of honoring those loyal to Thebes and punishing disobedience, and a watchman reports that Polynices’ corpse has been covered with dust and given burial rites. Creon rejects the suggestion of divine intervention and orders the watchmen to find the person responsible.

CHORUS. body

Antigone is caught burying the dead body of Eteocles despite Creon's edict and openly defends her action by appealing to the unwritten laws of Heaven. Creon sentences Antigone to be shut alive in a cave-like tomb, rejecting appeals from Antigone, Ismene, the Chorus, and Haemon. Ismene tries to share Antigone's guilt, but Antigone refuses to let her claim a deed she did not perform. Haemon argues that Thebes disapproves of Antigone's punishment, but he leaves in anger when Creon refuses to change his decision.

CHORUS body

Antigone is led away alive to a sealed tomb for burying Polynices, while lamenting that she will die unmarried and unmourned. Creon orders her removal and defends his refusal to bury Polynices, but Tiresias warns that the gods reject Theban sacrifices because the city is polluted by Polynices' unburied corpse and by Antigone's entombment. After Tiresias prophesies that Creon's own household will pay for these acts, the Chorus urges Creon to release Antigone and bury Polynices. Creon finally resolves to do both himself, though he fears that fate cannot be resisted.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus invokes Bacchus to heal Thebè’s suffering, after which the Messenger reports that Haemon killed himself beside the dead maiden in the burial vault. Eurydicè hears the news, leaves without speaking, and later kills herself after cursing Creon for the deaths of her children. Creon enters carrying Haemon’s corpse, recognizes that his decree and folly caused the deaths, and declares himself ruined. The Chorus concludes that wise conduct and piety to Heaven are the foundations of happiness, while pride brings punishment.

AIAS body

Athena reveals to Odysseus that Aias attacked the Greek army's tents but was driven by divine madness to slaughter livestock instead. Aias, believing the animals were the Greek generals and Odysseus, brings them into his tent and tortures them before recovering his reason and seeing the ruin he has caused. Tecmessa tells the Salaminian Mariners that Aias is now overwhelmed by grief and may harm himself. The unit also explains that Aias' dishonour began when Achilles' armour was awarded to Odysseus despite Aias' greater valour.

Of Aias, and his fortunes as they are. [AIAS _is discovered_ body

Aias, disgraced after Athena-driven madness led him to slaughter flocks, laments the loss of his honor and contemplates death. He briefly appears to yield to authority and tells Tecmessa and his sailors to protect his son Eurysakes, but secretly takes his sword and goes into the countryside. A messenger reports that Calchas has warned Teucer that Aias must be kept indoors, because Athena will destroy him during the present day if he roams abroad. Tecmessa and the sailors realize the danger and set out to find Aias, while Teucer is expected to come quickly.

_The scene changes to a lonely wooded spot._ body

Aias kills himself in a lonely wooded spot with the sword Hector gave him, after asking the gods to protect his body and send word of his death to his family. Tecmessa discovers Aias's corpse, and Teucer arrives, mourns him, summons Eurysakes, and prepares to defend his brother's burial. Menelaus orders that Aias remain unburied, claiming that Aias attacked the Achaean army, but Teucer rejects his authority and insists on burying him. The Chorus laments Aias's death, the sufferings of the Trojan War, and the loss of the protection and companionship he provided.

_Enter_ TEUCER. body

Teucer confronts Agamemnon, defending Aias and insisting that Aias receive burial despite the kings’ opposition. Agamemnon argues that Teucer is a slave’s son and threatens him, but Teucer answers by recalling Aias’s services and attacking Agamemnon’s ancestry. Odysseus persuades Agamemnon not to leave Aias unburied, though Agamemnon continues to hate Aias. Teucer declines Odysseus’s direct assistance, then begins the burial rites with the other comrades.

KING OEDIPUS body

Oedipus, king of Thebes, responds to a plague by seeking Apollo's guidance through Creon. Apollo's oracle commands that Laius's murderer be discovered and punished by exile or blood purgation, and Oedipus vows to pursue the killer. The Priest of Zeus and the Train of Suppliants appeal to Oedipus for the city's salvation, while the Theban Elders pray to the gods to end the pestilence. The opening account also states that Oedipus unknowingly killed Laius, married Jocasta, and fulfilled the prophecy of parricide and incest.

[OEDIPUS _has entered during the Choral song_ body

Oedipus publicly orders the discovery and punishment of Laius's murderer, declaring a curse on anyone who conceals the crime. The Chorus recommends Tiresias, who initially refuses to speak but then identifies Oedipus as the murderer and foretells his incestuous relations, exile, blindness, and ruin. Oedipus accuses Tiresias and Creon of plotting against him, while Tiresias insists that truth and Apollo support his revelation. Tiresias leaves, declaring that the murderer is a foreign-born man believed to be a stranger but actually a true-born Theban.

CHORUS. body

Oedipus confronts Creon over Tiresias's accusation that he murdered Laius, but Jocasta intervenes and persuades Oedipus to hear the evidence. Jocasta recounts that Laius was killed by robbers at a crossroads and that his infant son was abandoned, while Oedipus realizes that he once killed an older man and his attendants at such a crossroads. Oedipus sends for the surviving shepherd of Laius, hoping the man's testimony will prove that the murderers were many rather than one. The Chorus prays for reverence toward divine law and questions the authority of prophecy unless its warnings are fulfilled.

_Enter_ JOCASTA. body

Jocasta rejoices when the Corinthian Shepherd reports that Polybus has died, believing this disproves the prophecy that Oedipus would kill his father. The Shepherd then reveals that Oedipus was not Polybus's biological son but was given to him as a foundling after being discovered on Cithaeron with pierced feet. The Theban Shepherd admits that Jocasta gave him the infant to destroy because of a prophecy that the child would kill his father, but he instead handed the child to the Corinthian Shepherd. Jocasta leaves in horror, while Oedipus realizes that the prophecies concerning his parentage and crimes may all have been fulfilled.

CHORUS. body

Jocasta dies by hanging herself in the bridal room after learning the truth about her marriage and children. Oedipus finds her, blinds himself, and begs to be expelled from Thebes because he has killed Laius and married his mother. Creon becomes regent, agrees to care for Oedipus's daughters, and says that the gods must be consulted before Oedipus is exiled. The Chorus concludes that no mortal should be called happy until life has ended without sorrow.

ELECTRA body

Orestes returns to Mycenae with the Old Man and Pylades to avenge Agamemnon's murder, following Apollo's oracle. The Old Man is to spread a false report that Orestes has died in a chariot accident, while Orestes and Pylades prepare offerings at Agamemnon's tomb. Electra continues to mourn Agamemnon and denounce Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, while the Chorus of Argive Women urges her to moderate her grief. Chrysothemis reports Clytemnestra's troubling dream and is sent to make libations at Agamemnon's tomb, but Electra tells her to withhold the offerings and instead use their hair and a maiden-zone in their prayer for Orestes' victory.

CHORUS. body

The Chorus tells its daughter that it would be an erring, senseless seer if the prophetic Power did not soon overtake the offender. A vision of the night has freed the Chorus from fear by assuring it that the Achaean lord who gave the daughter being is not forgotten, nor is the bronzen-griding axe that killed him. The Chorus foresees the Fury visiting Argive land to punish a blood-stained pair for their forbidden dalliance. It also recalls that the house of Pelops has long accumulated crime and misfortune, beginning with Myrtilus’s death.

_Enter_ CLYTEMNESTRA. body

Clytemnestra argues with Electra that the murder of Electra's father was justified because he sacrificed Clytemnestra's daughter at Aulis. An Old Man reports that Orestes died in a chariot accident at the Delphian games, and Clytemnestra rejoices that she is freed from fear of him and Electra. Electra is devastated by the report and believes she has lost her only hope of avenging her father. Clytemnestra takes the Old Man into the palace and leaves Electra outside to mourn.

_Enter_ CHRYSOTHEMIS. body

Chrysothemis believes that Orestes has returned after seeing offerings and a newly cut lock of hair at the ancient tomb of their father. Electra tells her that Orestes is dead and urges her to help kill Aegisthus, whom she identifies as the guilty author of their father's death. Chrysothemis refuses, arguing that they are women without sufficient strength and that such a plot would bring certain ruin. After their dispute, the Chorus praises filial piety, condemns the dishonor of the ancestral house, and honors Electra's loyalty and endurance.

_Enter_ ORESTES _and_ PYLADES, _with the urn_. body

Orestes and Pylades arrive at the palace carrying an urn and falsely report that Orestes has died in a chariot race. Electra mourns over the urn until Orestes reveals himself by his voice and Agamemnon's signet. The Old Man helps direct the plan, and Orestes and Pylades enter the palace, where Orestes kills Clytemnestra. Aegisthus then arrives, is deceived by the supposed corpse, recognizes Orestes, and is compelled to enter the palace to face death.

THE TRACHINIAN MAIDENS body

Dêanira fears for Heracles, who has been absent for fifteen months, and recalls the prophetic scroll stating that he will either die after his campaign in Euboea or thereafter live free from trouble. Hyllus learns that Heracles is besieging Oechalia and agrees to seek news of him. Lichas arrives with captive women and reports that Heracles is alive and victorious, but a Messenger reveals that Heracles destroyed Oechalia because he loved Iole, its king's daughter, and has sent her to his household. Dêanira is shocked by the disclosure and confronts Lichas, who continues to deny or obscure the truth.

DÊ. Nay, I entreat thee by His name, whose fire body

DÊ asks Lichas to tell her the whole truth about Heracles and the maiden, saying that Love can overpower mortals and immortals alike. Lichas admits that the maiden's love for Heracles caused him to conquer Oechalia, and that he concealed this from DÊ out of concern for her feelings. DÊ resolves not to resist what she regards as the will of Heaven and orders Lichas to carry her message and gifts to the maiden.

CHORUS. body

Deianira gives Heracles a robe treated with the Centaur's gift, hoping to secure his love and prevent his attachment to the captive maiden. When the ointment causes wool exposed to sunlight to dissolve and foam, Deianira realizes that Nessus deceived her and that the gift may be poisonous. Hyllus reports that Heracles was afflicted by the robe during a sacrifice, killed Lichas in agony, and ordered that he be carried away. Hyllus condemns Deianira as his father's killer, and she silently leaves, resolving that her own death should follow if Heracles dies.

CHORUS. body

Deianira dies by stabbing herself after realizing that the robe she sent to Heracles has caused his torment, while Hyllus learns that she acted innocently under Nessus's deception. Heracles is brought in suffering terribly, condemns Deianira before learning of her death, and then understands that Nessus has fulfilled the prophecy of his own death. He orders Hyllus to carry him to Mount Oeta, build a pyre, and burn his body, and he also commands Hyllus to marry Iole. Hyllus reluctantly swears to obey, while the Chorus mourns the ruin brought upon Heracles's household.

PHILOCTETES body

Odysseus brings Neoptolemus to the deserted shore of Lemnos to recover Philoctetes and the bow of Heracles, which prophecy says are necessary for Troy's capture. He instructs Neoptolemus to deceive Philoctetes by claiming hatred for the Achaean army and Odysseus, while Odysseus withdraws to avoid being recognized. Neoptolemus initially objects to lying but agrees to the plan after Odysseus argues that neither force nor persuasion can obtain the bow. The Chorus of Mariners joins Neoptolemus, and Philoctetes is heard approaching in great pain from his isolated dwelling.

_Enter_ PHILOCTETES. body

Philoctetes tells Neoptolemus how the Atridae and Odysseus abandoned him on Lemnos with a grievous plague, leaving him to survive alone for ten years. Neoptolemus recounts how Odysseus and the Atridae deprived him of Achilles' armour, and he declares his hatred for them. A merchantman reports that Odysseus and Diomedes are pursuing Philoctetes because Helenus prophesied that Troy cannot be taken without him. Neoptolemus agrees to take Philoctetes home, but after learning of the pursuit, he and Philoctetes prepare to depart with Philoctetes' bow and other necessities.

CHORUS. body

Philoctetes suffers a severe recurrence of his wound while Neoptolemus guards him and receives his bow. After Philoctetes sleeps, Neoptolemus reveals that he is expected to take Philoctetes to Troy so that the plague may be cured, and Philoctetes accuses him of betrayal and demands the bow back. Odysseus arrives, orders Philoctetes to be forced aboard, and claims that Zeus has decreed the voyage. Odysseus eventually releases Philoctetes, but Neoptolemus delays departure, still troubled by pity for him.

MONODY. body

Philoctetes, abandoned on Lemnos because of his wound, mourns his suffering and refuses to sail to Troy or aid the Achaeans. Neoptolemus returns the bow he took by deceit, but Philoctetes still rejects the proposed voyage until Heracles appears and declares that Zeus requires Philoctetes and Neoptolemus to conquer Troy together. Heracles promises that Philoctetes will be healed by Asclepius and will win glory through the conquest of Troy. Philoctetes then bids farewell to Lemnos, and he and the Chorus prepare to sail for Troy.

OEDIPUS AT COLONOS body

Oedipus, old and blind, arrives at Colonos led by Antigone and rests near the sacred grove of the Eumenides. An Athenian warns them that the ground is forbidden, and the Chorus of Village Guardians orders Oedipus to leave after learning that he is the fate-wearied son of Laius. Oedipus argues that his crimes were committed unknowingly and that he has come under divine direction, promising blessings to those who shelter him. The Chorus agrees to let Theseus decide his fate, and Ismene arrives to join her father and sister.

_Enter_ ISMENE. body

Ismene visits Oedipus and reports that Eteocles and Polynices are fighting over the Theban throne, while an oracle says that Oedipus will bring salvation to the Theban race whether alive or dead. Oedipus curses his sons and refuses to aid them, praising instead Antigone and Ismene for caring for him during his exile. The Chorus instructs Oedipus in an offering to the holy powers of the grove, and Ismene goes to perform it. Oedipus also recounts that he unknowingly killed his father and fathered children with his mother, insisting that divine law frees him from guilt for the killing.

_Enter_ THESEUS. body

Theseus welcomes Oedipus to Athens and agrees to protect him, accepting the promised benefit of Oedipus's burial place for the city. Oedipus explains that his sons seek to force him back to Thebes because a prophecy foretells their defeat by Athens, but he refuses to return and asks Theseus to let him remain. Creon arrives to take Oedipus back, then has Antigone seized and threatens to seize Oedipus as well. The Chorus of Colonian elders resists Creon's actions and calls for the ruler of Athens to intervene.

_Enter_ THESEUS. body

Theseus learns that Creon has seized Oedipus's two children from the sacred refuge at Colonos. He orders the people of Athens to pursue the captors and demands that Creon lead him to the abducted maidens. Creon claims that Athens would not willingly shelter Oedipus, whom he calls an incestuous parricide, while Oedipus argues that his crimes were committed unknowingly. Theseus rejects Creon's actions as unlawful and vows to restore the children to Oedipus.

CHORUS. body

Theseus returns Oedipus's daughters safely after rescuing them, and Oedipus praises him and Athens for their justice and protection. Polynices comes as a suppliant, asking Oedipus to support his campaign against Thebes, but Oedipus curses him and Eteocles, declaring that they will die by one another's hands. Antigone urges Polynices to abandon the war because the prophecy foretells mutual death, but he refuses and departs to face his doomed enterprise. The Chorus celebrates the rescue, anticipates battle, and reflects that death is the final release from life's suffering.

CHORUS. body

Oedipus recognizes that the Gods' thunder and lightning announce his imminent death and asks Theseus to come. He leads Theseus and his daughters to the sacred place where he is to die, entrusting Theseus with a secret that will protect Athens and asking him to safeguard Antigone and Ismene. Oedipus disappears from the sight of his companions in a mysterious, painless manner, leaving Theseus as the only witness. Antigone and Ismene mourn him and ask Theseus to help them return to Thebes, which he promises to do.
