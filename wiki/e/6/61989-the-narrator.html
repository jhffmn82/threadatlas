the narrator in An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall

Dinah Maria Craik · 1884

The narrator is a travelling woman and conductor of a Cornwall tour with two young companions, her “chickens.” She values holidays, modest preparation, economy, health, cheerfulness, and attentive enjoyment of scenery and people. Though her feet are feeble, she is steady-minded and protects the chickens from rash adventures and illness. The journey begins in a comfortable carriage from Falmouth toward the Lizard, with Charles; she discusses temperance, hears of his bereavement, and gathers Cornish heath. At Mullion Cove she lets the companions explore a difficult descent while she remains above, then visits a cave, admires the shore and sunset, observes tide-bound visitors, and returns with assistance. She enjoys tea at the Old Inn and notices farmyard animals and moonlight. On Sunday she walks about Lizard, attends church at Landewednack, watches sunset and moonlit country, and sees calm beauty turn into a violent storm, fearing the holiday spoiled. More than a year later, she finishes this account of fifteen innocent days as a memorial of “a good time.”

the narrator in An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall

Dinah Maria Craik · 1884

The narrator, a mother of six children, travels with girls around the Lizard and Kynance Cove. She balances curiosity and affection with caution: at the Bellows and Asparagus Island she distinguishes sensible bravery from foolhardy risk, warns the girls away from Hell’s Mouth, and leaves before the tide blocks the route. Walking to Lizard Point, she receives news of sickness and death but finds consolation in moonlight. On her final day she is sad to leave, anxious over Church Cove’s slippery rocks, joins a boat trip and cave visit, hears John’s wreck account, and departs grateful for local kindness and companionship.

DAY THE SEVENTH John Curgenven had said last night, with his air of tender patronising, half regal, half paternal, which we declared always reminded us of King Arthur--"Ladies, whenever you settle to go to Kynance, I'll take you.

The narrator travels with the girls to Kynance Cove, cautiously joins them at the Bellows, and waits while they climb Asparagus Island. She speaks with an elderly clergyman about sensible bravery and foolhardy risk, helps warn her girls against Hell's Mouth, and leaves before the tide cuts off the route. She later walks to Lizard Point, hears sad news of sickness and death, and finds consolation in the moonlight.

" DAY THE NINTH And our last at the Lizard, which a week ago had been to us a mere word or dot in a map; now we carried away from it a living human interest in everything and everybody.

The narrator feels sad to leave the Lizard and apprehensive about crossing the rough, seaweed-covered rocks at Church Cove. She joins the boat trip, listens to John's account of the Brest wreck, visits the caves, and later records her gratitude for John, the laundress, the apple giver, and the Helstone hosts.
