Abstract written by a model

A fan moves air by centrifugal force: air enters at its center, is gradually given motion and velocity by curved blades, turns through a right angle, and escapes at the circumference. It must overcome air inertia and frictional resistance at the blades. Curved blades, easy curves, and suitable proportions reduce violent deflection and noise, especially at high speeds; blades may be narrower at their tips for silent operation. Air exit speed cannot exceed fan-tip velocity and is about two-thirds of it in a good fan. Higher-pressure designs use a widening arithmetical-spiral case, longer narrower blades, and smaller inlet openings. Power depends on blade area, air pressure, and the center-of-effort velocity; calculated ideal efficiency is never fully attained.

THE EFFICIENCY OF FANS.

A fan puts the weight of air in motion, overcoming inertia and frictional resistances. Air enters at the center and escapes at the circumference, with its motion changed through a right angle; gradually curved blades reduce violent deflection and noise. The fan's power requirement depends on blade area, air pressure, and the velocity of the center of effort, while its casing should widen as an arithmetical spiral toward the discharge opening.
