Scientific American Supplement No. 360, November 25, 1882

Abstract written by a model

This document is a wide-ranging illustrated technical and scientific periodical, assembling reports, inventions, experiments, industrial methods, agricultural practices, natural-history notices, and social commentary. Its central concern is practical improvement: saving fuel and labor, raising production, analyzing materials, and applying scientific knowledge in industry and daily life. It opens with metallurgy and power, including John Gjers’s fuel-saving soaking pits for steel ingots, L. Clemandot’s compression tempering of steel, William Barnet Le Van’s comparison of steam-power systems, furnace-gas analysis with Dr. Buente’s burette, and the “Universal” gas engine of J. A. Ewins and H. Newman. Other engineering articles describe river works near St. Louis, gas furnaces, fan efficiency, coal-refuse fuel, textile sizing, coke breaking, printing machinery, arc lighting, and apparatus for entering flames. Several accounts emphasize quantified performance, such as fuel savings, hourly capacities, and operating costs. Chemical and manufacturing subjects follow through pure water for photography, black phosphorus, steep water, the revivifying of bone-black, soap making, cotton seed oil, and rattlesnake poison. Food, health, and household use recur in discussions of the soy bean, human food and energy, apple jelly manufacture, and agricultural lime burning. The document also turns to public resources and development: a Southern New Mexico mining rush, the United States building-stone supply, a Census-described expansion of farms, improvements in fruit culture, and M. Pifre’s solar-heat apparatus, which operated machinery and prepared food and drink. Professor L. M. Haupt supplies a practical astronomical method for establishing a true meridian. Natural history and culture broaden the issue’s scope. It records the newly obtained specimen of Rheinardius ocellatus, describes the Maidenhair tree and the planned Woods of America collection, and examines Chinese finger and palm impressions as signatures. Matthew Arnold’s essay on “Lucidity” argues for intellectual clarity through schools and universities. Across its many subjects, the document moves from industrial processes and instruments to agriculture, energy, surveying, natural specimens, and reflections on society, consistently presenting observation and experiment as tools for progress.

SOAKING PITS FOR STEEL INGOTS.

John Gjers describes the soaking pit process for rolling steel ingots using their own initial heat, without a fuel-fired heating furnace. The process places freshly stripped ingots in individually covered, preheated brick-lined pits for 20–30 minutes, allowing internal heat to become more evenly distributed. At the Darlington Steel and Iron Company's Works, about 125 tons per shift were bloomed by this method, and double-head rails were rolled directly from treated ingots with satisfactory quality. The process is intended to reduce fuel use, labor, furnace maintenance, oxidation, and loss of steel yield.

TEMPERING BY COMPRESSION.

L. Clemandot devised a method called tempering by compression for treating metals, especially steel. The process heats metal to a cherry red, compresses it strongly, and maintains pressure until it cools completely. The resulting metal is exceedingly hard, has contracted molecularly, and has a fine grain that resembles polished nickel. Compressed steel also acquires coercitive force and retains magnetism, with no loss observed after three months.

ECONOMICAL STEAM POWER.

In “Economical Steam Power,” William Barnet Le Van explains that economical steam power depends on the coordinated arrangement of engines, boilers, and transmission accessories. He compares single and subdivided power systems, engine forms, condensing and non-condensing engines, and different engine classes according to their first cost, operating hours, fuel, water, repairs, and depreciation. Using a flouring mill in Philadelphia as an example, he shows that replacing two engines with one first-class automatic engine greatly reduced coal and feed-water consumption and saved $4,954 over 300 operating days.

RIVER IMPROVEMENTS NEAR ST.

River improvements near St. Louis on the Mississippi River are progressing satisfactorily through jetty construction, mattress placement, bank revetment, and related work. At Twin Hollow, Carroll's Island, and Beard's Island, engineers are addressing sand bars and protecting or concentrating the river channel. About 1,200 men, thirty barges and scows, two steam launches, and the stern-wheel steamer A. A. Humphreys are employed. The work has cost about $200,000, and an appropriation of approximately $600,000 for the ensuing year offers prospects for a clear channel.

BUNTE'S BURETTE FOR THE ANALYSIS OF FURNACE GASES. .. _Mode of Measuring the Gases and Absorption_.

Dr. Buente's burette is presented as a simpler and more easily operated apparatus for analyzing blast-furnace gases than the various Orsat apparatus. The procedure describes filling the burette, measuring gas volumes, and absorbing carbonic acid, oxygen, and oxide of carbon with specified liquids. Combustion in a heated tube, using atmospheric air and a palladium wire, permits quantitative analysis of hydrogen and oxide of carbon. Changes in gas volume after absorption or combustion are used to calculate the proportions of the gases.

THE "UNIVERSAL" GAS ENGINE.

The Universal gas engine was invented by J. A. Ewins and H. Newman and made by T. B. Barker of Birmingham. It is a simple, single-acting gas engine made in sizes from one-eighth to one horse-power, with larger sizes being developed. Its main new feature is a rotary ignition valve that uses radial slots to pass flame into the cylinder and ignite the gas-air charge. The engines were reported to work successfully and to have low operating costs.

] GAS FURNACE FOR BAKING REFRACTORY PRODUCTS.

The gas furnace for baking refractory products is a smaller annular furnace designed for small establishments, with a baking chamber set into the ground and covered by movable refractory-clay slabs. Gas enters through channels, pipes, and vertical conduits before emerging through tuyeres into the baking chamber. A smoking furnace supplies hot gases for preliminary heating, with adjustable cold-air intake allowing its output temperature to vary from about 30 to 50 degrees upward. The smoking apparatus heated by gas avoids the cinders, dust, and difficult gradual heating associated with ordinary smoking systems.

THE EFFICIENCY OF FANS.

The unit explains the work required to move air and the factors affecting the efficiency of fans. It describes how blade shape, speed, area, and casing influence air motion, power consumption, noise, and pressure. It presents a calculation estimating the power and air delivery of a fan, while noting that such efficiency is not achieved in practice. Mr. Buckle's recommended fan proportions for pressures of 3 to 6 ounces per square inch are also given.

MACHINE FOR COMPRESSING COAL REFUSE INTO FUEL.

The unit concerns the utilization of coal refuse to manufacture agglomerated artificial fuel for railways, steamboats, industry, and domestic heating. It describes the preparation of coal refuse, including sifting, washing, partial drying, mixing with coal dust and pitch, crushing, steaming, and conversion into a plastic paste. It also describes Bilan's machine, which compresses the paste into spherical conglomerates using four coordinated moulding wheels. The machine can produce about 40 tons of globular artificial fuel per day.

HANK SIZING AND WRINGING MACHINE.

The unit describes a hank sizing and wringing machine made by Heywood & Spencer of Radcliffe, near Manchester. The machine uses geared hooks to twist hanks, hold them briefly while excess size is wiped off, and then reverse the twist before removal. It can be used for sizing and fancy dyeing, with running performed on one side while finishing is performed on the other. The makers claim production rates of about 36 10-pound bundles per hour at finishing and 45 bundles in 2-pound wringers at running.

] IMPROVED COKE BREAKER.

The improved coke breaker used by the South Metropolitan Gas Company consists of a single drum with cutting edges that breaks coke against a fixed grid. Its capacity can be changed by adding or removing cast rings, while the grid distance controls the fineness of the broken coke. The machine requires little power, produces relatively little waste, and can separate the output into larger coke, clean breeze, and dust. At full capacity, it can break about 8 tons per hour, and broken coke generally sells for 2s. to 2s. 6d. more per ton than large coke.

] IMPROVEMENT IN PRINTING MACHINERY.

The improvement in printing machinery is the design of Alfred Godfrey of Clapton. It uses a vibrating rack, A, and a pinion, a1, supported by a radial arm, a2, mounted on the shaft, a3, which supports the driving wheel, a4. The arrangement is intended to transmit easy, uniform, and silent motion to the rack and table through the vibrating movement of the arm, pinion, and rack.

] A CHARACTERISTIC MINING "RUSH.

The unit describes a mining rush in Southern New Mexico, centered on the newly active Perche district near Lake Valley and Hillsboro. Jack Shedd made the first great strike in the Perche district on August 15, finding surface blocks of exceptionally rich silver ore. The discovery drew about 2,000 prospectors and led to more than 1,000 claims, while properties such as the Solitaire and Bullion attracted investors and development. The Perche district appeared likely to become a major mining center despite uncertainty about some discoveries.

THE SOY BEAN.

The unit concerns the Soy bean and its nutritional qualities and uses. The British Medical Journal reports that Prof. E. Kinch describes it as more like animal food than any other known vegetable production because it is rich in fat and albuminoids. It is widely used as food in China and Japan, has been cultivated with fair success in Italy and France, and provides foods and useful fodder from its straw.

ON A NEW ARC ELECTRIC LAMP. .. _Electrical Arrangement_.

W.H. Preece describes a new arc electric lamp and the system invented by M. Abdank. The system separates the regulator from the lamp, allowing the lamp to be installed in inaccessible locations while the regulator remains accessible. A balance using two solenoids controls a break that makes minute adjustments to the carbon electrodes as carbon is consumed, maintaining a steady electric arc. An automatic cut-off protects series-connected lamps by replacing a failed lamp with an equivalent resistance.

APPARATUS FOR OBTAINING PURE WATER FOR PHOTOGRAPHIC USE.

The unit concerns methods and apparatus for obtaining clean or distilled water for photographic use. It explains that rain-water collected after roofs have been washed can provide clean soft water, and describes an automatic shunt for diverting the initial dirty flow. It also discusses glass and metal distillation apparatus for preparing highly pure water, especially for nitrate baths and emulsions. The text details photographic problems caused by chlorides, lime, calcium oxalate, and other impurities in water.

[Illustration] BLACK PHOSPHORUS.

P. Thenard examines black, or dark gray, phosphorus and challenges the view that it is always ordinary phosphorus mixed with traces of a metallic phosphide. A specimen became white when melted and stayed white after sudden cooling, but turned black again when allowed to become superfused and then contacted with white or black phosphorus. When part of the specimen was dissolved in carbon disulphide, only a trace of very pale yellow matter remained undissolved, appearing to be amorphous phosphorus.

COMPOSITION OF STEEP WATER.

The unit describes the composition of water in which malt has been steeped, according to M. C. Leeuw. It contains 0.56 percent organic matter and 0.52 percent mineral matter, for a total of 1.08 percent dry matter, including 0.033 percent nitrogen. Its mineral matter includes potash, phosphoric acid, lime, soda, magnesia, sulphuric acid, traces of oxide of iron, and no chlorine or silica.

212 " SCHREIBER'S APPARATUS FOR REVIVIFYING BONE-BLACK. .. _The Animal Black Furnace_.

Schreiber's apparatus revivifies bone-black by washing it with water and acid in a rotary washer, then drying, reheating, and cooling it for reuse. The apparatus includes filters, wells, a washer, an elevator, an automatic drying system, and a continuous furnace. The animal black furnace uses undulating cast-iron retorts heated by a coke fire, followed by cooling pipes that deliver the treated black ready for bagging. An apparatus of the described dimensions can revivify 120 hectoliters of bone-black in twenty-four hours using 360 to 400 kilogrammes of coke.

] SOAP AND ITS MANUFACTURE, FROM A CONSUMER'S POINT OF VIEW.

The article explains to consumers how to make pure, neutral potash soap and contrasts it with soda soap. It gives directions for mixing caustic potash lye with tallow, grease, or oil, and describes how the mixture becomes soap after standing warm for a week. It recommends cotton seed oil for manufacturers and laundry soap, warns that products sold as crystal, ball, or rock potash are actually caustic soda, and discusses the possible benefits of fleece scouring for sheep owners.

COTTON SEED OIL.

S.S. Bradford reports on using cotton seed oil in place of olive oil for lead plaster and several pharmacopoeial liniments. He found that cotton seed oil produced lead plaster of equally good quality when one and one-half pints of boiling water were used instead of one-half pint. It performed well in Linimentum Ammoniae, Linimentum Camphorae, and Linimentum Chloroformi, but was unsuitable for Linimentum Calcis. Bradford also observed that cotton seed oil turned reddish when mixed with liquor plumbi subacetatis, providing a way to detect small amounts of it in olive oil.

THE FOOD AND ENERGY OF MAN.

Prof. de Chaumont’s lecture explains how food is used to build and repair the human body, produce energy, and generate animal heat. It classifies food into nitrogenous substances, fats, starches and sugars, salts, water, and air, emphasizing the need for adequate quantities and variety. The lecture also discusses alcohol, tea, coffee, tobacco, and oatmeal gruel in relation to fatigue and useful work. It concludes by addressing scurvy outbreaks among seamen and urging varied provisions, including potatoes and lime-juice, aboard ships.

RATTLESNAKE POISON.

Henry H. Croft discusses whether rattlesnake poison is an alkaloid and reports tests of its reaction with iodine. Mr. Pedler had reported that platinic chloride destroys or neutralizes snake poison, probably by forming an insoluble double platinic chloride. Croft observed a dense light-brown precipitate when iodine solution was added to poison from a rattlesnake, and he describes iodine in potassium iodide as a favored Texas remedy for rattlesnake bites. He questions the usefulness of potassium permanganate after poison has been absorbed and reports finding ammonium urate in the snake's bladder.

THE CHINESE SIGN MANUAL.

The unit describes the use of finger and palm impressions as signatures in China, especially in courts, the army, and certain domestic transactions. Chinese courts require criminals to validate confessions with a thumb-print, while digital signatures are also used to prevent personation and repudiation. The text discusses observations by Henry Faulds and W. J. Herschel concerning skin-furrows, heredity, government registration, medical jurisprudence, and ethnology. It concludes that Chinese and European finger rugae differ, but their variation is too great for reliable ethnic or hereditary classification.

LUCIDITY.

Matthew Arnold argued that lucidity was the great need of England and an indispensable condition for serious intellectual and spiritual construction. He contrasted the French national tendency toward lucidity with the English national tendency toward seriousness, while warning that both qualities could be accompanied by serious defects. He identified Voltaire as the chief agent of France's eighteenth-century movement toward lucidity and compared him with Luther. Arnold criticized the Salvation Army and the Oxford movement associated with Dr. Pusey for building on assumptions that lucidity would have exposed as no longer valid, and he endorsed schools and universities as the best means of cultivating lucidity.

ON SOME APPARATUS THAT PERMIT OF ENTERING FLAMES.

The unit describes apparatus and experiments designed to protect people entering flames, focusing on Humphry Davy's wire-gauze discovery and Chevalier Aldini's protective suit. Aldini's demonstrations included handling hot iron, walking over fires, breathing amid flames, and entering burning passages while shielded by metallic gauze and asbestos cloth. Hot-house and furnace experiments involving Joseph Banks, Charles Blagden, Dr. Solander, and others showed that people could withstand very high dry temperatures, partly because perspiration cools the body. The account also considers human respiration in flames and proposes that Egyptian mystery fire-walking may have used a concealed fire beneath a grate.

THE BUILDING STONE SUPPLY.

The unit describes the development and distribution of the building stone supply in the United States, including granite, sandstone or freestone, limestone, marble, slate, and other stones. It explains that early settlers mainly used wood and imported brick and stone, but American quarrying expanded during the nineteenth century. Major quarrying centers and uses are identified, including construction, paving, monuments, lime production, roofing, and sidewalks. The account names notable buildings, quarries, and regions associated with these materials.

AN INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION.

The Census reports that the number of farms in the United States nearly doubled within twenty years, despite slower population growth. The increase resulted from the division of great estates, the creation of farms in newly settled regions, and growing markets supplied by cities and towns. Farm growth was substantial in older Northern States and especially rapid in several Southern States and the Northwest. The expansion of farm ownership and occupancy is described as an industrial revolution that may produce important political changes.

A FARMER'S LIME KILN.

The unit describes a farmer’s lime kiln, based on an article furnished by a correspondent of the Country Gentleman. The kiln is a 13-foot-square, 25-foot-high stone structure with an arched firebox, an iron-clamped wall, a fire-brick-lined well, and a railway track for loading. It can produce about 100 bushels of lime in 24 hours and uses coal, with the required quantity and heat regulated through experimentation. The text explains that successful burning requires skill and judgment, and that the kiln’s dimensions may be reduced or modified for wood.

THE MANUFACTURE OF APPLE JELLY.

Dewitt C. Peck reports on five apple jelly factories operating in Oswego County, New York, including the largest factory near the village of Mexico. The Mexico factory uses water power, specialized washing, pressing, clarification, and evaporation equipment to turn natural, ungrafted, crabbed, frozen, and other apples into jelly. It produces 1,500 to 1,800 pounds of jelly per ten-hour day, with improvements expected to raise output above one ton daily, and it also recovers apple seeds whose value covers the wages of the workers. The factory was erected late in 1880 and produced about forty-five tons of jelly that year.

IMPROVED GRAPE BAGS.

The improved grape bag, invented by M. Pelletier of Paris, uses a curved glass square to protect grapes and concentrate sunlight, hastening their maturity. Experiments reportedly found that curved glass produced temperatures averaging 7 degrees higher than plain glass, while the bags gave temperatures about 10 degrees higher than uncovered grapes. The device is proposed for outdoor grape cultivation on walls and may also help ripen pears and other choice fruits. The text argues that better training and care, combined with such bags, could improve grape production despite uncertain, cool summers.

UTILIZATION OF SOLAR HEAT.

The solar heat utilization apparatus, developed from Professor Mouchon's idea and advanced by M. Pifre, concentrated solar rays to heat a boiler and power machinery. At a fete in the Tuileries Gardens, it operated a printing machine, made cider and coffee, and set a pump in motion despite weak sunshine. A portable version could boil water for several men, providing drinking and cooking water in the field. The writer urged English public and military authorities to investigate it promptly for use by soldiers in India and Egypt.

HOW TO ESTABLISH A TRUE MERIDIAN. / INTRODUCTORY. .. ] APPROXIMATE EQUATION OF TIME.

Professor L. M. Haupt presents a practical method for establishing a true meridian without relying on the magnetic needle. The method uses observations of Polaris to determine latitude and then applies Polaris's azimuth at eastern or western elongation to mark the meridian. Haupt explains the relevant astronomical definitions, gives an azimuth table and refraction table, and describes field procedures using a transit and other surveying equipment. He also explains how an established meridian can be used to determine magnetic variation, local time, and difference of longitude, with the equation of time applied to solar observations.

|__________|____________| THE OCELLATED PHEASANT.

The Museum of Natural History of Paris received a magnificent adult specimen of the bird named Rheinardius ocellatus, previously known only from a few caudal feathers. The specimen confirmed that the bird is a distinct genus in the family Phasianidae, placed after Argus and linking that genus with the true pheasants. Rheinardius ocellatus has a very long, broad tail of twelve ocellated feathers and differs from the common Argus of Borneo in its plumage, tail, head, and body proportions. The specimen was captured at Buih-Dinh in a previously unexplored part of Tonkin and given to the museum by Mr. Maingonnat.

THE MAIDENHAIR TREE.

The text describes the Maidenhair tree, Gingkgo biloba, as an ornamental deciduous tree with fan-shaped leaves and unusual botanical features. It is classified as a conifer allied to the Yew, though it lacks resin, and is known from Japan and China mainly near temples and buildings. The species has a long fossil history and was introduced into Europe in the eighteenth century, where male trees are much more common than female trees. The illustrated tree at Broadlands, Hants, is about 40 feet tall and has a trunk measuring 7 feet in girth.

] THE WOODS OF AMERICA.

The American Museum of Natural History is preparing The Woods of America collection, consisting of hundreds of logs representing nearly every tree variety indigenous to the United States. The specimens are being seasoned, diagrammed, and cut and polished to display their bark, growth rings, sapwood, heartwood, and grain. Prof. Bickmore is directing a method intended to prevent logs from splitting during seasoning. The completed exhibition is intended for scientific, educational, architectural, forestry, and commercial use.
