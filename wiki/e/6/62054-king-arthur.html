King Arthur in An Unsentimental Journey through Cornwall

Dinah Maria Craik · 1884

King Arthur is a legendary or possibly nonexistent king of Britain, regarded by some as a Cornishman and by others as an ideal knight or noble modern gentleman. Debate over whether he lived is central: practical minds doubt him, while tradition presents him as a figure of goodness and knighthood amid a violent age. He is described as the son of Uther Pendragon and Ygrayne, raised by Sir Ector, proclaimed king after Uther’s death, founder of the Order of Knights of the Round Table, and husband of Queen Guinevere. His story ends at Camelford, where Mordred conquers and grievously wounds him. Arthur orders Sir Bedevere to cast Excalibur into a mere, then sails with three queens to Avillion to be healed. Accounts differ over whether he was buried, remains in fairy land, or will return to Cornwall; he is also believed to revisit Tintagel as a chough, and a mound is identified as his grave.

4 sections; each heading opens the text

"Shall we get a boat?

King Arthur is imagined in connection with John Curgenven's refined and beautiful face. The narrator considers whether Arthur ever lived and recalls the belief that he sailed to Avillion to be healed and may return someday.

DAY THE FIFTH "Hope for the best, and be prepared for the worst," had been the motto of our journey.

The travelers debate whether King Arthur ever existed. Some argue that, if he existed, he may have been only a rough barbarian rather than the noble figure presented by Tennyson.

DAY THE THIRTEENTH Into King Arthurs land--Tintagel his birth-place, and Camelford, where he fought his last battle--the legendary region of which one may believe as much or as little as one pleases--we were going to-day.

King Arthur is presented as the son of King Uther Pendragon and Ygrayne, raised by Sir Ector, and proclaimed king after Uther’s death. He founded the Order of Knights of the Round Table, married Queen Guinevere, and was defeated and mortally wounded by Mordred at Camelford. He was carried to a mere, where he ordered Sir Bedevere to throw Excalibur into the water, and then departed in a boat with three queens; some traditions say he was buried, while others say he remains in fairy land and will return.

DAYS FOURTEENTH, FIFTEENTH, AND SIXTEENTH-- And all Arthurian days, so I will condense them into one chapter, and not spin out the hours that were flying so fast.

King Arthur appears in the narrator's reflections as a possible ideal of goodness and knighthood shining through a violent and morally brutal age. His soul is said to be believed to revisit Tintagel in the shape of a chough, and a driver later points out a mound said to be his grave.
