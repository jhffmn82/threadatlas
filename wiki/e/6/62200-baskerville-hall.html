Baskerville Hall

place · 1 work · 13 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Baskerville, the Hall, the home of his fathers, the home of my own people, the house, the old house, this house of gloom, this household, this roof
  • In The Hound of the Baskervilles: Baskerville Hall is an ancient manor house and family home in Devonshire, the ancestral seat and residence of the Baskerville family. Set near the moor and yew alley and surrounded by wood, it has ivy-covered granite walls, twin ancient towers, a dark avenue, a large heavily timbered hall, and a second-floor western lattice window with the nearest outlook onto the moor. Sir Charles lived there and took his final nightly walk from the yew alley; its grim reputation and the danger feared for a Baskerville make occupancy uncertain. After inheriting, Sir Henry resolves to return despite warnings, arriving with Dr. John Watson to be received by Barrymore and his wife. The Hall initially seems gloomy, though morning sunlight makes it appear less so. Its nights remain troubled by a woman crying, unexplained sobbing, suspicions about Barrymore, secret business, and threats associated with the escaped Selden, a stranger, noises, fog, rain, and the surrounding moor. Barrymore and his wife use the Hall to signal Selden by candle and provide him food. While Sir Henry lives there with Watson, he plans extensive renovation and refurnishing to restore its grandeur; Stapleton believes an inhabited Hall would benefit poor people on the moor. Watson is expected to remain with Sir Henry, but after later night events Holmes and Watson bring Sir Henry back feverish and delirious for Dr. Mortimer’s care. The Hall’s history is finally tied to Stapleton, who established himself nearby and arranged the circumstances of Sir Charles’s death as Sir Charles prepared to leave on medical advice.

Baskerville Hall in The Hound of the Baskervilles

Arthur Conan Doyle · 1901/1902

Baskerville Hall is an ancient manor house and family home in Devonshire, the ancestral seat and residence of the Baskerville family. Set near the moor and yew alley and surrounded by wood, it has ivy-covered granite walls, twin ancient towers, a dark avenue, a large heavily timbered hall, and a second-floor western lattice window with the nearest outlook onto the moor. Sir Charles lived there and took his final nightly walk from the yew alley; its grim reputation and the danger feared for a Baskerville make occupancy uncertain. 14 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2.

Baskerville Hall is the ancestral residence of the Baskerville family and the home where Sir Charles Baskerville lived. It contains the yew alley from which Sir Charles took his final nightly walk, and its grim reputation could have made it difficult to find a tenant.

Chapter 3.

Baskerville Hall is the home of the Baskerville family and lies beside the yew alley and the moor. Holmes says that Sir Henry might be expected to return to the home of his fathers, although Dr. Mortimer fears the place is deadly for a Baskerville.

Chapter 4.

Baskerville Hall is the home of Sir Henry’s people and the destination under consideration after his inheritance. Although Holmes warns that there may be danger there, Sir Henry declares that no person or devil will prevent him from going.

Chapter 5.

Baskerville Hall is the Devonshire residence to which Sir Henry Baskerville plans to return. Barrymore is reported to be present there and in charge of the Hall.

Chapter 6.

Baskerville Hall is the ancient home of the Baskerville family, with ivy-covered granite walls, twin towers, a dark avenue, and a large, heavily timbered hall. Sir Henry Baskerville and Dr. Watson arrive there, and Barrymore and his wife receive them. During the night, Dr. Watson hears the sound of a woman crying somewhere in the house.

Chapter 7.

In the morning sunlight, Baskerville Hall seems much less gloomy to Dr. Watson and Sir Henry than it had the previous evening. The Hall remains associated with the unexplained sobbing heard during the night and with suspicions surrounding Barrymore.

Baskerville Hall is the place where Sir Henry has come to live. Beryl says Stapleton wants the Hall inhabited because he believes this would benefit the poor people of the moor.

Chapter 8.

Baskerville Hall is the isolated household from which Watson sends his report and where he, Sir Henry Baskerville, and other residents live under concern about the escaped convict. Watson believes that secret business is occurring inside the house.

Chapter 9.

Baskerville Hall is being prepared for major renovation and refurnishing. Sir Henry has engaged an architect, a London contractor, decorators, and furnishers to restore the grandeur of his family home.

Baskerville Hall is the place from which the Barrymores send a candle signal to Selden. The signal tells him that food is ready and helps him identify where to collect it.

Chapter 10.

Baskerville Hall is surrounded by fog, rain, and the bleak moor, contributing to the atmosphere of danger. It is the home of Sir Henry and is repeatedly connected with the threats posed by Selden, the stranger, and the unexplained noises.

Chapter 12.

Baskerville Hall is the place where Dr. John Watson is expected to remain with Sir Henry Baskerville. Sherlock Holmes reminds Watson that his duty requires him not to stay away from the Hall for too long.

Chapter 14.

Baskerville Hall is where Holmes and Watson take Sir Henry after the night’s events. Sir Henry later lies there delirious with a high fever under Dr. Mortimer’s care.

Chapter 15.

Baskerville Hall was the ancestral home near which Stapleton established himself. Sir Charles was preparing to leave the Hall on Dr. Mortimer’s advice when Stapleton arranged the circumstances of his death.
