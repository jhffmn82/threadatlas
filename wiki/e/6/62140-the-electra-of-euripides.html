The Electra of Euripides

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The document introduces Euripides’ Electra as a realistic, tightly constructed, intellectually rebellious tragedy that revises the Orestes-and-Electra legend associated with Aeschylus and Sophocles. Its central concern is the moral horror of matricide and Apollo’s command, while Electra is presented as the chief figure: damaged by grief, hatred, poverty, frustrated ambition, lost youth, and love for Agamemnon. Living in a poor mountain hut near Argos, she suffers under Clytemnestra and Aegisthus and is married to an honorable Peasant. Orestes returns secretly from exile with Pylades, initially conceals his identity, and is recognized by the Old Man through a childhood scar. The siblings plan revenge: Orestes goes to kill Aegisthus at a feast, while Electra lures Clytemnestra by falsely claiming to have borne a son. A messenger announces Aegisthus’s death, and Electra celebrates over his body. When Clytemnestra arrives, Orestes hesitates, fearing the sin, but Electra presses him onward. They kill their mother in repayment for Agamemnon’s murder and Iphigenia’s sacrifice, then are overwhelmed by guilt. Castor and Polydeuces assign their futures: Electra will marry Pylades, and Orestes will face trial, exile, and eventual release; Apollo’s command is blamed for the deed.

Introduction[1]

The introduction presents Euripides' Electra as a realistic, tightly constructed, intellectual, and rebellious tragedy that challenges conventional classicism. It compares Euripides' treatment of the legend of Orestes and Electra with those of Aeschylus and Sophocles, focusing on the moral horror of matricide and the role of Apollo's command. Euripides portrays Orestes and especially Electra as psychologically damaged real people rather than heroic or villainous figures. Electra is identified as the tragedy's central character: a woman consumed by grief, hatred, love, poverty, frustrated ambition, and the loss of her youth.

ELECTRA

The unit introduces Electra, who lives in a poor hut on the mountains of Argos and mourns Agamemnon while enduring hardship under Aegisthus and Clytemnestra. Orestes returns secretly from exile with Pylades to plan vengeance for Agamemnon's murder, and he questions Electra without revealing his identity. Electra describes her suffering, her marriage to the Peasant, the dishonor shown to Agamemnon's grave, and her determination to kill Clytemnestra. The Peasant welcomes Orestes and Pylades, and Orestes recognizes his humble household as honorable.

[ORESTES _and_ PYLADES _go in, following the_ SERVANTS.

Electra's brother Orestes returns from exile with Pylades and is recognized by the Old Man through a scar from his childhood. The Old Man tells Orestes that Aegisthus is holding a feast for the wild-wood nymphs outside the city, and Orestes plans to approach him there. Electra devises a scheme to lure Clytemnestra to her house by claiming that she has borne a son. Orestes and the Old Man depart to begin the plan to kill Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, while Electra remains with a sword, awaiting news.

CHORUS.

The Chorus recalls the golden lamb that brought kingship to the house of Atreus and describes the cosmic disorder caused by its theft. A messenger reports that Orestes and Pylades have killed Aegisthus during a sacrificial feast, and Electra celebrates the victory and condemns Aegisthus before his body is removed. Orestes presents Aegisthus's corpse and then sees Clytemnestra approaching. Electra urges Orestes to carry out the planned matricide, while he fears the sin and consequences of killing their mother before entering the house.

LEADER.

Electra and Orestes kill Clytemnestra and Aegisthus in retribution for the murder of Agamemnon and the sacrifice of Iphigenia. Tormented by the deed and by blood-guilt, they mourn Clytemnestra before Castor and Polydeuces appear to announce their futures. Electra is to marry Pylades and leave Argos, while Orestes must go to Athens, face trial, and endure exile before being freed from persecution. Castor and Polydeuces declare that Apollo’s command caused the deed, and that justice and fate will determine its consequences.
