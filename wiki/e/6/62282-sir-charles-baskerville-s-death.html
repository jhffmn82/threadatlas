Sir Charles Baskerville's death in The Hound of the Baskervilles

Arthur Conan Doyle · 1901/1902

Sir Charles Baskerville’s death is the central event of a continuing murder investigation. After taking his customary nightly walk in the yew alley, he was found dead at its far end, without visible injury or mark. Dr. James Mortimer noted terror on his face and fresh footprints of a gigantic hound; other evidence suggested that Sir Charles had waited at the moor-gate before fleeing down the alley. The coroner’s jury attributed the death to cardiac exhaustion, but the unexplained tracks, reports of a spectral creature, and signs of fear left open questions of crime, human agency, and supernatural causes. The death deeply shocked Barrymore and his wife, as well as Beryl and Stapleton, and Beryl believed it strengthened fears about the Baskerville family’s curse. Holmes investigates after Dr. Mortimer’s consultation, while Watson revisits the alley and gate and seeks further particulars. Barrymore reveals that Sir Charles had gone to the gate to meet a woman, and a burned letter leads to Mrs. Laura Lyons. Her appointment, made for the same place and hour and later canceled under Stapleton’s influence, becomes a key connection. Holmes and Watson conclude that Sir Charles died of fright and exhaustion after being pursued by the hound. Stapleton had arranged for him to wait at the gate, released the painted hound, and thereby indirectly caused his death; however, Holmes says the evidence would not prove this to a jury.

Narrative, section by section

12 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2.

Sir Charles Baskerville's death occurred after he went out for his customary nightly walk through the yew alley. His body was found at the far end of the alley with no physical injuries, but Dr. James Mortimer observed signs of terror and fresh footprints that he identified as those of a gigantic hound.

Chapter 3.

Sir Charles Baskerville's death is surrounded by unexplained evidence, including enormous marks near his body, reports of a spectral creature, and signs that he waited at the moor-gate before fleeing. Holmes questions whether a crime occurred and how it was committed, while considering both ordinary and supernatural explanations.

Chapter 5.

Sir Charles Baskerville's death remains the central background to the investigation. Holmes considers the death together with the surveillance, the boot incidents, and the other recent mysteries as part of an unusually complex case.

Chapter 6.

Sherlock Holmes asks Dr. Watson to seek fresh particulars concerning the death of Sir Charles Baskerville. Barrymore and his wife say that Sir Charles’s death shocked them and made their surroundings at Baskerville Hall painful.

Chapter 7.

Dr. Watson continues to question the circumstances of Sir Charles Baskerville’s death, including Barrymore’s movements and the possibility that someone pursued Sir Charles. Jack Stapleton suggests that Sir Charles died of fright after seeing a dog because of his weak heart.

A short walk brought us to it, a bleak moorland house, once the

Beryl and Stapleton were deeply shocked by the death of Sir Charles, whom they knew intimately because he often walked across the moor to their house. Beryl says the tragedy strengthened her fear that the Baskerville family's curse had some basis.

Chapter 8.

Watson revisits the yew alley and the moor-gate to reconstruct the fatal night of the old gentleman’s death. He imagines that the man saw something terrifying on the moor, fled down the alley, and died from horror and exhaustion, while still wondering whether a human agency was involved.

Chapter 10.

Sir Charles’s death becomes more mysterious when Barrymore reveals that he had gone to the gate to meet a woman. A burned letter bearing the initials L. L. suggests that locating this woman could provide important facts about the death.

Chapter 11.

Sir Charles Baskerville's death occurred at the same place and hour at which Mrs. Laura Lyons had asked him to meet her. Mrs. Lyons says that she learned of his death in the newspaper the next morning.

“We’re at close grips at last,” said Holmes as we walked together

Sir Charles was found dead without any mark upon him. Holmes and Dr. Watson believe that he died of sheer fright caused by the hound, but Holmes says they cannot prove this explanation to a jury.

Chapter 13.

Holmes explicitly describes the death of Sir Charles Baskerville as a murder investigation. Mrs. Laura Lyons's appointment with Sir Charles, arranged and then canceled under Stapleton's influence, is presented as a significant connection to the crime.

Chapter 15.

Stapleton arranged for Sir Charles to wait near a gate after receiving Mrs. Laura Lyons’s letter. He released the painted hound, which chased Sir Charles down the yew alley, where Sir Charles died from heart disease and terror.
