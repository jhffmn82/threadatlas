Later he gives the horse an easier day, has the travelers walk heavy hills where appropriate, and offers to gather blackberries while they proceed on foot. On their final day, he offers to take them anywhere despite his overnight return to Falmouth, plans the horse’s rest, and parts mournfully while reassuring them that he often makes the journey.

DAY THE SECOND Is there anything more delightful than to start on a smiling morning in a comfortable carriage, with all one's _impedimenta_ (happily not much!

Charles drives the narrator and her two young companions from Falmouth to the Lizard. He is a dependable, courteous Cornishman and a lifelong teetotaller; he tells the narrator that his wife died after their baby died, and he helps collect heaths and point out local features.

"Shall we get a boat?

Charles arranges a boatman, warns that the sea and landing can be dangerous, and attends faithfully to the narrator and companions. He points out the Lizard Lights and later guides the narrator along a narrow path on top of the hedge walls.

] DAY THE THIRD "And a beautiful day it is, ladies, though it won't do for Kynance.

Charles arrives early with a detailed plan for taking the women travelers to Kennack Sands, Poltesco, Cadgwith, and later Mullion. He is attentive to the women's comfort and sightseeing, while also showing concern for his horse and arranging a demanding return journey to Falmouth.

We had just time to dine, repose a little, and communicate our address to our af

Charles arrives punctually, reassures the travelers about his horse, and drives them from the Lizard Downs through Mullion to Mullion Cove. He guides and assists them at the cove, helps the narrator through a sea-cave, and later arranges the carriage and directions back to the farm and Old Inn. He then plans to drive the horse to Falmouth and return when the travelers need him.

DAY THE FIFTH "Hope for the best, and be prepared for the worst," had been the motto of our journey.

Charles appears at the travelers' door soaked but smiling, wearing no hat and carrying sympathy for his ladies. He confirms that he brought a party to the Lizard that day, and that they could not have had a dry thread left on them.

" DAY THE EIGHTH And seven days were all we could allow ourselves at the Lizard, if we meant to see the rest of Cornwall.

Charles arrives as agreed to take charge of the women during their journey back toward civilisation. He arranges the same carriage and horse, proposes routes through Mullion and Gunwalloe, and willingly modifies the plan to include a boat excursion to the sea-caves.

Gratefully we "talked them over," as we drove on through the pretty country roun

Charles shows the travelers the ruins of disused tin mines and explains that Cornwall's mining industry has declined, forcing many former workers to emigrate or starve. He recommends an old Norwegian boatman for their bathing excursion and invites them to row around St. Michael's Mount by moonlight, offering to help them reach the shore.

] DAY THE TENTH I cannot advise Marazion as a bathing place.

Charles drives the travelers through Cornwall and gives his horse a gentle day before their planned visit to the Land's End. He makes them walk up several heavy hills between Penzance and Castle-an-Dinas, then stops where the carriage can go no farther and offers to gather blackberries while they continue on foot.

DAY THE TWELFTH Monday morning.

Charles offers to take the narrator and her companions wherever they wish during their last day, despite needing to drive back to Falmouth during the night. He plans to rest and feed the horse until the middle of the night and says that the moon and the good road between Penzance and Falmouth will help them. He is mournful at parting, but reassures them that he often makes the journey and does not mind it.
