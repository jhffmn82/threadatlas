Women of Troy in The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · -0414

Women of Troy are captive Trojan women, the living dramatic focus of the Troaedes, taken as spoils of war and awaiting Greek masters. Called from their huts by Hecuba, they emerge in fear, mourn Troy, and dread separation, enslavement, and unknown Greek destinations. After lots are cast, they are assigned individually to Greek lords and told to follow Cassandra’s bridal cries. They continue lamenting Troy’s destroyed rites, temples, altars, images, festivals, dead, and children, while grieving their forced departure as slaves and wishing Helen never returns to Sparta.

The conquered women are the living dramatic focus of the Troaedes. Four of them are presented as clearly lit and heroic figures, while others appear in varying grades of characterization as nameless, barely articulate voices of eternal sorrow.

the Phrygian matrons in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

The Phrygian matrons are captive Trojan women and Hecuba’s attendants who form a chorus. They lament Troy’s destruction, their dead sons, husbands, and children, and recall Paris’s voyage and choice of Venus; by the end, they are taken to Greece for servitude and separation.

women of Troy in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

The women of Troy are the city’s inhabitants and defenders, but Theano says they are not trained for battle. After watching Penthesileia, they consider leaving armed, then accept her counsel and remain behind the walls. During the attack they wake terrified, mourn, hide, or rush to aid husbands and children. After Troy’s ruin, the Argives capture and divide them as captive thralls, and they lament enslavement, home, and family.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

But on the grave-mound of Menoetius' son

The women of Troy watch Penthesileia's prowess and, urged by Tisiphone, prepare to leave the city armed for battle. Theano warns that they lack the training of warriors, and they accept her counsel and remain behind the walls.

BOOK XIII

The Trojan women wake amid the attack and cry out in terror, often without time to dress or cover themselves. Some try to hide their nakedness, some tear their hair and beat their breasts, and others rush into danger to aid their husbands or children before being captured or killed.

BOOK XIV.

Troy's women are dragged from the ruined city and divided among the Argive leaders as captives. They lament their enslavement, their lost home, and the children and families left behind.
