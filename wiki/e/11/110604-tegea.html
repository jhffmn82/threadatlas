Tegea in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Tegea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tegea is a district and town, called by poets the lot of Aphidas because Tegea and its neighbouring country were given to Aphidas. It received its name in the days of Tegeates, son of Lycaon. Its people were first distributed among eight parishes; Aphidas became a ninth during the reign of Aphidas. Aleus built the old temple of Athene Alea, made it the seat of his kingdom, and founded the town in its later form. Hippothous subsequently transferred the Arcadian kingdom’s seat from Tegea to Trapezus. The earlier temple of Athene Alea was destroyed by fire and rebuilt; Laodice sent it a robe from Cyprus. Tegea joined the Arcadians in war against Ilium, with the Persians, and at Dipæa, and repeatedly resisted the Lacedæmonians, including victories credited to its armed women and to snow weakening invaders. It dedicated offerings at Delphi from Lacedæmonian spoils and erected a statue of Philopœmen. Its temples, market-place, race-course, games, spring, festival, and statues mark its civic and religious life. Its boundaries and roads connect it with Megalopolis, Pallantium, the Lacedæmonian district, Thyrea, and Argos; the Alpheus marks one boundary and disappears into the plain of Tegea.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

8 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IV.

Tegea and the neighbouring country were given to Aphidas, so poets called Tegea the lot of Aphidas. Aleus later built the old temple to Athene Alea at Tegea and made it the seat of his kingdom.

CHAPTER V.

Hippothous transferred the seat of the Arcadian kingdom from Tegea to Trapezus. Laodice sent a robe from Cyprus to Tegea for Athene Alea, and the Lacedæmonians later invaded the district around Tegea but were defeated by the men of Tegea and by women who took up arms.

CHAPTER XLIV.

Tegea shares a district boundary at the Mound with Megalopolis and Pallantium, and the Manthuric plain lies on its borders about 50 stades from the city. Near the road to Tegea are the temple of Aphneus and the well of Leuconius.

CHAPTER XLV.

Tegea was said to have received its name in the days of Tegeates, son of Lycaon, and its inhabitants were originally distributed among eight parishes, with a ninth, Aphidas, added during the reign of Aphidas. Aleus founded the town in its later form. Its great temple of Athene Alea was rebuilt after the earlier temple was destroyed by fire.

CHAPTER XLVII.

Tegea has temples to Athene Alea, Athene Poliatis, and Artemis, as well as a race-course where the Aleæa and Halotia games are held. It also has a spring associated with the violation of Auge by Hercules and a temple of Hermes Æpytus about three stades from the spring.

CHAPTER XLVIII.

Tegea has a market-place containing temples, statues, pillars, altars, and tombs. Its women are said to have defeated the Lacedæmonians during the first invasion of Charillus.

CHAPTER LIII.

Tegea has four statues of Apollo Aguieus, one erected by each tribe, and temples and altars dedicated to several deities. Its traditions include the annual festival at the place dedicated to Zeus Clarius, where Tegeans are said to have defeated invading Lacedæmonians after snow weakened them.

CHAPTER LIV.

Tegea is bounded from the Lacedæmonian district by the Alpheus. Roads from Tegea lead toward Thyrea and Argos, and the plain of Tegea is one place where the Alpheus disappears into the ground.
