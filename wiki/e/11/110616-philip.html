Philip in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Philip, son of Amyntas, was a king who took control after Antigonus and sought to dominate Greece. He captured most Greek cities, reduced Greece while it was weakened after the Peloponnesian War, and weakened Athens by taking its islands and ending its naval supremacy. Elis joined the Macedonians and later aided his attack on the Lacedæmonians. He married Eurydice and had children, including Demetrius; an account calls Ptolemy his son by Berenice. Violent toward subjects, he poisoned Aratus, Euryclides, and Micon. Athens later honored him with gifts and statues. He died from sorrow after Demetrius was poisoned by Perseus.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

Philip II of Macedon and his son Alexander were represented by statues near those of the Egyptian kings in Athens. The Athenians honored them with gifts and statues, partly from communal flattery.

CHAPTER VI.

Philip took control after Antigonus and murdered Aratus by secretly giving him poison because Aratus criticized his violent conduct toward his subjects. Philip also poisoned the Athenian orators Euryclides and Micon, and later suffered grief when his son Demetrius was poisoned by Perseus.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Philip, the son of Amyntas, sought to dominate Greece. The people of Elis, weakened by internal factions, joined the Macedonians but did not fight against the Greeks at Chæronea; they did participate in Philip’s attack on the Lacedæmonians.

Philip in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Philip, son of Demetrius and a Macedonian king, received rule over the Macedonians from his stepfather Antigonus on coming of age. Closely imitating Philip son of Amyntas, he used bribes, poison, including against Aratus of Sicyon, and garrisons to secure power, raiding Athenians and Ætolians. He ended the Phocian or Sacred War, won at Chæronea, restored the Platæans, and stationed a garrison in Thebes to weaken Theban power. He terrorized Elatea and bought influential support. His power later suffered defeat at Cynoscephalæ by Flaminius and the Romans: most of his army was lost, and peace compelled him to remove garrisons from Greek towns seized in war. He was Andriscus's grandfather.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

Philip, the son of Demetrius, received rule over the Macedonians from his stepfather Antigonus when he came of age. He imitated Philip the son of Amyntas, used bribery and poison, established garrison towns called the keys of Greece, and raided the Athenians and Ætolians.

CHAPTER VIII.

Philip’s garrison held Eretria and Corinth, but he was defeated by Flaminius and the Romans at Cynoscephalæ. He lost most of his army and was required to remove his garrisons from the Greek towns seized during the war.

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I.

After he was victorious at Chæronea, Philip introduced a garrison into Thebes and restored the Platæans as part of his efforts to destroy Theban power.

CHAPTER III.

Philip put an end to the Phocian or Sacred War in its tenth year after the plunder of the temple.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Philip, the son of Demetrius, inspired great terror among the people of Elatea and won over the most influential townsfolk by bribes. Under him, Elatea remained faithful to the Macedonians instead of accepting Titus Flaminius's invitation to revolt.
