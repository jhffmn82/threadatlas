Theagenes of Thasos in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Theagenes of Thasos was an athlete, conventionally identified as Timosthenes’ son, though Thasians said Hercules, disguised as Timosthenes, was his father. Famous from boyhood for carrying off a bronze statue, he gained renowned strength and defeated Euthymus in Olympic boxing. He won at the Pythian, Nemean, Isthmian, and other contests, including a long course at Phthia, totaling 1,400 crowns. After death, statues and honors spread through Greece and among barbarians; he was reputed to cure diseases.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XI.

Theagenes was said by the Thasians to be the son of Hercules disguised as Timosthenes. As a boy, he became famous for carrying away a bronze statue, and he later won numerous athletic victories, including three at the Pythian games, nine at Nemea, ten at the Isthmus, and many others, for a total of 1,400 crowns. After his death, he was honored in various parts of Greece and among the barbarians, and was believed to cure diseases.

CHAPTER VI.

Theagenes of Thasos outboxed Euthymus while attempting to win both the boxing and pancratium prizes in the same Olympiad. Because he was exhausted in the boxing contest, he did not receive the pancratium crown and was fined two talents, one for offending the god and one for injuring Euthymus; he paid the divine fine in the 76th Olympiad and thereafter did not compete again as a boxer.
