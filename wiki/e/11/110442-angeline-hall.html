Abstract written by a model

Angeline Hall, born Chloe Angeline Stickney, was a college-educated woman, teacher, writer, homemaker, mother, and the wife of Asaph Hall. Described as a pioneer among college women, she believed women had the same capacities as men for knowledge, virtue, and truth, supported co-education, and pursued intellectual work through science, philosophy, history, genealogy, poetry, essays, and a planned teaching work on Prometheus. In her college days she wore the bloomer costume and enthusiastically supported abolition of skirts, though she and her husband later quarreled over woman suffrage, poetry, and the value of her work. Her life unfolded through strenuous service to family and others. At Shalersville Institute she taught, learned oil painting from Mary Gilman, and nursed Asaph Hall during a severe fever. During difficult years, including the war, she supported him materially and emotionally, cared for the family, joined him in Washington despite dangers and disease, and successfully advocated for his appointment to the vacant Observatory professorship; she later advocated for George W. Hill. She managed household and family affairs so that her husband could work, while doing cooking, washing, sewing, ironing, cleaning, and other work, commonly with one servant. At 18 Gay Street, where she lived for twenty-five years, she raised four sons, Samuel, Angelo, Percival, and Asaph Hall Jr., and sent them all through Harvard College. She taught them before academic age, demanded truth and obedience, organized their household and yard work, nursed them herself, and trained them in moral principles. Her domestic life also included gardening, concern for poor relatives, care for her mother and sisters, and leadership of the Stickney family after her mother’s death in 1878. Religious faith remained important: she was brought up in her mother’s church, became Unitarian, later withdrew after a church quarrel, and expressed a faith that had outgrown superstition in “To Science.” Late in life she participated in historical and philosophical societies, attended Academy meetings, performed charity work among poor people in Washington and Georgetown, and visited the Home for Destitute Women and Children for her last fourteen years. In declining health, bent from years of toil, she continued to travel, write letters, and visit the Adirondacks. She died of a stroke of apoplexy in her sixty-second year at North Andover, Mass., at Mrs. Berrien’s house.

An Astronomer's Wife

Angeline Hall is the subject of Angelo Hall’s biography and is described as a typical American and a Bachelor of Arts. She had four sons, sent each of them through Harvard College, and was born as Chloe Angeline Stickney.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER X.

Angeline Hall supported Asaph Hall during their difficult years and made plans for her sister Ruth and her husband to move to Michigan. At Shalersville she taught, learned to paint in oil, cared for Asaph during a severe fever, and wrote that he was a noble, true man and that they were very happy. She later returned to Rodman with enough money to repay Joseph Downs, while continuing to suffer from poor health.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XII.

Angeline Hall quarreled with her husband over woman suffrage, her poetry, the value of her work, and whether she should wear the bloomer costume. She eventually devoted herself chiefly to domestic cares, while maintaining close interest in her mother, sisters, aunts, and family genealogy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XIII.

Angeline S. Hall supported Asaph Hall during the war, cared for their family, and joined him in Washington despite the city’s dangers and diseases. She wrote Captain Gillis urging that Hall receive the vacant Observatory position on the basis of his ability, and she later advocated for George W. Hill’s appointment to the corps of Professors of Mathematics, U.S. Navy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XIV.

Angeline Hall lived at 18 Gay Street for twenty-five years and raised her four sons there. She trained them through strict moral instruction, absolute demands for truth and obedience, household work, early teaching, careful nursing, frugality, and devoted affection.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XV.

Angeline Hall believed that women possessed the same capacities as men for knowledge, virtue, and truth, and she lived according to a broad ideal of intellectual, moral, domestic, and social usefulness. She was a pioneer among college women, a devoted wife and mother, an accomplished homemaker, a counselor and leader, a benefactor to poor neighbors, and an enthusiastic supporter of Professor Hall’s astronomical research. She also wrote poetry and essays, studied philosophy and history, researched her family genealogy, and made several trips to the Adirondacks late in life.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XVI.

Angeline Hall writes numerous family letters describing household labor, children, relatives, gardening, travel, health, and education. She manages domestic work in Georgetown and Washington, D.C., travels to visit family and academic acquaintances, and works on a planned Prometheus Unbound while reflecting on philosophy, ethical teaching, and science.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ EPILOGUE.

The mother came from the northern land to college, questioning the future years. Her lamp of knowledge burned through the bitter winter gloom, and she loved and learned with Science.

Abstract written by a model

Chloe Angeline Stickney, later Angeline Hall, was the sixth child of Theophilus Stickney and Electa Cook, born on All Saints’ Day in 1830 in Rodman. Fatherless from birth, she grew up solemn, industrious, and devoted to learning, beginning school at three, working from childhood, and developing ambitions for education and public distinction. She studied at Rodman Union Seminary and, from 1848, spent nine years alternating between teaching and being a pupil. An excellent, patient, firm teacher, she taught in several places, won her pupils’ respect and affection, and seldom used punishment. Poverty, poor health, and religious scruples complicated her determination to attend college. She raised funds through teaching painting and tissue-paper flowers, entered New York Central College, studied mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, Greek, German, and political economy, taught mathematics while enrolled, and graduated with an A.B. in 1855 despite an exhaustion-related breakdown shortly before graduation. At college she embraced woman suffrage and wore the bloomer costume; she also wrote poetry and maintained intense religious commitments. A sincere Baptist from age sixteen, she corresponded with Asaph Hall during their courtship, disclosed her father’s history before their engagement, encouraged his religious preparation, and married him in April 1856. Though often delicate or ill, she supported his scientific career through severe economy, teaching him German, encouraging his studies, and assisting his observatory work. Her religious views broadened, while she continued to seek truth, study nature, and reconcile science with religion. As wife, mother, and bread-giver, she made a home characterized by unselfishness, charity, justice, hospitality, and tender care, raising four sons. She welcomed poor and despised people without condescension, resisted false social distinctions, and avoided causing pain through wit. Her life ended after a silent decline; she died quietly, painlessly, and without complaint.

Angeline Hall supported her husband’s scientific career despite poor health and urged him to continue his studies. She economized extensively, taught him German, awakened him for observatory work, encouraged his ambitions, and gradually experienced improved health and broader religious views.
