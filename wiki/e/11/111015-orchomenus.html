Orchomenus

place · 1 work · 26 quoted facts · 15 connections
Also called: Orchomenian, Orchomenian district, Orchomenians, old town of Orchomenus, town, town of Orchomenus

Abstract written by a model

Orchomenus is a Greek city and kingdom of the Minyae, beyond Laphystium. It is approached from Mantinea by two roads: one through Ladas’ course and several named sights, and another by Mount Anchisia along the boundary with Mantinea. Its old town stood on a hilltop, with ruined walls and marketplace; the later town lay inside the old circuit. Its territory includes a small marshy plain, routes toward Caphya, Stymphelus, and Pheneus, and connections to Caphyæ by a ravine road and a road beside the lake; its water is stored in a reservoir in Caphyæ’s plain. The city’s notable places include a well, temples of Poseidon, Aphrodite, Dionysus, and the ancient Graces, a cedar-tree image of Artemis, sacred meteoric stones, stone statues, the treasury and tomb of Minyas, and the tomb of Hesiod. Its people annually honor Actaeon and recovered Hesiod’s bones from Naupactus after consulting Delphi. Its history begins with Andreus as first inhabitant. Once famous, renowned, prosperous, and wealthy even during the Trojan War, it later faced an end likened to Mycenæ and Delos. After the posterity of Almus ended childless, the kingdom passed to Clymenus; its power declined through the deity, and Philip son of Amyntas restored it to the Orchomenians after the Thebans had driven them out. In the narrator’s day it had only moderate wealth.

Orchomenus is in one work on this site, Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Orchomenus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XII.

Two roads lead from Mantinea to Orchomenus. One passes Ladas' course, a temple of Artemis, the alleged tomb of Penelope, the well of Alalcomenea, and the ruins of Mæra; another passes Mount Anchisia, whose district boundary separates Orchomenus from Mantinea.

CHAPTER XIII.

Orchomenus had an old town on a hill, with surviving ruins of its walls and marketplace, while the contemporary town lay within the old circuit. It had a well, temples of Poseidon and Aphrodite with stone statues, and a wooden statue of Artemis in a cedar tree. Its territory included a small marshy plain and roads leading toward Caphya, Stymphelus, and Pheneus.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Orchomenus is connected to Caphyæ by a straight road through a ravine and by another road on the left of the lake. Water from its territory is held in a reservoir in the plain of Caphyæ.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Orchomenus is described as having been as famous and renowned as any Greek city. After reaching the height of prosperity, it was destined to meet an end similar to that of Mycenæ and Delos.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

Orchomenus had no child, so the kingdom devolved upon Clymenus, the son of Presbon. Its power was later reduced by the deity, and it was restored to the Orchomenians by Philip the son of Amyntas after they had been driven out by the Thebans.

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

Orchomenus has a temple of Dionysus, an ancient temple of the Graces, sacred meteoric stones, a well, the treasury of Minyas, and the tombs of Minyas and Hesiod. Its people recovered Hesiod’s bones from Naupactus after consulting Delphi, annually offer funeral rites to Actaeon, and say that Aspledon lost its inhabitants through lack of water.
