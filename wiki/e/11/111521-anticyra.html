Anticyra

place · 1 work · 7 quoted facts · 4 connections

Abstract written by a model

Anticyra, formerly Cyparissus and named for Anticyreus, was an ancient town below Medeon, renowned for hellebore on rocky hills. Its people were expelled first by Philip son of Amyntas and later by the Roman Otilius for backing Philip son of Demetrius; after being taken, it was razed. It had market-place statues, Poseidon’s temple, two gymnasiums, a conduit, and a tomb, and lay about 100 stades from Bulis amid uncertain mountain-road access.

Anticyra is in one work on this site, Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXVI.

Anticyra was formerly called Cyparissus and was said to have received its later name from Anticyreus, a contemporary of Hercules. It lies below the ruins of Medeon, and its people were expelled first by Philip son of Amyntas and later by the Roman Otilius for supporting Philip son of Demetrius, king of the Macedonians. Anticyra has brazen statues in its market-place, a small temple of Poseidon near the harbour, two gymnasiums, a conduit, and a tomb said to belong to the sons of Iphitus.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

Anticyra is about 100 stades from the port near Bulis. The text is uncertain whether a mainland road connects Anticyra with Bulis because of the precipitous mountains between them.
