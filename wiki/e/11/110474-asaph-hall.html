  • In An Astronomer's Wife: Asaph Hall 3rd, born October 15, 1829, was the eldest of Hannah (Palmer) Hall’s six children and descended from John Hall of Wallingford, Connecticut. Raised by his widowed mother, he helped his family redeem a mortgaged farm and took part in producing and selling cheese. After a three-year apprenticeship with Herrick and Dunbar and five years as a journeyman, he became a skilled, conscientious carpenter, building houses and applying projection principles to roof construction. At about twenty-four or twenty-five he began serious study, learning geometry and algebra with Mr. Rice and developing into an admired scholar and mathematician. He sought wider education at McGrawville and Ann Arbor, where Brünnow encouraged him and he studied astronomy and French without tuition. He married Angeline Stickney, studied with and taught alongside her at Shalersville Institute, and joined and was baptized among the Campbellites before leaving Ohio. In 1857 he went to Cambridge with fifty dollars, supported himself through carpentry and low-paid work at Harvard College Observatory, and added income through moon-culminations, almanac computations, and astronomical problem-solving. His computed comet orbit appeared in the Astronomical Journal, and he gained the respect of George Bond and other scholars. In 1862 he entered the U.S. Naval Observatory as an Aid, became a professor in 1863, observed Mars, served during the threat to Washington, and thereafter devoted himself to astronomy and permanent government service. He and Angeline had Samuel, Angelo, Percival, and Asaph Jr. The records also portray him as mechanically skilled, ambitious, attentive to his wife, outside religious sects at one stage but later a Campbellite, and opposed to woman suffrage, inferior poetry, and the bloomer costume.

Asaph Hall is introduced as a carpenter who later became an astronomer and as a descendant of a distinguished family. His inheritance slipped away after he was set up in business, and he died in Georgia in 1842 while selling clocks manufactured in his Goshen factory.

opening

Asaph Hall 3rd was born on October 15, 1829, and was the eldest of six children. After helping his widowed mother redeem a mortgaged farm, he apprenticed himself to carpenters at sixteen and worked eight years in carpentry. He later studied mathematics, distinguished himself at McGrawville, and was recognized by Brünnow and Benjamin Apthorp Gould as a promising scientific student.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER IX.

Asaph Hall courted Angeline Stickney after first encountering her as his geometry teacher and eventually replacing mischief with admiration. He accepted the risks associated with her family history and poor health, exchanged loving letters with her, pursued studies in Ann Arbor after their marriage, and carried his frail wife across fields to gather wild flowers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER X.

Asaph Hall made a favorable impression at Ann Arbor, leading Professor Brünnow to urge him to continue his studies and arrange for him to attend college without tuition fees. He taught at the Shalersville Institute, made a water-filled prism, held Saturday talks for boys, and concluded that he preferred carpentry to teaching. Before leaving Ohio, he joined the Campbellites and was baptized.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XI.

Asaph Hall went to Cambridge in 1857 with fifty dollars and began working at the Harvard College Observatory for three dollars a week, later receiving four dollars and then four hundred dollars a year. He supplemented his income by observing moon-culminations, computing almanacs, and solving astronomical problems, eventually earning the respect of George Bond and other scholars.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XII.

Asaph Hall opposed woman suffrage, discouraged Angeline Hall from writing poetry that he considered inferior, and objected to her adopting the bloomer costume because he feared it would harm their prospects. He later concentrated on astronomy and was described as a thoughtful husband who once extinguished a blazing lamp with his observing cap.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XIII.

Asaph Hall went to Washington in 1862, passed an examination, and became an Aid at the U.S. Naval Observatory. He observed Mars, searched for wounded friends, suffered a prolonged illness, became a professor in 1863, and helped man an intrenchment near Fort Lincoln during Jubal A. Early’s threat to Washington. He later showed Abraham Lincoln objects through the telescope and devoted the rest of his life to science.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XVI.

Asaph Hall receives letters from Angeline about their children, relatives, household matters, and travel. He is described as an astronomer whose work takes him to the Observatory and whose wife mends for him; Angeline also advises that Asaph Jr. should not go to Pulkowa.
