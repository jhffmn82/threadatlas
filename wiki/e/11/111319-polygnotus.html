Polygnotus

person · 1 work · 24 quoted facts · 12 connections
Also called: Polygnotus of Thasos, Polygnotus', the Thasian painter

Abstract written by a model

Polygnotus is a Thasian painter, son of Aglaophon, identified by Simonides as the artist of scenes from the capture of Ilium. His painting in The Lounge ranges from Troy’s destruction and the Greeks’ return to Odysseus’ descent to Hades. It depicts Epeus breaking Troy’s walls, the Wooden Horse, Ajax’s oath concerning Cassandra, Neoptolemus killing Trojan men, Phrontis, wounded warriors, and many named figures. On the left, it shows Acheron, Charon, punishment and honor in Hades, Theseus and Pirithous, Eurynomus, and Ocnus making rope while a she-ass consumes it, apparently satirizing Ocnus’ wife. Further figures include the daughters of Pandareus, Antilochus, Agamemnon, Achilles, Patroclus, Phocus, Iaseus, Actæon, Orpheus, Thamyris, and Marsyas. Polygnotus appears to draw on Homer, Lescheos, the Minyad, and Archilochus, while also adding or inventing names and details not found in those accounts. The painting culminates in a deliberate-looking gathering of enemies of Odysseus and a depiction of Tantalus following Archilochus.

Polygnotus is in one work on this site, Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Polygnotus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Polygnotus first appears

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXV.

Polygnotus painted scenes of the capture of Ilium and the Greeks’ return in The Lounge. He depicted Phrontis accurately according to Homer and represented several wounded warriors in accordance with Lescheos’s poem, suggesting that he had read it; the narrator thinks he invented the other names in the group.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Polygnotus painted scenes of Troy’s destruction, including Epeus knocking down the walls, the Wooden Horse, Ajax’s oath concerning the violation of Cassandra, and Neoptolemus killing Trojan men. He is said to have invented the names Metioche, Pisis, and Cleodice, since they are not mentioned by the cited poets.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Polygnotus of Thasos, the son of Aglaophon, is identified by Simonides as the painter of the depicted incidents in the capture of Ilium.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Polygnotus painted the left part of the painting with Odysseus’ descent to Hades, Acheron, Charon, and several figures receiving punishment or honor. He followed the Minyad’s description of Theseus and Pirithous entering Charon’s boat and included Eurynomus despite that figure not appearing in the cited poems.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Polygnotus represents Ocnus making rope with a she-ass consuming it, apparently as a skit on Ocnus’ wife. His picture also includes Ariadne, Phædra, and the other figures described in this unit.

CHAPTER XXX.

Polygnotus painted the daughters of Pandareus, Phocus, Iaseus, Actæon, Orpheus, Thamyris, Marsyas, and many other figures in the scene. He depicted Orpheus in Greek dress beside a willow, Thamyris blind and dejected with a broken lyre, and Marsyas seated near Olympus, who is learning to play the pipe.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Polygnotus painted the scene and seems to have deliberately gathered together enemies of Odysseus. He followed the account of Archilochus in depicting Tantalus, though it is uncertain whether Archilochus invented the additional detail of the overhanging stone.
