Lilæa in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lilæa is an ancient town in Phocis, below Parnassus on the route from Delphi, a winter day’s journey from Delphi and about 60 stades from Amphiclea. Named, by local tradition, for Lilæa the Naiad, it holds that Castalia is supplied by Cephisus. Its air is salubrious in spring, summer, and autumn, but cold in winter. Once taken and razed, it was restored, then besieged by Philip the son of Demetrius. After Patron led younger citizens in revolt, the Macedonian garrison left; Lilæa honored him with a statue at Delphi. It has a theatre, market-place, baths, and temples to Apollo and Artemis.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Lilæa is a winter day's journey from Delphi and about 60 stades from Amphiclea. After being restored, it was besieged by Philip the son of Demetrius, but Patron led the younger citizens against the Macedonian garrison and forced it to leave; Lilæa honored Patron with a statue at Delphi. The town had a theatre, market-place, baths, and temples to Apollo and Artemis, and was said to be named after Lilæa, one of the Naiades and a daughter of the Cephisus.
