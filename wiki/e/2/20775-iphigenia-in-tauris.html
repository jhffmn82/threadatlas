Iphigenia in Tauris in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · -0413/-0411

Euripides’ Iphigenia in Tauris is presented as a romantic play of threatened human sacrifice, exile, deception, recognition, and release rather than a true tragedy. Iphigenia, Agamemnon’s eldest daughter, tells how Artemis saved her from sacrifice at Aulis and made her priestess among the Tauri, whose custom is to kill foreign strangers at the goddess’s altar. Haunted by homesickness and grief, she dreams that her brother Orestes has died and makes funeral offerings for him. Orestes, pursued by Furies after killing Clytemnestra, arrives from Argos with Pylades under Apollo’s command to take Artemis’s divine image to Greece. After Orestes suffers madness and fights the Taurians, the two men are captured. Iphigenia questions them, learning of Troy’s fall, Agamemnon’s and Clytemnestra’s deaths, and Orestes’s misery. She proposes saving Pylades to carry a tablet and message to Argos, while Orestes accepts death. As she dictates the message, he recognizes her as his supposedly dead sister; they prove their identities through family memories and rejoice, though they remain endangered. Iphigenia then plans their escape. She tells King Thoas that Orestes, Pylades, and the image need purification at the sea, and the captive Greek women promise silence. She leads the men and image away, frees Orestes, and reaches a Greek ship. When Thoas orders pursuit, Athena intervenes: Orestes must bring Iphigenia and the image to Greece. The image is assigned a temple at Halae, Iphigenia an office at Brauron, and Thoas frees the women and permits the Greeks to depart.

PREFACE

The preface presents Euripides’ Iphigenia in Tauris as a romantic play based on a ritual in which a human victim approaches death but is released. It recounts how Artemis saved Iphigenia from sacrifice at Aulis and made her priestess among the Tauri, where she later encounters her brother Orestes, who has come with Pylades to take Artemis’s image to Attica. The preface examines Iphigenia’s grief, anger, homesickness, and eventual decision to save the Greek strangers, as well as the play’s themes of ritual, exile, deception, and recognition. It praises Euripides’ dramatic skill while distinguishing the play’s happy ending from the structure of a true tragedy.

CHARACTERS OF THE PLAY

The play concerns Iphigenia, Orestes, Pylades, Thoas, and a Chorus of Captive Greek Women. Iphigenia is Agamemnon’s eldest daughter and was supposedly sacrificed by him to Artemis at Aulis, while Orestes is pursued by Furies for killing Clytemnestra, who had murdered Agamemnon. The play was first performed between 414 and 412 B.C.

Iphigenia in Tauris

Iphigenia explains that Artemis rescued her from sacrifice at Aulis and made her priestess in Tauris, where strangers are killed at the goddess's altar. She dreams that her brother Orestes has died and performs funeral offerings for him. Orestes and Pylades arrive from Argos to steal the divine image of Artemis, but they are captured after Orestes suffers a fit of madness and fights the Taurians. A herdsman brings news of their capture, and Iphigenia prepares to perform the sacrifice, while the Chorus of Greek women mourns their exile and the ruin of Argos.

Iphigenia questions the bound Orestes and Pylades about Greece and learns that Troy has fallen, Agamemnon and Clytemnestra are dead, and Orestes lives in misery. She agrees to save Pylades by sending a tablet and a message to a friend in Argos, while Orestes and Pylades prepare to face the sacrificial rite. When Iphigenia dictates the message, Orestes recognizes her as his supposedly dead sister, and they confirm their identities through memories of their childhood and family. Reunited, they rejoice but remain trapped in Tauris, and Iphigenia searches for a way to help them escape to Argos before the executioners arrive.

LEADER.

Iphigenia learns that Orestes, pursued by the Furies and suffering from madness, has come with Pylades to take the image of Artemis to Greece as Apollo commanded. She devises a plan to pretend that the men and the image require purification at the sea, securing King Thoas's cooperation and arranging the strangers' passage toward the ship. The Greek women promise to keep the escape secret, while Iphigenia leads Orestes, Pylades, and the image out of the temple under the pretext of a sacred cleansing.

CHORUS. [STROPHE.]

The Messenger reports to Thoas that Iphigenia deceived the Tauric guards, freed Orestes, and carried the image of Artemis onto a Greek ship. Thoas orders pursuit, but Athena appears and commands him to stop, declaring that Orestes must take Iphigenia and the image to Greece. Athena assigns the image a temple at Halae, Iphigenia a sacred office at Brauron, and prescribes a blood rite in memory of the old religion. Thoas accepts Athena’s command, frees the women, and allows the Greeks to depart.

Iphigenia in Tauris in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · -0413/-0411

Iphigenia in Tauris is a romantic play set in Tauris, based on a human-sacrifice rite. Beginning in a tragic atmosphere of exile and sadness, it moves through peril, reflection, recognition, and Iphigenia and Orestes's escape to a happy ending.

PREFACE

Iphigenia in Tauris is described as a romantic play rather than a modern tragedy. It begins in a tragic atmosphere and proceeds through danger, escape, recognition, and a happy ending, while remaining marked by exile, homesickness, reflection, and sadness.
