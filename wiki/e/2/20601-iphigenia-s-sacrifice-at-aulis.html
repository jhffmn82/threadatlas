the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · -0413/-0411

The sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis is a human-sacrifice ritual ordered by Artemis to end the Greek fleet’s immobility during the Trojan expedition. Calchas declares that Agamemnon must sacrifice his daughter, whom Agamemnon promises to Artemis and brings to Aulis by falsely claiming she will marry Achilles. At the altar, she is believed to be killed by her father. At the last moment, however, Artemis prevents the sacrifice, removes Iphigenia, and substitutes a deer or fawn, leaving Agamemnon believing his sword struck her.

Iphigenia’s sacrifice at Aulis in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Iphigenia’s sacrifice at Aulis is an intended human offering to Diana, required by Calchas’s prophecy or oracle so that the Greek fleet may sail, the expedition may proceed to Troy and capture it, and Greece may gain freedom from barbarian rule. Agamemnon, compelled by this necessity, initially agrees and summons Iphigenia to Aulis under the false pretense of a marriage to Achilles. He later regrets the decision and tries to cancel it, while Menelaus wants it carried out. Clytemnestra, Iphigenia, and Achilles oppose the plan; Achilles says he will prevent it if possible, although many Greeks support it. Iphigenia is brought to the altar of Diana amid wedding-like preparations, including baths, hair, songs, garlands, baskets, purifying cakes, and lustral water. After pleading and lamentation, she accepts the offering willingly for Greece and calls for the sacrificial rites. At the altar, Agamemnon believes he has sacrificed her, and the sound of the blow is heard. Yet Iphigenia is no longer present: Diana substitutes a stag and carries Iphigenia away. Thus the rite is completed through the stag, while later accounts treat Iphigenia’s death as only supposed and recall her near-sacrifice at Aulis rather than an accomplished death.
