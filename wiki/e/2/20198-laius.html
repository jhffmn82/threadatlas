Abstract written by a model

In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Laius is king of the Cadmeans and Oedipus’s father who fathers and exposes Oedipus despite Apollo’s warning, then is unknowingly killed by his grown son. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Laius is a descendant of Cadmus, son of Labdacus, former king of Thebes, and Jocasta’s husband; he is Oedipus’s biological father, is killed by Oedipus at a crossroads in Phocis, and his unsolved murder brings pollution and plague to Thebes. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Laius is Jocasta’s husband and Oedipus’s father who exposes Oedipus after Apollo’s warning and is killed by him on a road in Phocis. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Laius is king of Thebes and Jocasta’s husband who exposes Oedipus after an oracle’s warning and is later unknowingly killed by his son on a road in Phocis. In The Library, Laius is the son of Labdacus who became king of Thebes, married Menoeceus’s daughter, carried off Chrysippus, and was killed by Oedipus in Phocis despite an oracle. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Laius is the son of Labdacus who was restored to the kingdom, exposed Oedipus after an oracle’s warning, and was killed by him; he also had sons by mistresses and was said to father the Sphinx. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Laius is king of Thebes who orders his newborn son Oedipus destroyed after a warning, but is unknowingly killed by him while traveling toward Delphi. In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Laius is the former king of Thebes and Jocasta’s husband who is murdered by Oedipus at a crossroads, leaving pollution on Thebes.

Laius in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where Laius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Laius, king of the Cadmeans and father of Oedipus, is warned by Apollo through the oracle of Delphi to remain childless to protect his house and land. He fathers Oedipus anyway, exposes the child on Mount Cithaeron, and is later killed unknowingly by his grown son.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Laius is warned by Apollo that having children will bring ruin on his house. After a child is born despite the warning, Laius exposes him on Mount Cithaeron, and later is killed unknowingly by his grown son, Oedipus.

Enter on one side THE SPY; on the other ETEOCLES and the SIX

Laius received Apollo's warning to die childless because only then could the land's welfare be secured. He resisted the warning, fathered Oedipus, and was later killed by him.

Laius in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · 449 to 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Laius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Laius is a descendant of Cadmus, son of Labdacus, former king of Thebes, and Jocasta’s husband. He is the biological father of Oedipus and of an infant son abandoned after being given to the Theban Shepherd. While traveling to Delphi with five companions in a chariot, he is killed at a crossroads in Phocis by Oedipus shortly before Oedipus becomes king. His unsolved murder brings pollution and plague to Thebes, leading Apollo’s command that the killer be found and punished.

Laius in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Laius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Laius, son of Labdacus, was left a one-year-old child, ruled over by Lycus, and expelled from Thebes by Zethus and Amphion. In the Peloponnese Pelops received him; he carried off Chrysippus. After Amphion died, Laius became king of Thebes and married Menoeceus’s daughter. Despite an oracle, he fathered Oedipus, who killed him in Phocis.

Laius in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Laius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Laius, son of Labdacus, was entrusted to Lycus after his father died and later restored to the kingdom. Warned that a son by Jocasta would kill him, he exposed Oedipus, who nevertheless killed him. He also had sons by mistresses and was said to father the Sphinx.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

Laius was the son of Labdacus and was entrusted to Lycus after Labdacus died. He was restored to the kingdom after the family of Amphion and Zethus was destroyed, but the oracle at Delphi warned that a son born to him by Jocasta would kill him. Laius therefore exposed Oedipus, though Oedipus eventually killed him.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Laius is said to have been the father of the Sphinx and to have told her the oracle given to Cadmus. He had sons by mistresses, while the oracle at Delphi referred only to his wife Epicaste and the male children born to her.

Laius in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912 · the work’s page · read it where Laius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Laius is the former king of Thebes, Jocasta’s husband, and Oedipus’s biological father. An oracle foretold that his child would kill him, so the infant was exposed and given away. Laius later traveled toward Delphi and was murdered at the crossroads where roads from Delphi and Daulis meet, initially said to be by robbers. His death left pollution on Thebes, prompting Oedipus to seek vengeance. The Herdsman’s testimony and Oedipus’s acknowledgment reveal that Oedipus killed Laius, fulfilling the prophecy.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

OEDIPUS THE KING

Laius was the former sovereign of Thebes and was murdered while traveling toward Delphi. His death has left a pollution on the land, and Oedipus undertakes to avenge him.

CREON.

Laius was said to have been murdered by robbers at a place where roads from Delphi and Daulis meet. Jocasta describes him as tall, lightly silver-haired, and similar in form to Oedipus; Oedipus suspects that the man he killed at the crossroads was Laius.

[Enter JOCASTA.]

Laius is identified as the former king whose household included the Herdsman and whose child was given away. The Herdsman confirms that the infant was Laius’s own son and that the child was exposed because of a prophecy that he would kill Laius.

[Enter SECOND MESSENGER.]

Laius is identified as Oedipus’s father and Jocasta’s former husband. Oedipus acknowledges that he murdered Laius.
