  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Thebes is both a wealthy hundred-gated Egyptian city and a Boeotian city involved in tribute, dynastic rule, resistance, conquest, and political struggles.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Thebes is the Boeotian guard and bulwark whose Cadmea is seized and recovered, and whose resistance to Macedon ends in its destruction before Cassander rebuilds it.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books: kingdom of Thebes.
  • In The Bacchae of Euripides, Thebes is Cadmus’s city and Semele’s birthplace, where Dionysus seeks recognition and Pentheus’s opposition leads to the destruction of the royal house.
  • In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Thebes is the seven-gated city where Dionysus establishes worship, Pentheus resists him, and the royal house is destroyed after the city’s women are driven into Bacchic frenzy.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Thebes is Cadmus’s city, where Lycus seizes power after killing Creon, rightful rule is restored, and Hercules is eventually excluded and led away toward Athens.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Thebes is Cadmus’s seven-gated city, enduring siege, pollution, the ruin of Oedipus’s house, and Pentheus’s destruction through resistance to Bacchus.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I: the city of Thebes.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Thebes is the ancestral city of Oedipus, Creon, and Antigone, suffering plague, pollution, civil conflict, war, and repeated appeals for healing.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Thebes is the city threatened by seven champions, defended by Eteocles and his chosen men, and ultimately kept secure while burial is granted to Eteocles but denied to Polynices.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Cadmus’ city is besieged by Argives seeking to raze and control it, while its defenders prepare the gates and walls against sack and enslavement.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Thebes is a seven-gated divine city, Pindar’s native state and mother, associated with Kadmos, Heracles, Dionysos, and celebrated victories.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Thebes is a seven-gated city of lovely dances where Amphitryon and Alcmena live, and whose cycle includes Argive battles, Oedipus, Polynices, and the Epigoni’s sack.
  • In The Library, Thebes is founded by Cadmus, ruled by successive dynasties, afflicted by the Sphinx, and subjected to war, capture, abandonment, and Argive takeover.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Thebes is the Boeotian city founded by Cadmus, whose walls, rulers, rites, disasters, and inhabitants are shaped by the fates of Cadmus’s descendants and their enemies.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Thebes is a seven-gated city marked by plague, self-sacrifice, civil warfare, and the foretold deaths of two brothers.
  • In The Library, Thebes is Cadmus’s settlement, named for Thebe, which endures monsters, tribute, dynastic changes, Dionysian frenzy, siege, the deaths of Eteocles and Polynices, and eventual destruction by the Argives.
  • In The Library: kingdom of Thebes.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Thebes is a Boeotian city with a long history of conflict, decline, destruction, restoration, and numerous temples, monuments, tombs, and traditions.
  • In Description of Greece, Thebes is a place ruled by Lycus and Creon, involved in conflicts over Antiope and burial, resistance to the Lacedaemonians, revolt against Ptolemy, and severe punishment.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II: Thebais.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Thebes is Cadmus’s city, later afflicted by the Sphinx, famine, and pestilence, and involved in Bacchic worship and the conflict between Eteocles and Polynices.
  • In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Thebes is a plague-stricken seven-gated state whose safety and sovereignty depend on Oedipus, and which survives siege before suffering further pollution.

Thebes in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Thebes is a Boeotian and Greek city, described as the guard and bulwark of Boeotia, with the Cadmea as its citadel. Its history repeatedly centers on control of that stronghold and resistance to outside power. The Lacedaemonians seized the Cadmea and subjected the city, but, after exiles returned with Athenian help, the people rose, supported the siege, and recovered it. Aided by Athenian soldiers, Thebes defended Boeotia against Agesilaus and forced his retreat. Under Epaminondas, it opposed a general Greek decree, sought command on land, retained sole jurisdiction over Boeotia, and campaigned against the Lacedaemonians; it also assisted against the Phocians and sent Pammenes to aid Artabazus. Philip later placed a garrison there. The decisive crisis came when Thebes planned to expel the Macedonian garrison, rejected Alexander's command and sovereignty, and prepared resistance. Alexander advanced, besieged, stormed, plundered, and razed the city; inhabitants were killed or enslaved, and its territory was divided among neighboring Boeotians. Later Cassander rebuilt Thebes with broad assistance and strengthened its garrison.

Thebes is a city, presented both as a great city of Egypt, called Heliopolis and also Diospolis by the Egyptians, and as Thebes in Boeotia. Its origin is uncertain: it is said to have been built by Osiris, though its first founder is disputed; Greek tradition is said falsely to have made it Osiris’s birthplace. Near it, Egyptians report mice spontaneously forming from soil. At its height it had a circuit of about one hundred and forty furlongs, public buildings, temples, donations, tall private houses, royal monuments of gold, silver, ivory, colossi, and obelisks, and a reputation for wealth, learning, astronomy, one hundred gates, and twenty thousand war chariots. Once a royal center, it declined in magnificence after the court moved to Memphis. In later accounts it suffers tribute under Erginus while Creon rules; Hercules defeats Erginus and receives Megara and charge of the city. It is then ruled by Laius, Oedipus, and his sons, resists one expedition, and is later captured and razed by the Epigoni. It shelters allies and fugitives after a battle, is attacked and forced to surrender supporters of Persia, becomes the Lacedaemonian center over Boeotia, withstands Myronides, and finally receives Athenian exiles and secretly aids Thrasybulus.

Thebes is a city, called first among cities, whose safety and greatness are presented as the chief concern of rule. It is the native and ancestral home of Oedipus, Creon, and Antigone, and is associated with sacred shrines, altars, towers, and its people. It first suffers under the Sphinx, but Oedipus solves the riddle, saves and protects the city, and rules it prosperously. Oedipus seeks to purge the pollution, but the discovery of his crimes leads to his banishment from Thebes. The city is also bound to the consequences of his fate: it seeks his death in a foreign land, believing this will determine a war's outcome, while Oedipus refuses to return because his presence would serve Theban interests. The city defeats the assault and is celebrated as victorious and restored to peace, with Creon credited with saving it. Yet Creon's punishment of Antigone divides him from Thebes, whose people mourn her. Polynices' unburied corpse then pollutes the city when animals carry his remains to its altars, provoking failed rites and hostile leagues. Thebes endures violent woe but is invoked as a city upheld and needing healing.

Thebes is a seven-gated, divine city associated with Kadmos and the people of Kadmos. In myth it receives Zeus at Amphitryon’s doors, gives birth to Dionysos, and is the land of birth of Heracles through Alkmene; it nurtures Herakles and Iphikles by Dirke’s waters. Its remembered glory includes Teiresias’s wise counsels, Iolaos’s timely deed, the Spartoi, Adrastos’s defeat and return to Argos, and the establishment of a Dorian colony of the men of Lakedaimon. Thebes in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Thebes is a city and town in Bœotia, bounded from the district of Platæa by the Asopus and approached by roads from Platæa, Potniæ, and Chalcis. First inhabited by the Ectenes under Ogygus, it was repeopled by the Hyantes and Aones after the Ectenes died out. Cadmus established Cadmea; Amphion and Zethus joined the lower town to it, named the city for Thebe, and gave it seven-gated walls. Cadmea later became its citadel. Its history is marked by repeated conflict, power, and decline. It faced attacks by Erginus, Argives, and the Epigoni, and endured dynastic conflict. It was formerly at the head of Greece and secured Greece's freedom and autonomy through its arms, but was governed by an oligarchy during the Persian invasion. After Chæronea, Philip placed a garrison there; after his death it was expelled, and warnings of ruin followed. Alexander attacked and laid Thebes waste, while Cassander later restored it and rebuilt its old walls. It nevertheless grew progressively weaker. In the Roman advance, after joining the Achæans in war, its inhabitants abandoned it; Pytheas was captured and executed, while Metellus spared its temples, buildings, and fugitives. Thebes preserves a dense landscape of gates, temples, monuments, statues, tombs, and traditions: Cadmus's settlement site, sanctuaries of Dionysus, Demeter, Ammon, Fortune, Aphrodite, and Hercules, tombs of Menœceus and Melanippus, sites tied to Oedipus, Antigone, Amphion, Semele, and others, and annual rites and funeral sacrifices. In the author's day its lower part was deserted except for temples, and only the citadel and a few inhabitants remained; the inhabited citadel was then called Thebes rather than Cadmea.

Thebes in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Thebes is a city founded by Cadmus, who built it where Apollo's oracle said a cow stopped, with five earth-born survivors; he and Harmonia later left when it became odious to them. It was afflicted by the Sphinx, then famine and pestilence after OEdipus became king. Native to Bacchus, it received his followers despite Pentheus. Under Eteocles and Polynices' disputed alternating rule, it withstood the Seven against Thebes, whose army fled.

Thebes in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Thebes is a city and state, a seven-gated city associated with its royal household, elders, chariots, gods, and Dionysus as a favored home. Under Oedipus after Laius’s death, it founders in plague, bloodshed, failed crops and herds, barren childbirth, and death. Apollo’s command makes its salvation depend on finding Laius’s murderer and purging the resulting pollution. Oedipus, once honored as its savior and king, is revealed through the royal household’s discoveries to be unclean; he asks exile from the city of his sires. The State is said unknowingly to have bound him in an impious marriage and thereby undone him. Later Thebes seeks to keep or recover Oedipus because oracles connect its sovereignty and safety to him: the location of his tomb determines whether a curse falls on it, and his corpse may threaten it if it is hostile to the land sheltering him. Ruled through a disputed succession by Oedipus’s sons, it is besieged by Polyneices’ expedition and seven commanders. Oedipus foretells mutual bloodshed between the sons; Thebes survives the Argive attack and celebrates its defense. Finally, it is polluted by Polyneices’ exposed flesh being eaten by dogs and crows, and the Chorus calls on Dionysus to save it again.
