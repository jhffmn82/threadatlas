Abstract written by a model

In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Danaus leads his daughters from Egypt to Argos, advocates for their protection, reports the pursuing fleet, and counsels them to honor Argos and preserve their chastity. In Heroides and Amores, Danaus orders his daughters to kill their husbands, counts the dead, and violently imprisons Hypermestra when Lynceus survives. In The Library, Danaus flees with his fifty daughters to Argos, gains its kingdom, permits their marriages to the sons of Egyptus, and later gives his daughters to athletic-contest victors. In The Library, Danaus flees Egyptus’s sons by ship, receives the kingdom of Argos from Gelanor, names its inhabitants Danai, and directs his daughters’ marriages and remarriages. In Description of Greece, Danaus wins Argos from Gelanor, builds temples to Lycian Apollo and Athene, and tries his daughter Hypermnestra for sparing Lynceus.

Danaus in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Danaus is the son of Belus, descended through Io, Epaphus, and Libya, and the father, guide, and advocate of the Danaids. He leads his daughters from Egypt to Argos to escape the marriages sought by the sons of Aegyptus. At Argos, he directs them to seek sanctuary at the altars, display their suppliant wands, and speak modestly and submissively as an armed host approaches. He appeals for protection, requests guidance through the city, and is received as an honored elder and advocate. After the Argive vote secures protection, he reports the pursuing fleet, obtains armed aid, and counsels his daughters to honor Argos, preserve their reputation, and value chastity.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Danaus accompanies his daughters, the Danaids, when they flee Egypt and arrive at Argos seeking sanctuary from their pursuing kinsmen and suitors.

THE SUPPLIANT MAIDENS / DEDICATION

Danaus descends from Io through Epaphus, Libya, and Belus. He is the father of the daughters who flee with him to Argos to escape the marriage sought by the sons of Aegyptus.

The Suppliant Maidens

Danaus leads and advises his daughters after their flight from Egypt. He sees dust, wheels, and an approaching armed host, and tells the daughters to cling to the altar, hold their suppliant wands, and speak modestly and submissively.

Enter the KING OF ARGOS.

Danaus is the father of the Danaids and asks Pelasgus for attendants to guide him safely through the city. He is instructed to lay the branches of suppliance on the altars of the country’s gods so that the people will know of the Danaids’ appeal.

Re-enter DANAUS.

Danaus reports that the Argive vote has secured protection for his daughters. He observes the pursuing fleet, leaves to obtain aid, and returns with armed protection granted by the city; he then urges the Danaids to honor Argos, guard their reputation, and hold chastity dear.

Danaus in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Danaus, son of Belus and Anchinoe and twin brother of Egyptus, was a former king of Argos and father of fifty daughters by many wives. Fearing Egyptus’s sons after a kingdom quarrel, he built the first ship on Athena’s advice, fled with his daughters, touched Rhodes, and set up an image of Lindian Athena. At Argos he received the kingdom from Gelanor, named its inhabitants Danai, and directed his daughters’ marriages and remarriages.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

THE LIBRARY, II. 1. 1-3

Danaus was the son of Belus and Anchinoe and the twin brother of Egyptus. Fearing Egyptus’s sons after a quarrel over the kingdom, he built a ship on Athena’s advice, fled with his fifty daughters, and eventually reached Argos, where he became king and named the inhabitants Danai. He agreed to marry his daughters to the sons of Egyptus but gave the daughters daggers and later arranged athletic contests for their remarriage.

Danaus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Danaus wins Argos from Gelanor after the Argives read a wolf’s victory over a bull as an omen, with which he is identified. As king, he builds temples to Lycian Apollo and Athene, then tries his daughter Hypermnestra for sparing Lynceus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIX.

Danaus defeated Gelanor in the contest for the kingdom of Argos after the Argives interpreted a wolf's victory over a bull as an omen in his favor. He built the temple of Lycian Apollo and its associated monuments, and he put his daughter Hypermnestra on trial for sparing Lynceus.
