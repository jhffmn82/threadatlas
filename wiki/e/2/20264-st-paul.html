St. Paul in Novel-10146

Pioneer Press · 1862~

St. Paul is a Minnesota place that was the capital of the new territory of Minnesota and grew from a busy upper Mississippi steamboat destination into a “great city.” In its early period it had a levee, hotels, stage and steamboat connections, a population of about 10,000, and an active press. Capt. E.Y. Shelly, its first permanently located printer, arrived in 1849; by the 1850s newspapers, printers, editors, and competing Republican and Democratic enterprises made the city a news center. News first came by steamboats and mail coaches and later by telegraph lines. The city celebrated the Atlantic cable in 1858, supported the Five Million Loan by a majority of more than 4,000, and marked Union victories on July 4, 1863 with cheers, cannon, anvils, and firecrackers. Its civic life combined organizations, entertainment, public meetings, and practical services. A Sons of Malta lodge drew men from many occupations. Halls and theaters hosted performances, lectures, drills, political meetings, charitable events, conventions, concerts, and celebrations; a sanitary fair was held in the winter of 1864. Fire protection developed from required marked buckets in 1850 to volunteer companies, hand engines, cisterns, a 1859 parade, and a first steamer in 1866, amid destructive fires including one that destroyed thirty-four buildings in 1860. The central strain is war and loss. During the Sioux outbreak of 1862, St. Paul received refugees, organized home guards, patrolled the bluffs, and feared attack until the Battle of Wood Lake brought safety. Its residents served in the Second Minnesota, followed the battle of Mill Springs anxiously, and later included surviving veterans. The city mourned William Henry Acker through a public reception, funeral, burial, and Acker Post, its first association of Civil War veterans. It also deeply mourned Lincoln’s assassination, draping public and private buildings and holding memorial services. By the later account, it is presented as a transformed great city on the Mississippi River.

St. Paul received a Sons of Malta lodge in 1858, and the organization quickly attracted merchants, lawyers, doctors, printers, and many other men. The city hosted a large Sons of Malta excursion and banquet, as well as a barbecue at Minnehaha Falls. It was also the center of competing Republican and Democratic newspaper enterprises, including the Minnesotian, Times, Minnesotian and Times, St. Paul Daily Press, and Daily North Star.

THE GREAT SIOUX OUTBREAK IN 1862.

St. Paul experienced intense anxiety as reports of Sioux attacks and refugees arrived. Residents organized home guards, patrolled the bluffs, assisted refugees, and feared that the city itself might be attacked.

St. Paul residents petitioned in favor of removing the condemned Indians to Massachusetts and later prepared to receive prisoners being transported to Davenport. Government officers avoided the city by arranging for the steamer Favorite to pass it during the night.

FIREMEN AND FIRES OF PIONEER DAYS.

St. Paul required building owners in 1850 to keep marked fire buckets in good repair and later organized volunteer fire companies. The city purchased hand engines and constructed or requested cisterns, held its first fire department parade in 1859, and received its first steamer in 1866.

HOTEL FIRES.

St. Paul experienced numerous destructive hotel fires during its early history. In the March 16, 1860 fire, thirty-four buildings were destroyed, while the February 3, 1869 International hotel fire threatened the lower city but caused no deaths.

THE FIRST AMUSEMENT HALLS IN ST.

St. Paul had several early amusement and assembly halls that hosted minstrels, dramatic companies, musical performances, political meetings, military drills, lectures, and charitable events. The city was visited by performers and speakers including Dan Emmet, the Hutchinson family, Tom Marshall, Edward Everett, Ralph Waldo Emerson, and John B. Gough.

St. Paul had early entertainment venues including the People's theater, Mazurka hall, Mozart hall, Masonic hall, Agony hall, and the old opera house. The city also hosted patriotic meetings, sanitary fairs, concerts, political conventions, and Washington's Birthday celebrations.

PRINTERS AND EDITORS OF TERRITORIAL DAYS.

St. Paul was the capital of the new territory of Minnesota and the location of the Pioneer, Register, Chronicle, and Daily Democrat offices. Capt. E.Y. Shelly arrived there in July 1849, and many of the territorial printers later worked in its printing offices or enlisted for military service.

THE DECISIVE BATTLE OF MILL SPRINGS.

St. Paul had two full companies in the Second Minnesota and additional residents serving in other companies. Its residents closely followed reports of the battle, and newspaper offices were crowded with relatives and friends seeking news of the soldiers' safety; several surviving veterans later lived and worked in the city.

St. Paul is described as the home of William Henry Acker and the community that deeply mourned his death. His remains were returned there, received by citizens at the steamboat landing, displayed at Masonic Hall, and buried after a funeral at St. Paul's church attended by a large concourse. The first Grand Army post formed in St. Paul was named Acker Post to commemorate him.

THE DEATH OF PRESIDENT LINCOLN.

St. Paul felt Lincoln’s assassination especially deeply. Its public and private buildings were draped in mourning, and memorial services were held throughout the city.

St. Paul is not named directly in the unit, but the setting describes a great city on the Mississippi River whose residents include people labeled “St. Paul’s finest.”
