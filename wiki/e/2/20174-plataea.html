Abstract written by a model

In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Plataea is a Boeotian battlefield where the Persian rear guard and Xerxes’ army under Mardonius are destroyed by Lacedaemonians, Athenians, and Tegeans, and where Darius foretells widespread death as testimony against mortal pride. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Plataea allies with Athens and seeks an Athenian garrison, but the Thebans invade, expel its inhabitants, raze the city, and force the Plataeans to settle in Athens. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Plataea is a free city allied with the Athenians that was chosen for the Greek confederacy’s confrontation with the Persians; after Greek victory, vows were made for a festival and athletic contests, while later Theban and Lacedaemonian attacks ended with surrender, executions, and the city’s destruction. In Description of Greece, Platæa is friendly to the Athenians and tries to preserve peace between the Athenians and Peloponnesians, but Archidamus captures it after a siege despite its lack of responsibility for the conflict. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Platæa is a town near Eleutheræ, associated with Cithæron, the Asopus, monuments to the fighters against the Medes, and temples, hero-chapels, Gargaphia, and Leitus’s tomb; it is presented as Asopus’s daughter in Zeus’s deception of Hera, commemorated by the Eleutheria festival.

Plataea in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Plataea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Plataea is a free city allied with the Athenians at the war’s opening. It was chosen for the Greek confederacy’s confrontation with the Persians, where a Greek victory led to vows of a festival and athletic contests. Later, Theban forces and local traitors tried to seize it, but its inhabitants prevailed with Athenian aid and resisted Archidamus’s siege. After a prolonged Lacedaemonian siege, hunger and losses forced surrender; its defenders were executed and the city destroyed.

CHAP. HI.

Plataea was chosen as the place where the Greek confederacy would confront the Persians for the liberty of Greece. The Greeks vowed to establish a festival and athletic contests there if they were victorious.

I.

Plataea is identified as the place of a Greek victory after which the Athenians returned their families to Athens and rebuilt the city's walls. Pausanias had also commanded at Plataea before undertaking later operations against Persian garrisons.

Platæa in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Platæa first appears

Abstract written by a model

Platæa is a town reached from Eleutheræ, ruled by Cithæron and bounded from Thebes by the Asopus. Near its entrance are tombs of fighters against the Medes, a trophy, and Zeus Eleutherius’s altar, statue, and Eleutheria festival. Within are hero-chapels and temples. It is presented as Asopus’s daughter in Zeus’s deception of Hera, whose reconciliation the festival commemorates, and contains Gargaphia and Leitus’s tomb.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II.

Platæa is reached by the road from Eleutheræ and has nearby tombs, a trophy, the altar and statue of Zeus Eleutherius, and the festival called Eleutheria. Within the town are a hero-chapel to Platæa and a temple of Hera.

CHAPTER III.

Platæa was presented as the daughter of Asopus and was used as the supposed bride in Zeus's deception of Hera. The festival commemorating Hera's reconciliation with Zeus was associated with Platæa and the Platæans.

CHAPTER IV.

Platæa contains the temple to Arean Athene, a temple of Eleusinian Demeter, the tomb of Leitus, and the fountain Gargaphia. The Asopus forms the boundary between the districts of Platæa and Thebes.
