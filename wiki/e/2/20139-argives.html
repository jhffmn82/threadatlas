Abstract written by a model

In Four Plays of Aeschylus, the Argive people, as the commonwealth of Argos, vote to shelter the Danaids and swear to protect them from seizure. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Argives judge and condemn Orestes and Electra, support Polynices against Thebes, banish the Heraclids, and fight Athens under Eurystheus. In Hecuba and other plays, the Argives form Eurystheus's army, demand the surrender of Hercules' children, attack Athens, and are defeated and put to flight. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Argives side with the Tegeans and their allies against the Lacedaemonians, invade Laconia, and kill Alexander and two hundred defenders while entering Tegean territory. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Argives destroy Mycenae, join or lead wars against the Lacedaemonians, capture Orchomenum and Corinth, and annex Corinthian territory. In Description of Greece, the Argives preserve distinctive political and sacred traditions, maintain temples and memorials, and repeatedly wage war or form alliances with neighboring peoples. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Argives conduct religious rites, wage wars and campaigns, award athletic victories, aid Epaminondas and the Thebans, and dispute with the Lacedæmonians.

the Argive people in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Argive people first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Argive people, citizens of Argos also called Pelasgians, constitute the commonwealth whose counsel Pelasgus must seek. Asked to judge the supplicant Danaids, they unanimously vote to shelter them, keep them free from violent seizure, provide Danaus armed escorts, and bind themselves by oath and public curse to protect the maidens.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Enter the KING OF ARGOS.

The people of Argos must be consulted before Pelasgus can decide whether to protect the Danaids. Pelasgus hopes that seeing the signs of suppliance will make them pity the Danaids and reject the young men’s violent pursuit.

Re-enter DANAUS.

The Argive people unanimously vote to let the Danaids remain free and to prevent anyone from carrying them away by force. They also grant Danaus armed escorts and accept responsibility for protecting the maidens.

Argives in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Argives are the citizens and fighting people of Argos. At the beginning, they judge Orestes for matricide: they reject him and Electra, forbid anyone to shelter or speak with them, guard the palace, and, despite some support for banishment or a defense of Orestes, condemn them to death, with stoning or the sword considered. Menelaus seeks to summon or persuade them, while their expected hostility shapes events at the palace. They next appear as a large army led by Polynices against Thebes, supporting his effort to recover his rule with horsemen, shields, chariots, princes, and ten thousand spears. They are also the power that has banished the Heraclids and whose laws condemn them; through Copreus, they threaten war on Athens unless the Heraclids are surrendered. Finally, under Eurystheus, the Argives advance on and fight Athens and Hercules' descendants. They initially break the opposing spear line, but are ultimately driven into flight and defeated.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

9 sections; each heading opens the text

ORESTES.

The Argives have decreed that no one may receive Orestes and Electra under a roof or at a hearth, or speak to them. They are to decide whether Orestes should be stoned or killed by the sword, and Helen fears their hostility.

MENELAUS, ORESTES, CHORUS.

The Argives have rejected Orestes and do not allow him to live among them. They are expected to decide his fate that very day, with death by stoning presented as the sentence.

PYLADES, ORESTES, CHORUS.

The Argive citizens surround the palace with guards and decide the fate of Orestes and Electra. Their assembly condemns them to death, though the Messenger reports that some citizens favored banishment or argued in Orestes' defense.

PHRYGIAN, CHORUS.

The Argives are expected to respond if they hear the noise at the palace. Menelaus calls on them to come armed to his aid, while Orestes tells Menelaus to persuade the city not to condemn him and his companions.

THE PHŒNICIAN VIRGINS.

The Argives form the army that Polynices leads against Thebes. Their forces approach the city with numerous horsemen, shields, chariots, and renowned leaders.

POLYNICES, CHORUS.

The Argives march against Thebes and support Polynices in his attempt to recover his rule. Their army includes many princes of Argos and Mycenae and is described as numbering ten thousand spears.

THE HERACLIDÆ.

The Argives are presented as the people from whom the Heraclids have been banished and whose laws condemn them to death. Copreus threatens that Argive forces will wage war against Athens if the Heraclids are not surrendered.

CHOR. This land is ever willing to aid in a just cause those in difficulty;

The Argives advance toward Athens under Eurystheus, with their warriors ready to aid their leader. Their army has not yet attacked when Demophon first sees it, but it is close enough for its general to be distinctly visible.

SERV. O mistress, I bring news most concise for you to hear, and to myself

The Argives fight against Athens and the descendants of Hercules under Eurystheus's leadership. Their forces initially break the opposing spear line but are eventually driven into flight.

Argives in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

Argives are an army of inhabitants and forces of Argos under Eurystheus, hostile to Athens over its protection of the children of Hercules. Represented by Copreus and Eurystheus, they demand the children’s return and threaten armed conflict. Numerous and fierce, they wait near Athens, attack, initially break through Athenian lines, then give way and are defeated and put to flight.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

THE CHILDREN OF HERCULES.

The Argives, represented by Copreus and Eurystheus, demand the return of the children of Hercules and threaten Athens with armed conflict. Their forces are described as numerous and fierce, and Copreus announces that they will advance against Athens if the children are protected.

DEMOPHOON, IOLAUS, CHORUS.

The Argive army is led by Eurystheus and waits on a mountain ridge while its leader considers the safest route and position for an attack. It is close to the Athenian forces, and its approach causes the sacrifices and defenses of Athens to be set in motion.

SERVANT, ALCMENA, CHORUS.

The Argive army fights against the opposing forces, initially breaks through their lines, and later gives way. It is ultimately put to flight after a fierce battle.

Argives in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

Argives made war on Mycenae, conquered and destroyed it, enslaving its citizens and dedicating spoil to the god. They joined Athenians against Lacedaemonians and Thessalians at Tanagra; later led a coalition against Lacedaemonians, captured Orchomenum, lost at Mantinea, and restored democracy. They then aided Corinthian faction, took Corinth and its castle, and joined its territory to their own.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. XIII.

The Argives attacked Mycenae with a large army after the Spartans became unable to assist it. They conquered the city, enslaved its citizens, dedicated a tenth of the spoil to the god, and destroyed Mycenae.

CHAP. XIX.

The Argives joined the Athenians against the Lacedaemonians and Thessalians. They fought alongside the Athenians at Tanagra.

CHAR VIIL

The Argives led the coalition against the Lacedaemonians and formed an alliance with the Athenians. They fought in Arcadia, captured Orchomenum, suffered defeat at Mantinea, and later restored their democracy after an oligarchic seizure of power.

CHAP. IX,

The Argives joined the confederacy against the Lacedaemonians and fought with the Boeotians. They assisted the Corinthian faction in the sedition, later took Corinth and its castle, and joined the territory to their own.

Argives in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

Argives are a Greek people defined in these records by distinctive political traditions, extensive sacred memory, and recurrent warfare, especially with the Lacedæmonians. They said their people had uniquely been divided into three kingdoms, preserved accounts of royal succession, the Heraclidæ’s return, and Mysius’s hospitality to Demeter, and credited Phoroneus rather than Prometheus with fire. Seeking equality and home rule, they progressively curtailed their kings: Medon’s line retained only the title, and Meltas was deprived of the kingdom. Their city maintained many temples, tombs, statues, and memorials. Their traditions included Atheras and Mysius receiving Demeter, annual torch rites for Hypermnestra and Lynceus, and claims concerning heroes, the Palladium, and tombs, some claims disputed by the narrator. Survivors of the shipwreck at Caphareus, cold and hungry, prayed for rescue, found Dionysus’s cave, ate goats and wore their skins, then brought its statue home for continuing worship. Conflict shapes much of their history. They defeated the Lacedæmonians near Hysiæ, commemorated the victors, resisted Cleomenes through Telesilla and Argive women, and repeatedly contested Thyrea. They destroyed Asine after its people aided Lacedæmonian ravaging, while preserving Apollo’s temple there and burying Lysistratus nearby. They also dispossessed Orneæ and Tiryns, supported Deiphontes in Epidauria, aided Helos, and secretly and openly supported Messenians, including in the restoration of Messene. Later they became friendly with Athenians, allied with Mantineans and Athenians against the Lacedæmonians, and were said to dispute antiquity with Athenians more than other Greeks.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Argives said that Mysius had been a host of Demeter and that their people had once been divided into three kingdoms. They also preserved traditions about the succession of kings and about the Heraclidæ’s return and claims in the Peloponnese.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Argives reduced the power of their kings until Medon and his descendants retained only the royal title, and they later deprived Meltas of his kingdom. They built and maintained numerous temples, tombs, statues, and memorials in their city, fought repeatedly with the Lacedæmonians, and were defended by Telesilla and the women of Argos against Cleomenes.

CHAPTER XXIII.

After being shipwrecked at Caphareus, some Argives escaped to shore in cold and hunger, found a cave of Dionysus, killed and ate wild goats sheltering there, and used their skins for clothing. They later carried the statue of Dionysus from the cave to their homeland and worshipped it; the Argives also claimed to possess several tombs and the Palladium, claims the narrator disputes.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Argives are said to have erected or maintained monuments and sanctuaries connected with their heroes and myths, including the monument of the sons of Ægyptus and the tomb of those who defeated the Lacedæmonians near Hysiæ. They supported Deiphontes in taking possession of Epidauria after Pityreus gave up the land, and later dispossessed the people of Orneæ and Tiryns, incorporating the people of Orneæ among themselves. Argive traditions also identify Œnoe as the burial place of Œneus and preserve the annual torch procession commemorating Hypermnestra and Lynceus.

CHAPTER XXXV.

The Argives say that Atheras and Mysius received Demeter hospitably, while Colontas rejected her. They destroyed Asine after its people joined the Lacedæmonians in ravaging Argive territory, and they later claimed to have recovered Thyrea justly by conquest.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

The Argives fought the Lacedæmonians under Cleomenes, and about 5,000 fled into a grove sacred to Argus. They also supported the people of Helos against the Lacedæmonians.

CHAPTER VI.

The Argives were involved in recurring hostility with the Lacedæmonians. They fought over Thyrea, punished the Asinæans for supporting the Lacedæmonians, and later received money from Timocrates through Cylon and Sodamas to stir up war against Sparta.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Argives became friendly with the Athenians after the Lacedæmonians dismissed the Athenian troops from Ithome. They later assisted the Messenians, chose Epiteles as their General and the restorer of Messene, and sacrificed to Argive Hera and Nemean Zeus during the restoration.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

The Argives refused to surrender Hercules when the people of Elis demanded compensation for the killing of the sons of Actor. They later assisted Hercules in his campaign against Elis and subsequently allied with the Mantineans and Athenians against the Lacedæmonians.

Argives in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Argives, also called Danai, are a Peloponnesian group marked by warfare, religious offerings, athletic judgment, and later political conflict. In ancient times they introduced fire into Lerne from the shrine of Pyronian Artemis. They conducted two expeditions against Thebes, associated with Polynices and the sons of Polynices, captured Thebes, took Tiresias and spoil to Apollo at Delphi, and dedicated a brazen horse and other votive statues from victory spoils at Œnoe. In a later campaign they entered Bœotia with Arcadian and Messenian allies, initially defeated the Thebans at Ismenius, then were destroyed except for Adrastus. They honored the Epigoni and aided Epaminondas and the Thebans in restoring the Messenians. At Nemea they awarded dead Creugas victory after Damoxenus violated conditions. Finally, Gallus arbitrated their dispute with the Lacedæmonians and treated them contemptuously.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XI.

The Argives were involved in disputes with the Lacedæmonians that Gallus was sent to arbitrate. Gallus treated them with great contempt.

CHAPTER XL.

The Argives awarded the dead Creugas the victory at Nemea because Damoxenus had violated the mutual agreement governing their boxing match. They also erected a statue to Creugas in Argos.

CHAPTER IX.

The Argives brought an army from the middle of the Peloponnese into Bœotia and were joined by allies from Arcadia and Messenia. They defeated the Thebans at the first onset at Ismenius but were later destroyed, except for Adrastus.

CHAPTER X.

The Argives dedicated statues of those associated with Polynices’ expedition against Thebes, along with the chariot of Amphiaraus and other figures. They said these offerings were made from spoils won at Oenoe in Argolis with Athenian allies, and they also erected statues of the Epigoni and figures connected with restoring the Messenians.
