Abstract written by a model

In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Eteocles rules Cadmus’ folk, commands the defense of Thebes, guards the seventh gate against Polynices, and dies fighting him before receiving honorable burial rites. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Eteocles gains and defends the Theban throne, fights Polynices in single combat, dies with him, and receives sacred burial honors. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Eteocles rules Thebes, refuses to share power with Polynices, prepares the city for war, fights and dies beside his brother, and arranges Creon’s succession and Antigone’s marriage. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Eteocles first reigns under his agreement with Polynices, refuses to surrender the government, and dies fighting him. In The Library, Eteocles rules Thebes, breaks his compact with Polynices, prepares for battle, and is slain by him in single combat. In The Library, Eteocles agrees to alternate rule with Polynices, refuses to surrender the kingdom, organizes Thebes against the Argives, and dies fighting his brother. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Eteocles rules Andreis, retains its name, founds tribes, grants a village to Almus, establishes worship of the Graces, and dies fighting his brother Polynices. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Eteocles rules Thebes, refuses to yield the kingdom after his year, and dies with Polynices in single combat.

Eteocles in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where Eteocles first appears

Abstract written by a model

Eteocles, son of Oedipus and Iokaste and twin brother of Polynices, is king and ruler of Cadmus’ folk. During the Argive siege, he commands the Cadmeans to arm, hold the walls, and resist fear. He selects defenders for the seven gates, dismisses the enemy champions’ boasts, and becomes the seventh defender. Though warned by the Chorus, he chooses to guard the seventh gate himself against Polynices, asserting his claims as ruler and brother. In single combat, the brothers kill one another. Because Eteocles defended Cadmus’ city, the state orders careful and honorable burial rites for him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Eteocles is one of the two sons of Oedipus and Iokaste and is generally treated as the elder brother. In The Seven against Thebes he urges the Cadmeans to resist Polynices and dies in single combat against him.

THE SEVEN AGAINST THEBES

Eteocles, ruler of Cadmus’ folk, orders the city’s defenders to arm themselves and hold the walls against the Argive attack. He rebukes the Chorus for spreading fear, urges obedience and silence, and declares that he will join six chosen men to defend the seven gates.

Enter on one side THE SPY; on the other ETEOCLES and the SIX

Eteocles hears The Spy's report and appoints defenders for the gates, repeatedly rejecting the enemy champions' boasts and emblems as sources of fear. At the seventh gate he decides to confront Polynices himself, claiming the right of ruler against would-be ruler and brother against brother, despite the Chorus's warnings.

Enter THE SPY.

Eteocles dies after killing and being killed by Polynices in the seventh-gate battle. The city decrees that he receive careful and honorable burial because he defended Cadmus’ city.

Eteocles in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Eteocles first appears

Abstract written by a model

Eteocles is the son of Jocasta and Oedipus, brother of Polynices, and king and ruler of Thebes through possession of its sceptre. After agreeing to alternate rule with Polynices, he keeps power beyond his term, deprives Polynices of land and throne and driving him into exile. He values kingship above all else, refuses reconciliation or division of power, and prepares Thebes for war against Argos by appointing commanders for its seven gates. As defender and commander of the Theban army, he seeks to kill Polynices, orders that Polynices be left unburied if defeated, and proposes that the brothers fight alone for the palace. In their duel at the Electran gate, Eteocles wounds Polynices but is mortally wounded in return and dies beside him. Before dying, he gives Creon dominion over Thebes and arranges Antigone's marriage to Haemon.

Eteocles is the son of Jocasta and Oedipus and the brother of Polynices. Although he had agreed to rule Thebes for one year before yielding the throne to Polynices, he remained in power and drove Polynices into exile.

Eteocles comes to the conference after arranging the defenses of Thebes. He admits that he desires kingship above all else and refuses to surrender the sceptre or share the land with Polynices, threatening to kill him and accepting the prospect of war.

Eteocles tells Creon that his attempts to reconcile with Polynices have failed and prepares to lead the Theban forces against Argos. He agrees to place seven chosen commanders at the seven gates and hopes to meet and kill Polynices in battle. Before departing, he orders that Polynices remain unburied in Theban land if he is victorious and asks Creon to care for Jocasta and attend to Antigone's marriage to Hæmon.

MESSENGER, JOCASTA, CHORUS.

Eteocles commands the Theban defenses and proposes to fight Polynices alone so that the survivor will hold the palace. In the duel he wounds Polynices, but Polynices mortally wounds him in return; Eteocles dies beside his brother.

ANTIGONE, CREON, CHORUS.

Eteocles dies fighting Polynices at the Electran gate. Before his death, he gives Creon dominion over the land and arranges Antigone's marriage to Haemon.

Eteocles in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Eteocles first appears

Abstract written by a model

Eteocles is a king of Andreis, described variously as son of Oedipus, Andreus through Euippe, or the river Cephisus, and called Cephisiades in some poems. As king he retained the country’s name, founded the tribes Cephisias and Eteoclea, granted a village to Almus, and was credited with first sacrificing to and establishing worship of three Graces. After Oedipus’s death, he quarrelled with his brother Polynices; they fought and killed each other. Accounts differ on whether succession went to his son Laodamas or Almus’s posterity, as he died without male progeny.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

Eteocles sent for Polynices after Oedipus’s death, but the brothers quarrelled when Polynices returned. Eteocles and Polynices challenged each other and killed one another, after which the kingdom passed to Laodamas, Eteocles’s son.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Eteocles became king and allowed the country to retain the name Andreis. He established the tribes Cephisias and Eteoclea, naming one after the river Cephisus and the other after himself.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Eteocles was said by the Bœotians to have been the first to sacrifice to the Graces. He was also credited with establishing the worship of three Graces, though their names were not remembered.

CHAPTER XXXVI.

Eteocles died without any male progeny, after which the succession passed to the posterity of Almus.
