Abstract written by a model

In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, it is the sea-storm that strikes the returning Greek fleet, causing many Greeks and ships to be lost. In Hecuba and other plays, it is the storm Neptune causes after Troy’s fall to disperse the Greek fleet in the Aegean Sea. In The Trojan Women of Euripides, it is the storm Athena and Poseidon send to drown Greek soldiers and ships near Euboea and the surrounding reefs and islands. In The Library, it is the storm Zeus sends at Athena’s request that founders many Greek ships near Tenos and separates Menelaus from the others. In The Library, it is the shipwreck at Caphereus in which Greek ships mistake a beacon for a harbour and many Greeks die. In The Fall of Troy, it is a divinely caused tempest that prevents the Argives from sailing and destroys the Achaean fleet, ships, and men near Euboea and Caphereus.

storm and shipwrecks in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where storm and shipwrecks first appears

Abstract written by a model

A divinely caused tempest, raised at Achilles' request or for his benefit, prevents the Argives from sailing until Polyxeina is sacrificed. Summoned by Athena through Iris and Aeolus, it darkens sky and sea, unleashes winds and waves, and destroys the Achaean fleet, ships, and men near Euboea and Caphereus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK XIV.

A storm raised at Achilles' request prevents the Argives from sailing until Polyxeina is sacrificed. Calchas foresees a later destruction of the Argives near the Capherean Rocks, but the unit ends before that event occurs.

When nigh Euboea's windy heights they drew,

The storm is summoned by Athena through Iris and Aeolus, who releases the winds from their mountain dwelling. It darkens the sky, churns the sea into towering waves, and destroys ships and men around Euboea and Caphereus.
