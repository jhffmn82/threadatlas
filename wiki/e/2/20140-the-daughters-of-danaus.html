the daughters of Danaus in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where the daughters of Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

The daughters of Danaus are a sister-band of suppliant maidens, descended from Io through Epaphus, who flee Egypt with their father to escape forced marriages to the sons of Aegyptus. Carrying wool-wound olive-shoots, they seek protection and chastity in Argos. The Argive people grant them protection; when the pursuing fleet arrives, they resist the Herald, cling to sanctuary, and are offered secure lodging in the city.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

The Suppliant Maidens

The daughters of Danaus are exiles who tore themselves away from unwanted marriages and crossed the sea with their father. They carry olive-shoots wound with wool as signs of supplication and are instructed to maintain modesty while seeking protection in Argos.

Re-enter DANAUS.

The Danaids receive the Argive people’s protection but remain terrified when the pursuing fleet approaches. They cling to the sanctuary against the Herald of Aegyptus, are threatened with forced removal, and are offered secure lodging within the city.

Chorus of the Daughters of Danaus in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where Chorus of the Daughters of Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Chorus of the Daughters of Danaus is a collective group of Danaus’s daughters and suppliants. Descended from Io through Epaphus, Libya, and Belus, they regard Argos as their ancestral home. They flee Egypt with Danaus because they horrify at marriage to the sons of Aegyptus and seek protection from them in Argos. At sacred altars they appeal to Zeus, other gods, Pelasgus, and Justice, even threatening suicide if surrendered. After Danaus returns, they praise Argos and pray for its peace, prosperity, fertility, and freedom from plague and civic bloodshed. As pursuers approach, they express terror, resist the Herald, thank the King for intervening, and continue praying that Zeus will prevent the hated marriage.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

The Danaids are the daughters of Danaus who flee Egypt to escape forced marriage with the sons of Aegyptus. In The Suppliant Maidens they seek protection at Argos, and later tradition within the trilogy suggests that all but one kill their bridegrooms.

THE SUPPLIANT MAIDENS / DEDICATION

The daughters of Danaus hold marriage to the sons of Aegyptus in horror. They flee with their father over the sea to Argos, where they receive shelter and protection.

The Suppliant Maidens

The Chorus declares that it fled Egypt to escape forced marriages to the sons of Aegyptus. It appeals to Zeus and other gods for protection, identifies Argos as its ancestral home through Io and Epaphus, and takes refuge as a band of suppliants at the sacred altar.

Enter the KING OF ARGOS.

The Danaids claim Argive descent through Io, Epaphus, Libya, and Belus. They seek refuge from the sons of Aegyptus, threaten to hang themselves from the statues if they are surrendered, and appeal to Pelasgus, Zeus, and Justice for protection.

Re-enter DANAUS.

The Chorus praises Argos and prays for the city’s prosperity, peace, fertility, and freedom from plague and civic bloodshed. It later expresses terror at the approaching pursuers, resists the Herald, gives thanks for the King’s intervention, and prays that Zeus will avert the hated marriage.

daughters of Danaus in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where daughters of Danaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

The daughters of Danaus fled with Danaus to Argos, were given as brides, and all but Hypermnestra killed their sleeping bridegrooms. They buried and honored the dead, were purified by Athena and Hermes, and later had their marriages awarded through an athletic contest.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

THE LIBRARY, II. 1. 1-3

The fifty daughters of Danaus fled with him to Argos and were allotted as brides to the sons of Egyptus. All but Hypermnestra killed their bridegrooms while they slept; they later buried the heads at Lerna and honored the bodies before the city, and Athena and Hermes purified them at Zeus’s command.
