  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Antigone buries Polynices in defiance of Creon’s command, is condemned to a stone tomb, and dies by hanging.

  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Antigone drags Polynices’ corpse until she can throw it onto Eteocles’ blazing funeral pile.

Antigone in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Antigone is a virgin princess of Thebes, daughter of Jocasta and Oedipus and sister of Polynices and Eteocles. She longs to see and embrace exiled Polynices, accompanies Jocasta to stop her brothers’ conflict, and mourns Jocasta and both brothers after their deaths. Defying Creon’s prohibition, she resolves to bury Polynices, rejects marriage to Haemon, and chooses to guide Oedipus in exile.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

THE PHŒNICIAN VIRGINS.

Antigone persuades the Tutor to let her leave the virgin's apartments so that she can view the Argive army. She recognizes Polynices among the approaching forces and longs to embrace her brother after his long exile.

MESSENGER, JOCASTA, CHORUS.

Antigone is summoned from the palace by Jocasta and accompanies her toward the armies to prevent Eteocles and Polynices from killing each other. She later stands with her dying mother and brothers, and the Messenger says that she brings the dead toward the city for their friends to lament.

Antigone in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Antigone is a young, unmarried daughter of Oedipus, sister of Ismene, Polynices, and Eteocles, and the betrothed of Haemon. She is defined above all by devoted kinship and piety: she cares for, guides, and accompanies her blind father through hardship, and she honors her brothers, urging Polynices to abandon the war that will destroy him and Eteocles. After Oedipus disappears, she mourns him and seeks to return to Thebes. There Creon’s attendants seize her during an earlier conflict, though she is later rescued. When Creon forbids Polynices’ burial, she asks Ismene to share the dangerous task but resolves to act alone if necessary. She buries Polynices, declaring that divine and unwritten heavenly laws outweigh a mortal ruler’s command, and accepts death rather than submit. Condemned by Creon, she is led alive into a stone tomb, grieving that she will have neither marriage nor children. She dies by hanging in the death-chamber; Haemon dies beside her.

Antigone in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Antigone is Oedipus’s daughter and the sister of Ismene and Polyneices. Most importantly, she is devoted to her blind father: she serves as his eyes, guides him during his wandering, cares for him, and performs filial duty. She leads him into Colonus, identifies Athens and its sacred grove, pleads for the Chorus to shelter him, and remains with him while Ismene performs offerings. After Creon’s guards seize and drag her away, she and Ismene are held captive and mistreated, but Theseus returns them safely. She then urges Oedipus to hear Polyneices and begs Polyneices to abandon his attack on Thebes, promising burial rites if he dies. Following Oedipus’s death, she mourns the labor of guiding him, accepts that his burial place must remain hidden, and plans to return to Thebes to stop her brothers’ conflict. Condemned to death, she is confined alive in a rocky tomb, where she is found dead by strangulation with a linen noose; Haemon dies beside her, and she is described as his bride in death.

Antigone in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Antigone is Oedipus’s daughter, Ismene’s full sister, and sister of Polyneices and Eteocles; she is betrothed to Haemon and descended from Oedipus’ incestuous marriage. She cares for and accompanies Oedipus in exile, remaining beside him and helping fulfill his instructions. What matters most to her is piety toward the dead and obedience to divine, unwritten laws above human ordinance. When Creon forbids Polyneices’ burial, she resolves to bury him, asks Ismene’s help, and proceeds alone after Ismene refuses. Caught and brought before Creon, she admits the act, defends it as required by Heaven and conscience, and is condemned to death. She rejects Ismene’s shared guilt, then bids farewell to her country while mourning her lost marriage, family, and future as she is taken to a living tomb.

Antigone remains beside Oedipus and appeals to Colonus to prove the glory attributed to it. She warns Oedipus that Creon is approaching with his company.

ANTIGONE

Antigone resolves to bury her brother Polyneices despite Creon’s prohibition and asks Ismene to help. She rejects Ismene’s refusal, declares that she will act alone, and accepts the possibility of an honorable death in obedience to the eternal laws of Heaven.

[Enter GUARD bringing ANTIGONE]

Antigone is brought before Creon after being caught burying the corpse. She openly admits the deed and defends it by appealing to the unwritten laws of Heaven, saying that she chose to die rather than leave her dead brother unburied. Creon condemns her to death, and she rejects Ismene’s attempt to share her guilt.

[Enter HAEMON]

Antigone bids farewell to her country and laments that she is being taken to a living tomb as an unwed bride. She defends her burial of Polyneices as an act required by conscience and mourns her father, mother, brother, marriage, and future.
