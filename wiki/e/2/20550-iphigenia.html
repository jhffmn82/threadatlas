  • In Iphigenia in Tauris, Iphigenia is Artemis's unwilling priestess who recognizes Orestes, deceives Thoas, and escapes Tauris with Orestes, Pylades, and Artemis's image.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Iphigenia is Agamemnon's daughter who is nearly sacrificed at Aulis, becomes Artemis's priestess at Tauris, recognizes Orestes, and escapes with him and Pylades.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Clytemnestra's daughter is sacrificed by Electra's father at Aulis, and her death becomes a disputed justification for Clytemnestra's murder of him.
  • In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Iphigenia is Agamemnon and Clytemnestra's daughter whose sacrifice at Aulis becomes Clytemnestra's reason for murdering Agamemnon.
  • In The Library, Iphigenia is rescued from sacrifice at Aulis by Artemis, becomes a Taurian priestess, recognizes Orestes and Pylades, and escapes with Orestes carrying the wooden image.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Iphigenia is Agamemnon's daughter who is selected for sacrifice to Diana but spared when Diana substitutes a hind.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Iphigenia is Agamemnon's daughter for whom a doe is substituted at Aulis, and she is identified with an Artemis statue at Ægira.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Iphigenia is saved from sacrifice by Diana, becomes her priestess at Tauris, and escapes with Orestes, Pylades, and Diana's statue.

Iphigenia in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · -0413/-0411

Iphigenia is the eldest daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, sister of Orestes and Electra, and a Greek woman from Argos. Thought dead after a supposed sacrifice at Aulis, she was saved by Artemis through substitution by a fawn and taken to Tauris. There, unwillingly serving as Artemis’ priestess, she consecrates captured strangers before they are slain. Exile, homesickness, and bitterness toward those connected to her supposed murder shape her life, while she rejects the Taurian human-sacrifice custom as unworthy of divine goodness. After a prophetic dream makes her believe Orestes dead, she performs his funeral rite. When Orestes and Pylades are captured, she questions Orestes about Greece and her family, seeks to send a tablet to Argos, reveals herself as Iphigenia believed dead, and recognizes him as her surviving brother. Reunited, she learns Electra lives and is married to Pylades, but fears the rite will still kill Orestes. Determined to save the Greek strangers, she devises a false purification for Orestes and Pylades, persuades King Thoas to allow it, leads them from the temple, frees Orestes, and carries Artemis’ image to their ship. Athena then assigns her to Brauron, where she is to serve, live, die, and be buried.

Iphigenia in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Iphigenia is a mortal Greek and Argive, chiefly identified as the daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra and the sister of Orestes and Electra. She is summoned to Aulis under the deceptive promise of marriage to Achilles, but Calchas has declared that she must be sacrificed to Diana so the Greek ships may sail. After first begging Agamemnon to spare her, she chooses to give her body willingly for Greece, seeking its freedom and the destruction of Troy. At the altar, Diana saves her by divine substitution and takes her away, though those in Argos believe she has died. She is brought unwillingly to Tauri and made Diana's priestess, captive in a barbarian land where she must prepare Greek strangers for human sacrifice. Believing a dream means Orestes has died, she mourns him. When Orestes and Pylades arrive as captives, she questions them about Greece, offers to save one by sending a letter to Argos, and initially prepares the other for sacrifice. The letter and shared memories reveal that Orestes is her brother. Reunited, she plans their escape. She deceives Thoas by claiming that Orestes and Pylades require purification, then leaves Tauris with Orestes, Pylades, and Diana's sacred image. She is finally directed to guard Artemis at Brauron. Her record also preserves differing parentage claims, naming Agamemnon and Clytemnestra in the usual tradition, and Theseus and Helen according to Antoninus Liberalis.

Iphigenia willingly offers her body for sacrifice on behalf of her country and all Greece, asking that no one restrain her. At the moment of sacrifice she vanishes, and the Messenger says that she has flown to the Gods and now lives among them.

Iphigenia recounts that Diana rescued her from sacrifice at Aulis and brought her to Tauri, where she serves as priestess and prepares Grecian strangers for sacrifice. She believes her dream signifies that Orestes is dead and mourns him before learning that two Greek strangers have arrived.

CHOR. Thou hast told wondrous things concerning him who has appeared,

Iphigenia regrets that she has become hostile toward strangers after learning through dreams that Orestes is dead. She questions Orestes and Pylades about Greece and learns that Troy has fallen, Helen lives with Menelaus, Agamemnon is dead, Clytemnestra is dead, and Orestes is supposedly nowhere. She offers to save one stranger by sending her letter to Argos, then prepares to sacrifice the other, not knowing that Orestes is her brother.

CHOR. I deplore thee now destined to the gory streams of the lustral

Iphigenia arranges for Pylades to carry her letter to Argos and promises to help him escape the barbarian land. She reveals through the letter that she is alive and asks Orestes to remove her from the land where she serves in the sacrifices of the Goddess. She recognizes Orestes through his answers about the Atreus and Thyestes story, the woven images, her departure for Aulis, and the spear of Pelops.

IPH. O dearest one, no more, for thou art dearest. I hold thee, Orestes,

Iphigenia recognizes Orestes as her long-lost brother and rejoices that he has survived. She recalls the sacrifice arranged by her father and fears that she, Orestes, and Pylades may be discovered, but devises a plan to escape with them and Diana’s image.

Iphigenia tells Thoas that the sacred image has turned away because the two strangers bear the pollution of matricide. She pretends to purify them and the image, orders that the strangers be taken to the shore, and escapes with Orestes and the image. Minerva commands her to become guardian of the temple of the Goddess at Brauron.
