The Aeneid in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

This document summarizes the twelve books of Aeneas’s journey from the ruins of Troy to victory in Italy. Driven by fate to establish a new Trojan line, Aeneas survives Juno’s hostility, storms, loss, war, and personal grief. After a storm scatters his fleet, he reaches Carthage, where Queen Dido welcomes him. Venus and Cupid cause Dido to love him, and at her request Aeneas recounts Troy’s fall, his escape with Anchises and Ascanius, Creusa’s death, and his long wanderings through Thrace, Delos, Crete, the Strophades, Epirus, Sicily, and Drepanum, where Anchises dies. Jupiter orders Aeneas onward; he leaves Dido, who curses him and kills herself. In Sicily Aeneas honors Anchises with funeral games, then continues toward Italy despite the loss of Palinurus. At Cumae, the Sibyl guides him into the underworld, where Anchises reveals the future Roman line, Rome’s greatness, and the early death of Marcellus. Aeneas then reaches Latium, where King Latinus receives the Trojans and fate points to Aeneas’s marriage with Lavinia. Juno resists this outcome by sending Alecto to inflame Amata and Turnus, beginning war between Trojans, Latins, and Rutulians. Aeneas gains allies from Evander, Pallas, and the Tuscans, and receives divine armor from Venus and Vulcan. The war brings major losses: Nisus and Euryalus die attempting to summon Aeneas; Turnus kills Pallas; Aeneas kills Lausus and Mezentius; and Camilla is slain by Aruns. Peace proposals fail despite Latinus’s recognition that fate favors Aeneas. In the final book, a planned single combat is interrupted, Aeneas is wounded and healed, and he attacks the Latin city. Amata kills herself believing Turnus dead. At last Aeneas and Turnus fight alone. Juno accepts peace preserving Latin identity, but Aeneas, seeing Pallas’s belt on Turnus, kills him in vengeance.

Narrative, section by section

BOOK I

After seven years of wandering, Aeneas and the Trojan fleet are driven toward Italy by a storm raised by Aeolus at Juno’s request. Neptune calms the sea, and Aeneas reaches a secure Libyan harbor with six other ships, where the Trojans hunt, feast, and mourn their scattered companions. Venus appeals to Jupiter, who confirms that Aeneas will found the Trojan line in Italy and that his descendants will lead to Rome’s greatness. Jupiter sends Mercury to ensure that the Trojans receive a friendly welcome in Carthage, where Queen Dido is expected to encounter Aeneas.

Meantime, in shades of night Aeneas lies:

Aeneas explores the unfamiliar Libyan coast after a sleepless night and encounters Venus disguised as a huntress. Venus tells him that he is near Carthage, a Tyrian colony ruled by Dido, and recounts how Dido fled Tyre after her brother Pygmalion murdered her husband, Sichaeus. Dido and her companions seized a fleet, carried away Pygmalion’s treasure, and founded the new settlement after landing in Libya. Venus then asks Aeneas about his origins and his purpose in the region.

To whom, with sorrow streaming from his eyes,

Aeneas and his surviving companions reach the Libyan coast after storms scatter the Trojan fleet. Venus guides Aeneas and Achates, concealed by clouds, into Dido’s rising city, where they see paintings of the Trojan War and find the missing Trojans pleading for refuge. Dido welcomes Aeneas, promises aid, and holds a feast, while Venus sends Cupid in Ascanius’s form to inspire Dido with an irresistible love. Venus hides the real Ascanius in a magical sleep while Cupid prepares to carry out the deception.

Cupid meantime assum’d his form and face,

Cupid assumes the form and face of Aeneas and follows Achates to the feast, where he presents gifts and secretly kindles Dido's love for Aeneas. Dido hosts the Trojan and Tyrian guests with food, wine, prayers, music, and conversation. During the feast, Iopas sings of the heavens and nature, while Dido questions Aeneas about Priam, Hector, the Trojan War, and his flight and wanderings. She finally asks him to recount the Grecian stratagems, the fall of the town, and the hardships he endured.

BOOK II

Aeneas recounts to the queen how Troy was destroyed after the Greeks, weary of the ten-year siege, used Sinon and the wooden horse to deceive the Trojans. Laocoon warns that the horse conceals Greek soldiers, but the Trojans interpret the appearance of two serpents as proof that Minerva favors admitting it. The Greeks hide their fleet at Tenedos, return by night, and release the warriors concealed inside the horse. The Greek soldiers then kill the sentries, open Troy’s gates, and begin the sack of the city.

“’Twas in the dead of night, when sleep repairs

Aeneas describes how Hector’s ghost warned him that Troy was doomed and charged him to escape with the Trojan gods. He recounts joining Pantheus, Coroebus, and other Trojans in a night battle against the Greeks, briefly using captured Greek arms before the disguise failed. The Greeks overrun the Trojan palace, kill its defenders, and slaughter Priam after Pyrrhus pursues and kills Polites. The account presents the fall and sack of Troy as the fulfillment of divine fate.

“Then, not before, I felt my curdled blood

Aeneas sees Helen hiding in Vesta’s temple and nearly kills her, but Venus appears and reveals that the gods, not Helen and Paris, are destroying Troy. Aeneas returns to Anchises, who first refuses to flee but changes his mind after omens involving Ascanius and a celestial sign. Aeneas carries Anchises, leads Ascanius, and attempts to escape with Creusa, but loses her during the flight. Creusa appears as a ghost and tells Aeneas that fate has decreed a long journey to Latium, where he will found a new kingdom.

BOOK III

Aeneas recounts how he and the Trojan survivors leave the fallen Troy, build a fleet near Ida, and settle first in Thrace, where he discovers that Polydore was murdered and buried beneath a bleeding myrtle. They sail to Delos, receive Apollo’s oracle, and mistakenly interpret it as directing them to Crete, but the household gods reveal in a dream that their true destination is Hesperia, or Italy. After abandoning Crete, Aeneas’s fleet is scattered by a storm and reaches the Strophades, where the Harpies attack them and Celaeno prophesies that famine will precede their settlement. Aeneas then visits several shores, including Epirus and Chaonia, and meets Andromache and Helenus at Buthrotus before asking Helenus for further guidance toward Italy.

“The prophet first with sacrifice adores

Aeneas recounts to the queen how Helenus prophesied the dangers and route of the Trojan voyage, including the sow omen, the straits, Scylla, Charybdis, Cumae, and the Sibyl. Helenus and Andromache provide gifts and advice before Aeneas, Anchises, Ascanius, and the Trojans depart from Epirus and sail along Sicily and Italy. On Cyclopian shores, Achaemenides is rescued after being abandoned in Polyphemus’s cave, and the Trojans escape the Cyclopes. Aeneas finally reaches Drepanum, where Anchises dies after their many hardships.

BOOK IV

Dido confesses to Anna that she loves Aeneas, though she remains bound by her vows to the dead Sichaeus. Anna encourages the relationship, and Dido and Aeneas become united in a cave during a storm arranged by Juno, after which Fame spreads reports of their affair. Jupiter sends Mercury to command Aeneas to leave Carthage and fulfill his fate of founding a future empire in Ausonia and Latium for Ascanius and Rome.

The pious prince was seiz’d with sudden fear;

Aeneas secretly prepares the Trojan fleet to leave the Tyrian court after Jove commands him to seek the promised Latian lands. Dido discovers his plan, confronts him, and pleads with him to stay, but Aeneas says that fate, the gods, and the divine command compel his departure. Dido curses Aeneas and sends Anna to ask for a delay, yet Aeneas remains firm while the Trojans ready their ships. Dido is left overwhelmed by grief and facing death.

The wretched queen, pursued by cruel fate,

Dido, devastated by Aeneas’s departure, prepares secret funeral rites and resolves to die. Aeneas, warned by Hermes, orders the Trojans to sail away before dawn, and Dido curses him, his people, and their descendants. Dido kills herself on the funeral pile with Aeneas’s sword, while Anna discovers the deception and tries unsuccessfully to save her. Juno sends Iris to cut Dido’s fatal hair, releasing her soul from her body.

BOOK V

Aeneas and the Trojan fleet are driven by a storm from the sea to Sicily, where Acestes welcomes them. Aeneas honors Anchises with sacrifices at his tomb and announces funeral games, including a naval race and footrace. Cloanthus wins the naval race after appealing to the sea gods, while Euryalus wins the footrace with Nisus’s help; Aeneas distributes prizes to the contestants. The Trojans later sail toward Italy, and Palinurus warns that the storm makes their original course impossible.

The race thus ended, and rewards bestow’d,

At the funeral games, Dares challenges Entellus to a gauntlet fight but is defeated after Entellus recovers from a fall and overwhelms him. Entellus wins the bull, sacrifices it to Eryx, and renounces further combat. In the archery contest, Eurytion kills the dove, while Acestes receives the prize after his arrow burns through the sky as an omen. Aeneas then orders Ascanius and the Trojan boys to perform a mounted military display, a game that Ascanius later teaches to the Latins.

Thus far the sacred sports they celebrate:

Juno sends Iris to incite the Trojan matrons to burn Aeneas’s fleet, but Jove’s storm extinguishes the fire after four ships are destroyed. Nautes and the shade of Anchises advise Aeneas to leave the old and fearful behind in Sicily and continue toward Italy, while founding a settlement called Acesta, later Acesta, under Acestes. Venus asks Neptune to protect Aeneas’s voyage, and Neptune promises that the fleet will reach Latium, though one destined man will perish. Sleep then overcomes Palinurus, who is thrown into the sea, and Aeneas takes the helm as the fleet passes the Sirens’ cliffs.

BOOK VI

Aeneas reaches the Cumaean shore and, guided by the Sibyl of Cumae, seeks Apollo’s prophecy about the Trojan quest for Italy. Apollo speaks through the Sibyl, foretelling wars in Italy and advising Aeneas to obtain the golden bough and bury the dead Misenus before entering the underworld. Aeneas finds the bough with the aid of Venus’s doves, while the Trojans perform Misenus’s funeral and prepare sacrifices. Aeneas and the Sibyl then begin their descent toward the underworld.

Ye realms, yet unreveal’d to human sight,

Aeneas and the Sibyl enter the Underworld so that Aeneas may seek Anchises in the Elysian grove. They cross Acheron with Charon after displaying the golden bough, and they pass Cerberus, the judgment of souls, the mournful fields, and the spirits of the dead. Aeneas encounters Palinurus, Dido, and Deiphobus, while the Sibyl explains the fates of the buried and unburied dead and describes the punishments in Tartarus. At last they reach Pluto’s palace, where the Sibyl fixes the golden bough before they proceed.

Where long extended plains of pleasure lay:

Aeneas and the Sibyl enter the Elysian fields, where they meet Anchises and learn about the souls’ purification, forgetfulness, and return to mortal bodies. Anchises shows Aeneas the future Italian race, including Silvius, Romulus, Julius Caesar, Augustus, and many Roman leaders, and foretells Rome’s greatness and imperial rule. He also reveals the brief life and early death of the younger Marcellus, then explains Rome’s future wars and customs before sending Aeneas through the ivory gate to his ships.

BOOK VII

Aeneas and the Trojans reach Latium by way of the Tiber and recognize the place as the destined end of their wanderings. Latinus receives the Trojan embassy, grants the newcomers land and hospitality, and declares that fate requires his daughter Lavinia to marry a foreign prince. Juno, angered by the Trojans’ success, resolves to delay the marriage and stir war among the Trojans, Latins, and Rutulians. The unit introduces the prophecies and portents that foretell a foreign ruler, Lavinia’s marriage, and the coming conflict in Italy.

Thus having said, she sinks beneath the ground,

Juno sends Alecto to inflame Amata and Turnus against the proposed marriage of Lavinia and the Trojan guest. Alecto drives Amata into madness, incites the Latian women, and persuades Turnus to take up arms. After Ascanius wounds the household stag, fighting breaks out between the countrymen and the Trojans, and Almon and Galesus are killed. The Latian king refuses to authorize the war, but Juno opens the gates of war, causing the cities of Italy to prepare for battle.

And now the mighty labour is begun

The passage catalogs the Italian leaders and forces assembled to aid Turnus against the Trojans. Mezentius brings his host from Etruria, while his son Lausus rides beside him; other leaders include Aventinus, Messapus, Clausus, Halesus, Ufens, Umbro, Virbius, and Camilla. It describes the origins, appearance, arms, and territories of their troops, including the large Sabine forces led by Clausus and the diverse army gathered under Turnus. Camilla leads the Volscians as a swift and impressive warrior woman, choosing warfare over spinning and weaving.

BOOK VIII

Turnus prepares the Latian forces for war and sends Venulus to seek Diomede’s aid, while Aeneas seeks support from Evander. Tiber appears to Aeneas, prophesies the white sow and thirty piglets, and guides his ships safely to Evander’s settlement, Pallanteum. Evander welcomes Aeneas, recalls his friendship with Anchises, promises troops, and sends Pallas with him. Evander then explains the annual Hercules festival and recounts Hercules’s slaying of Cacus, before showing Aeneas the early sites associated with Rome.

Now night had shed her silver dews around,

Venus persuades Vulcan to forge divine armor for Aeneas, and Vulcan directs the Cyclops to make it. Evander offers Aeneas the support of the Tuscans and sends Pallas with him, while Aeneas receives a heavenly sign that the armor and aid are destined for war against Turnus. Venus presents Aeneas with the completed armor, including a shield depicting Rome’s future history from its beginnings through Augustus’s victory at Actium. Aeneas and his forces then depart to join the Tuscan army.

BOOK IX

Juno sends Iris to tell Turnus that Aeneas has left the Trojan camp, and Turnus leads the Rutulians in an assault. Turnus and his forces burn the Trojan ships, but the ships are transformed by the Mother of the Gods into sea nymphs. The Trojans defend their camp while Nisus and Euryalus propose secretly leaving to summon Aeneas. Ascanius and the Trojan council approve their mission, honor their courage, and equip them for the journey.

Thus arm’d they went. The noble Trojans wait

Nisus and Euryalus conduct a nocturnal raid on the sleeping enemy host, killing many soldiers and taking spoils. As dawn approaches, they leave the camp, but Euryalus is exposed by the moonlight shining on Messapus's helmet and is pursued by Volscens's three hundred horse. Nisus returns to defend Euryalus, kills Sulmo, Tagus, and Volscens, but cannot prevent Euryalus's death. Nisus then dies beside Euryalus, and their friendship is praised as deserving immortal fame.

Now rose the ruddy morn from Tithon’s bed,

Turnus and the Volscians lead a fierce assault on the Trojan walls, while the Trojans defend their trenches, towers, and gates. Euryalus’s mother mourns when Turnus displays Euryalus’s severed head, weakening Trojan morale. Ascanius kills Numanus with his first shot in battle, but Apollo orders him to withdraw from further fighting. Turnus enters the Trojan camp through an opened gate, kills many defenders, and is eventually forced back by Mnestheus and the rallied Trojans before escaping across the river.

BOOK X

Jupiter summons the gods and forbids them to take sides in the war between the Trojans and the Latins, leaving the outcome to fate. Venus accuses Juno and the Rutulians of attacking the Trojans, while Juno blames the Trojans and Venus for bringing war into Italy; Jupiter ends their debate by declaring the Trojans and Rutulians equal before fate. Aeneas reaches the Tuscan camp, secures Tarchon’s aid, and sails with the Tuscan army to relieve the besieged Trojan camp. The Tuscan and Trojan forces land, and Aeneas begins the battle by killing several enemy warriors while Turnus organizes the opposing defense.

But, in another part, th’ Arcadian horse

Pallas rallies the fleeing Arcadians and kills several Latin warriors, including Halesus, before Turnus confronts and kills him. Turnus strips Pallas’s belt, while Aeneas, learning of Pallas’s death, cuts through the enemy and kills many warriors in vengeance. Juno saves Turnus by creating a phantom of Aeneas that lures him onto a ship, which carries him away from the battle and restores him to his father. Jupiter permits Juno to delay Turnus’s immediate death but refuses to alter his destined fate.

Meantime, by Jove’s impulse, Mezentius arm’d,

Mezentius rallies the Rutulians and kills several warriors before confronting Aeneas in battle. Aeneas wounds Mezentius, but Lausus shields his father and is killed by Aeneas. Grieving for Lausus, Mezentius returns to fight Aeneas with his horse Rhoebus, whose wounding causes Mezentius to fall. Mezentius asks for burial beside Lausus and dies by Aeneas’s hand.

BOOK XI

Aeneas honors Mezentius by erecting a trophy, grants the Latins a twelve-day truce to bury their dead, and sends Pallas’s body with a solemn procession to Evander’s city. Evander mourns Pallas and demands vengeance against Turnus, while Aeneas declares that Turnus must fight him for Lavinia and peace. In the Latian court, Drances blames Turnus for the war, but Turnus retains royal support; meanwhile, Diomedes refuses to aid the Latins, and Latinus recognizes that fate favors Aeneas’s marriage to Lavinia.

Thus, full of anxious thought, he summons all

Venulus reports to the Latian senate that Diomede refuses an alliance with the Latins and urges them to make peace, praising Aeneas as a formidable and pious warrior. The Latian king proposes giving the Trojans land along the Tiber, building ships for them, and sending gifts and senators to negotiate peace. Drances blames Turnus for the war and urges the king to give Lavinia to Aeneas, while Turnus angrily defends his conduct and offers to meet Aeneas in single combat. The council remains divided between accepting peace and continuing the war.

While they debate, nor these nor those will yield,

Aeneas leads the Trojans toward the city, causing the Latins to arm and prepare for war. Turnus organizes the defenses, orders an ambush, and assigns Camilla and the Volscians to face the Tyrrhene troops. Camilla offers to lead the fight, while Diana recounts Camilla’s upbringing and foresees her death. Diana sends Opis to avenge Camilla if she is slain and to preserve her body and arms.

She said. The faithful nymph descends from high

The Trojan and Tuscan forces battle the Latian army and Camilla’s troops, with the field repeatedly changing hands before the fighting intensifies. Camilla kills many warriors, but Aruns fatally wounds her while she pursues Chloreus. Diana’s attendant Opis then kills Aruns, after which the Volscians flee and the Latins retreat toward the city. Turnus leaves his ambush when he hears of Camilla’s death, and the Trojan and Latin armies finally face one another before nightfall.

BOOK XII

Turnus demands single combat with Aeneas, and Latinus reluctantly agrees after warning him of the dangers. Aeneas and Latinus swear a peace treaty, but Juturna stirs the Rutulians and Latins by disguising herself and using a divine omen, causing the armies to resume battle. Aeneas tries to stop the fighting and declares that he alone will settle the dispute with Turnus, but an unknown arrow strikes and wounds him.

When Turnus saw the Trojan quit the plain,

Turnus routs the Trojans and kills many warriors until Eumedes and other fighters fall before him. Aeneas is wounded, but Venus secretly heals him with dittany, allowing him to return to battle and lead an assault on the city. Juturna disguises herself as Turnus’s charioteer and keeps Aeneas from confronting him, while the armies fight across the battlefield. Aeneas attacks the city, and the queen, believing Turnus dead, hangs herself, driving the Latins into grief and confusion.

Now Turnus rolls aloof o’er empty plains,

Turnus learns that Aeneas is attacking the town and resolves to face him alone, while Juturna tries unsuccessfully to protect him. The armies withdraw as Turnus and Aeneas meet in single combat, but Turnus's borrowed sword breaks and he is eventually struck down by Aeneas. Jove orders Juno to abandon her efforts, and she secures the preservation of the Latins' name, language, and customs after peace is made. Aeneas spares Turnus until he sees Pallas's golden belt, then kills him in revenge for Pallas.

Aeneid in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Virgil’s great epic poem concerns Aeneas, culminates in Aeneas killing Turnus, and afterward recounts his marriage to Lavinia, Iulus founding Alba Longa, and Romulus and Remus founding Rome.

NISUS AND EURYALUS

The Aeneid closes with Aeneas killing Turnus. The story afterward says that Aeneas marries Lavinia, Iulus founds Alba Longa, and Romulus and Remus later found Rome.

Chapter XXVIII

The Aeneid is Virgil’s great epic poem and supplied the unit’s account of Aeneas. It is ranked next to the poems of Homer among the highest forms of epic poetry.
