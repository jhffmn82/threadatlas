Greek cities in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Greek cities are a group defined by struggles over self-government, ancient laws, liberties, democracy, exiles, and civic property. Alexander grants freedom from tribute and self-rule, while ordering most exiles restored. They defend Greek liberties and aid Darius with money for foreign soldiers. Later promises from Polyperchon offer democracies, liberties, recalled exiles, estates, and civic franchises, though some former officials face punishment. The sequence ends with Demetrius and Cassander’s peace guaranteeing ancient laws and liberties to cities in Greece and Asia.

the Greeks in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Greeks were forces in Africa under Archagathus and his commanders, composed among Agathocles's forces and at war with the Carthaginians. They initially conquered several cities under Eumachus, but at Tunis were hemmed in by land and sea without provisions. After attacking the Carthaginian camp near the river Himera, reinforcements turned the battle against them; many were killed and survivors fled. After Agathocles fled, they received three hundred talents, restored their towns, and either joined the Carthaginians or settled at Selinus.

Greeks in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · -0471

Greeks resist the Persian invasion by land and sea to defend Athens and freedom. They remain by their ships overnight, attack at dawn calling for freedom, country, families, temples, and ancestral graves, defeat Persian ships at Salamis, assault troops on a nearby islet, and destroy Mardonius’ army near Plataea. Their victories prompt a warning that Persia should not attack Greek lands again.

Greeks in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Greeks are an ancient population who, though dejected, are driven by danger to defend Greek liberty and security. They assemble an allied army at Thermopylæ against the Galatians, led by Athenians under Callippus and reinforced by contingents from Bœotia, Phocis, the Locrians, Megarians, Ætolians, Macedonians, and Syrians. They send picked light-armed soldiers and cavalry to guard the Sperchius, then fight at Thermopylæ.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XX.

The Greeks are said to have been very dejected but are induced by the urgency of the danger to fight bravely for their country. They assemble at Thermopylæ with contingents from several Greek regions, along with troops and ships from other allies, and send picked light-armed soldiers and cavalry to the Sperchius.

ancient Greeks in Musical Instruments

Carl Engel · 1875-08

Ancient Greeks possessed many musical instruments, though most constructions and capabilities remain uncertain. Their system was generally believed derived from Egypt, while more complete instruments chiefly came from Asia and Asia Minor. They favored the lyre, used kithara, aulos, syrinx, and monochord in performance and teaching, and reserved salpinx and keras for war.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IV.

The Greeks possessed many musical instruments, but the exact construction and capabilities of most remain uncertain. Their more complete instruments appear to have been chiefly of Asiatic origin, including instruments adopted from the peoples of Asia-minor. They used lyres, harps, kitharas, auloi, syrinxes, trumpets, drums, cymbals, and other instruments in performance, teaching, war, and worship.
