Medea in Medea

Euripides · -0430

Euripides’ Medea is a tragedy centered on the collapse of Medea and Jason’s relationship and on Medea’s transformation from abandoned wife into avenger. Medea, a princess and enchantress from Colchis, once helped Jason and the Argonauts, left her family and homeland for him, and bore him two children in Greece. Now Jason plans to marry Creon’s daughter, a Corinthian princess, claiming the match will bring security and royal connections. Medea sees it as betrayal and as proof of women’s dependence and vulnerability in marriage and exile. The Nurse, Attendant, and Corinthian women fear Medea’s grief and anger. Creon orders Medea and her children expelled, but she gains one day’s delay. She vows that the princess, Creon, and Jason will die, yet needs a place of refuge. When Aegeus, king of Athens, promises her protection, she can pursue revenge without fear of having nowhere to go. Medea pretends to reconcile with Jason and sends her children to the royal bride with a poisoned robe and golden carcanet. Jason accepts the apparent reconciliation and hopes the children may remain in Corinth. The gifts kill both the princess and Creon. Medea then faces the conflict between love for her children and the desire to wound Jason completely. Despite the Chorus’s horror and the children’s cries, she kills them. Jason arrives after the deaths. Medea appears above him in a winged-dragon chariot, holding the children’s bodies and denying him burial rites. She declares her vengeance complete; Jason is left grieving, childless, and unable to repair the destruction.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction presents Euripides’ Medea as a tragedy of character and situation about the collapse of Jason and Medea’s relationship rather than as a romantic adventure. It recounts how Medea, princess and enchantress of Colchis, aided Jason and the Argonauts, abandoned her family, and followed Jason to Greece, where he later accepted a Corinthian princess’s hand. The play begins when Jason’s betrayal leads Creon to condemn Medea to exile, and it develops into a study of oppression, revenge, and the destructive transformation of suffering into hatred. The introduction emphasizes the play’s intense dramatic power, its early originality, and its deliberately unsettling refusal to offer moral consolation.

MEDEA

The Nurse and the Attendant describe Medea's grief after Jason abandons her to marry Creon's daughter, and they fear that her anger may lead to violence. Medea emerges and explains to the Corinthian women the hardships of marriage, exile, and dependence on a husband, asking them not to betray her if she finds a way to punish Jason. Creon orders Medea and her two children to leave Corinth, but Medea persuades him to grant them one more day. Creon warns that Medea or either child will die if they remain after the following sunrise.

MEDEA.

Medea, facing exile after Jason’s marriage to another bride, declares that the bride, her father, and Jason will die. She considers burning the house, stabbing the wedding pair, or using poison, while fearing that she will have nowhere to escape afterward. Medea invokes Hecate and resolves to act boldly, while the Chorus and the women lament her abandonment and suggest that women may become terrible and glorious in future stories.

_Enter from the right_ JASON.

Medea confronts Jason over his betrayal, reminding him that she rescued him and sacrificed her family and homeland for his sake, while Jason defends his remarriage as a way to secure prosperity and royal connections for himself and their children. Jason offers Medea aid for her exile, but she rejects it, and he leaves after blaming her curses and anger for her suffering. Aegeus, king of Athens, then meets Medea and reveals that he is childless; he swears to receive and protect her in Athens. Medea departs from the meeting with renewed confidence in the plan she intends to carry out before leaving Corinth.

CHORUS.

Medea learns that Aegeus will provide refuge in Athens and decides to feign reconciliation with Jason so that their children can deliver poisoned gifts to the royal bride. Jason accepts Medea’s apparent change of heart and agrees to ask that the children remain in Corinth, while Medea sends them with a robe and golden carcanet. After the royal bride accepts the gifts and the children are spared exile, Medea struggles between her love for them and her determination to hurt Jason, ultimately resolving to kill them. The Chorus laments the children’s threatened deaths, and Medea awaits news of the royal bride’s fate.

[_Enter_ MESSENGER.

The Messenger reports that Medea's poisoned gifts have killed the princess and the king. Medea resolves to kill her own children, and does so despite their cries and the Chorus's attempts to stop her. Jason arrives too late, and Medea appears above him in a winged-dragon chariot with the children's bodies, refusing to let him bury them. Medea declares that vengeance for Jason's betrayal has been completed, while Jason is left childless and grieving.

Medea (play) in Medea

Euripides · -0430

Medea is a tragedy of character and situation, first produced in Athens in 432 B.C. Its concentrated, passionate action examines the end of Medea and Jason’s romance through oppression and revenge, as Medea becomes the instrument of judgment against Jason. It received third prize at its first production.

INTRODUCTION

The Medea is described as a tragedy of character and situation rather than a romantic play. It focuses on the aftermath of Jason and Medea’s romance, presenting an intensely passionate study of oppression and revenge in which Medea becomes the instrument of judgment against Jason.
