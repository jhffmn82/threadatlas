Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, Argos is an Achaean homeland that sends eighty ships under Diomed, Sthenelus, and Euryalus, and to which Agamemnon proposes returning. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Argos is a sacred, horse-breeding city and land of Danaos, home of Hera, praised for its sacrifices, games, victories, people, and heroes. In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Pelasgian land protects the suppliant Danaids, while the Chorus prays that it will escape war, disease, corpses, and civic bloodshed. In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Argos is a kingdom and land, the ancestral home and refuge of Io’s descendants, Danaus, and the Danaids; it shelters the Danaids and later sends an alien armament against a city. In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Argos is Agamemnon’s fatherland and realm, receives news of Troy’s fall, suffers under Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, and is freed by Orestes. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Argos supports Polynices through his marriage to Adrastus’s child and the friendly army he raises there against Thebes. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Argive land is the place the Fury will visit to bring swift recompense. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Argos is the Greek city and ancestral home where Orestes is tried, its citizens condemn him and Electra, and its armies and rulers act in conflicts involving Thebes and the Heraclidae.

In Hecuba and other plays, Argos is hostile to Athens, ruled by Eurystheus, and expels or pursues the children of Hercules while supplying forces against Athens and their supporters. In The Electra of Euripides, Argos is the land, kingdom, and city containing the house of Atreus, where Aegisthus and Clytemnestra rule and Orestes must reclaim his fatherland. In The Trojan Women of Euripides: Cassandra is assigned to go to Argos as Agamemnon's bride. She calls herself a bride going to the king's bed there. In Iphigenia in Tauris, Argos is the ancestral homeland and royal home of Orestes and Iphigenia, from which they are separated and to which they hope to return. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Argos is governed by a democracy during violent conflict between popular leaders and the city’s great men, resulting in many prominent deaths. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Argos receives settlers, Polynices, and Tydeus, raises forces against Thebes, fights Sparta, endures an oligarchic coup, and gains Athenian support. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Argos is the kingdom of which Inachus was the first king, and Io is identified as his daughter. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Argos is Myscelos’s native city, whose law forbids citizens to leave without the magistrates’ permission. In The Library, Argos is a refuge and kingdom linked with Argus, Inachus, and the Peloponnese; it receives Tydeus and Polynices and is associated with the rulers Acrisius, Perseus, and Megapenthes. In Description of Greece, Argolis includes Sicyon after its people become Dorians, and its coast includes Epidaurus and Trœzen. In Description of Greece, Argos is a Peloponnesian city whose history includes royal divisions, wars with the Lacedæmonians, defense by Telesilla, democracy, and membership in the Achæan league. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Argos is a town important in the heroic age, associated with Danaus, the Achæans and Dorians, Creugas’s statue, and a road from Tegea. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Argolis is connected to Arcadia by mountain passes, and the Inachus flows through it as the Argive river. In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone: Argos shelters the exiled Polyneices, who marries Adrastus’ child, gains allies and armed support there, then gathers an army from it to attack Thebes.

Abstract written by a model

Argos is a Greek city, land, and ancestral home: the setting where Orestes is tried, the home of the Argive royal house, and the country remembered by Iphigenia, Orestes, and Pylades. It contains an Argive assembly and is later said to be named after Cyclopes. Its political life matters most. After the murder of Clytæmnestra, it excludes Orestes and Electra, watches and tries them, and prepares to decide whether Orestes will die; its citizens threaten stoning and condemn them. The records portray its people as able to deliberate well under good leaders but dangerous under evil or intemperate speakers. Though Menelaus cannot overcome it by force with his few allies, Apollo’s settlement orders Menelaus to let Orestes reign there; elsewhere, while Orestes is exiled, Menelaus rules it. Agamemnon wants Clytaemnestra to return there, yet fears the Greeks would ravage it if he fled there. In the final records, Iphigenia mourns it as her ancestral city, seeks to send a letter there, and wants Orestes returned; Orestes nevertheless says he is exiled from Argos.

Argos in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Argos first appears

Abstract written by a model

Argos is a city in the Peloponnese, named after Argos, the daughter’s son of Phoroneus. It became the kingdom of Acrisius after the division by the sons of Abas; Perseus later exchanged it with Megapenthes after killing Acrisius. Its kingdom was divided among three royal lines before Orestes acquired it with Sparta, and the Heraclidæ expelled Tisamenus. Argos later figured in conflict with the Lacedæmonians over Cynuria and in invasions by Cleomenes and Agesipolis. Cleomenes’ defeat of the Argives left it nearly without men, but Telesilla organized its defense. Under Aristomachus, Aratus helped establish democracy and bring Argos into the Achæan league. Pyrrhus entered while pursuing fugitives, and dispersed fighting followed in its temples, houses, and alleys. The city and its roads to Tegea, Mantinea, Lyrceia, Orneæ, Epidaurus, and Mycenæ are marked by temples, tombs, monuments, ruins, sacred places, and Argive traditions.

CHAPTER XIII.

Pyrrhus arrived at Argos while Antigonus was preparing to move against Spartan territory. After being victorious, Pyrrhus pursued fugitives into the city, where his forces became dispersed and fought in temples, houses, and alleys.

CHAPTER VI.

Argos was ruled by Aristomachus when Aratus persuaded him to establish a democracy and join the Achæan league. Adrastus had earlier ruled at Sicyon after fleeing Argos and later returned there.

CHAPTER XII.

Argos was named after Argos, the daughter’s son of Phoroneus, and later became the kingdom of Acrisius. Perseus exchanged it with Megapenthes after unintentionally killing Acrisius, and the Argives eventually destroyed Mycenæ.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Argos was reached by a road from Mycenæ marked by the tomb of Thyestes, Mysia, the river Inachus, and the gate of Ilithyia. Its kingdom was once divided among three royal lines, but Orestes eventually acquired Argos and ruled it together with Sparta. The Heraclidæ later expelled Tisamenus from Argos.

CHAPTER XIX.

Argos contains the temples, tombs, monuments, and market-place described throughout the unit. It was left nearly without men after Cleomenes and the Lacedæmonians defeated the Argives, but Telesilla organized its defense.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Argos contains the Hollow Way, temples of Dionysus, Æsculapius, and Aphrodite, and several tombs and other monuments. The narrator records and disputes various Argive traditions about figures and objects connected with the city.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Argos has roads leading to Tegea, Mantinea, Lyrceia, Orneæ, and Epidaurus, with numerous temples, tombs, ruins, and sacred places along them. Argive traditions describe the city’s dealings with Œneus, Lynceus, Orneæ, Tiryns, and Epidaurus, and say that the Argives dispossessed the inhabitants of Orneæ and Tiryns. Argos is also associated with the Danaids, whose annual torch procession commemorates the signals exchanged by Hypermnestra and Lynceus.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Argos was involved in repeated conflict with the Lacedæmonians, including wars over Cynuria and later invasions under Cleomenes and Agesipolis.

Argos is a town important in the days of the so-called heroes. Archander and Architeles arrived from Phthiotis, became sons-in-law of Danaus, and the population gained the Achæan name while Argives were privately called Danai. The Dorians expelled the Achæans, and Argos later lost good fortune with the overthrow of the Dorians. It has Creugas’s statue in Lycian Apollo’s temple and is reached by a road from Tegea near Hysiæ.

Argos was the place where Archander and Architeles, sons of Achæus, arrived from Phthiotis and became sons-in-law of Danaus. The Achæan name became attached to the population of Argos and Lacedæmon, although the Argives were privately called Danai, until the Dorians expelled the Achæans.

CHAPTER XL.

Argos had a statue of Creugas, which stood in the temple of Lycian Apollo.

CHAPTER LIV.

A high road leads from Tegea to Argos and passes temples of Æsculapius, Pythian Apollo, Demeter of Corythes, and Mystic Dionysus. After crossing Mount Parthenium, the road reaches Hysiæ in Argolis, identified as the boundary between the districts of Tegea and Argos.
