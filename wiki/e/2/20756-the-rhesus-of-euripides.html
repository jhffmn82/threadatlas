Murray’s introduction asks whether Rhesus is genuinely by Euripides or by a later anonymous poet. As dawn nears, Odysseus and Diomede enter on a night raid. Unable to find Hector, they receive Athena’s direction to kill Rhesus and seize his white horses. Athena deceives Paris so that the Greeks can escape. The Thracian camp is struck while asleep: Rhesus is killed and his horses taken. His wounded Charioteer initially blames Hector, but Hector attributes the deed to Odysseus. It was released as a Project Gutenberg eBook on February 4, 2011, with a most recent update on January 7, 2021. The printed edition was published in London by George Allen & Company, Ltd., in 1913.

INTRODUCTION

The introduction examines whether Rhesus was written by Euripides or by an anonymous later poet, weighing manuscript, performance, stylistic, and linguistic evidence. It argues that the play may be an early, pro-satyric work by Euripides later revised or reproduced by Euripides the Younger, while noting that most scholars favor a fourth-century archaistic origin. The play's style is compared especially with Alcestis and Cyclops, and its unusual atmosphere is linked to tragedy near the limits of the tragic convention. Its plot comes from the Doloneia, and Rhesus is presented as a divine Thracian king associated with Pangaion and with traditions known to the Athenians.

RHESUS

After the Trojans and their allies win a decisive victory, Hector learns that beacons and disorder in the Greek camp may signal a retreat. Aeneas persuades Hector to send a spy rather than immediately attack, and Dolon volunteers to infiltrate the Greek lines in a wolf disguise in exchange for Achilles' horses. A shepherd then reports that Rhesus has arrived from Thrace with a large, richly equipped army to aid Troy. Hector reluctantly welcomes Rhesus as a war-friend while the Trojan guards celebrate his arrival.

_Enter_ RHESUS _in dazzling white armour, followed by his_ [vv. 379-395]

Rhesus arrives at Troy with his Thracian army, boasting that he will defeat the Greeks, burn their ships, and end Troy's suffering within two days. Hector rebukes Rhesus for arriving late, while Rhesus explains that Scythian attacks delayed him and renews his promise to fight. After Hector and Rhesus leave, the Trojan guards change watches as dawn approaches. Odysseus and Diomede enter the camp during the Argive night raid, fail to find Hector, and are instructed by Athena to kill Rhesus and take his white horses before morning.

_Enter_ PARIS.

Athena deceives Paris by assuring him that Troy is safe while Odysseus and Diomede escape after raiding the Thracian camp, killing Rhesus and taking his horses. The wounded Thracian Charioteer reports that the attackers struck the sleeping army, and he accuses Hector of arranging Rhesus's death, though Hector insists that Odysseus was responsible. The Muse appears with Rhesus's body, curses Odysseus, Diomede, Helen, and Athena, and declares that Rhesus will be freed from ordinary burial and live as a spirit. Hector orders the dead buried, promises care for the wounded charioteer, and prepares Troy and its allies for battle at dawn.

NOTES

The notes explain how the Rhesus play presupposes the Iliad and closely follows the Doloneia, placing its action in the Trojan camp during the Trojan War. Hector, Dolon, Rhesus, Odysseus, Diomedes, Athena, the Guards, and the Greek spies are discussed in relation to the night mission, the killing of Dolon, and the death of Rhesus. The notes also examine the play's staging, its treatment of the Trojans, Greeks, Lycians, and Thracians, and its mixture of heroic, barbaric, lyrical, and Euripidean elements. The ending presents Rhesus's hidden burial and the Trojans returning to continued struggle under the shadow of disaster.

Abstract written by a model

The Rhesus of Euripides is a Greek tragedy and Project Gutenberg electronic work: a play translated by Gilbert Murray into English rhyming verse, with his explanatory notes, from a 1913 London edition. It is attributed to Euripides in manuscripts and ancient performance records, though its introduction emphasizes its early, severe, direct, grandiose style, rare words, Euripidean expressions, and imitation of Aeschylus; it also records three alternative openings and later reproductions. Closely based on and following the Doloneia, it presupposes knowledge of the Iliad and is set through one night in the Trojan camp. The action begins with the camp’s watch and the Watchers’ Song, includes the mission of Dolon and the arrival of Rhesus, a divine Thracian king of radiant martial splendour, son of the greatest River and a nameless Muse of the Mountain. Odysseus and Diomedes then attack; Rhesus’s death is central, alongside guards, spies, wolf-skin tokens, and divine appearances. The play ends with the Trojans’ final departure.

front matter

The Rhesus of Euripides is presented as a play translated into English rhyming verse with explanatory notes. Its printed edition was published in London by George Allen & Company, Ltd., in 1913.

INTRODUCTION

Rhesus appears in the manuscripts of Euripides, and the Athenian Didascaliae record that Euripides wrote a play of that name. Its style is severe, direct, grandiose, and unusually rich in rare words and strong color; the introduction compares it especially with Alcestis and Cyclops. Rhesus himself is presented as a divine Thracian king, son of a great River and the Muse of the Mountain, whose splendour, martial character, and death place him within traditions of heroic kingship.

NOTES

The Rhesus play takes place during a night in the Trojan camp and dramatizes the arrival of Rhesus, the mission of Dolon, and the attack by Odysseus and Diomedes. Its staging includes conjectural movements of Guards, spies, wolf-skin tokens, divine appearances, and the final departure of the Trojans.
