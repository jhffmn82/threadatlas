In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, this expedition was organized after the Persian defeat at Marathon and carried Xerxes from Susa through Sardis and into Europe. Persian invasion of Greece in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Persian invasion of Greece first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Persian invasion of Greece was a war between the Greeks and Persians, led by Xerxes and devastating Greece by land and sea. It was coordinated with the Carthaginian attack on Sicily. Greek resistance included Leonidas’s fight at Thermopylae, where Xerxes escaped a possible fatal attack and Leonidas’s remaining forces were destroyed. Xerxes’ forces ravaged Phocia, Boeotia, and Attica, destroyed Platea, and burned Athens and its temples, forcing Athenians from Attica. Persian attacks on the Greek fleet were weakened by storms and defeat at Salamis. The Greek confederacy defeated Mardonius at Plataea, while Themistocles was praised for preserving Greece and dividing enemy land forces by destroying a bridge. The war ended after two years with Mycale, Sestos’s capture, and Greek forces returning home.

During the invasion, Xerxes' forces devastated Phocia, Boeotia, and Attica, destroyed Platea, and burned Athens and its temples. Persian forces also attacked the Grecian fleet, but storms and the defeat at Salamis weakened their campaign.

CHAP. U.

The Persian invasion of Greece was coordinated with the Carthaginian attack on Sicily. The same day as Gelon's victory at Himera, Leonidas and the Greeks fought at Thermopylae; later, news of the Greek victory at Salamis caused Gelon to abandon his plan to send forces to Greece.

CHAP. HI.

The Persian invasion led to the destruction of Athens and its temples and forced the Athenians to abandon Attica. The invasion was resisted by the Greek confederacy, which defeated Mardonius at Plataea.

CHAP. IV.

The Medean war ended after two years with the battle of Mycale, the taking of Sestos, and the return of the Lacedaemonians and Athenians to their homes.

CHAP. VII.

Themistocles was praised for opposing the combined strength of Asia, defeating the Persians, preserving Greece during the war, and using a stratagem involving the destruction of a bridge to divide the enemy's land forces.

Persian invasion of Greece in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Persian invasion of Greece first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Persian invasion of Greece brought a barbarian landing at Marathon and Xerxes’ advance. Miltiades checked the Persian host; Greek forces under Leonidas gathered at Thermopylæ, though the Achæans stayed home. Leonidas fought there, while Themistocles and the Athenians fought at Salamis; Themistocles’ two sea-fights expelled Xerxes, followed by allied action at Platæa.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

During the invasion of Greece by Xerxes and the Medes, the Achæans did not join Leonidas at Thermopylæ, the forces of Themistocles and the Athenians at Salamis, or the allied forces at Platæa. The text suggests that they stayed behind to protect their country.

CHAPTER LII.

The Persian invasion is represented by the barbarian landing at Marathon and by Xerxes' advance into Greece. Miltiades checked the Persian host, while Leonidas at Thermopylæ and Themistocles in two sea-fights expelled Xerxes from Greece.

CHAPTER XX.

During the Persian invasion, Greek forces gathered at Thermopylæ under Leonidas and included contingents from Lacedæmon, Arcadia, Phocis, Locri, and other places. The text estimates the total force employed against the Persians at 11,200, although not all remained at Thermopylæ throughout the battle.
