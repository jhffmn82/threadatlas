Xerxes in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Xerxes is presented chiefly as a king of Persia and commander of its army and vast navy. Encouraged by Mardonius and aware of Carthaginian preparations, he resolved to invade Greece, assembled a huge force, crossed the Hellespont, marched through Thrace, and pressed repeated attacks at Thermopylae. After gaining the passes, he ordered Megabates against the Grecian fleet and led his army through Phocia, Boeotia, and Attica, where towns, temples, and Athens were destroyed. During a tumult he escaped his pavilion as Greeks entered it and killed those found there. His invasion ultimately failed: after Salamis he withdrew to Asia, persuaded that the bridge might be destroyed, leaving Mardonius with four hundred thousand men; after Plataea and Mycale he fled toward Ecbatana with part of his forces. Later Greek victories overcame him, while the earlier defenders' stand is said to have served Greek liberty even more. He received, acquitted, and honored Themistocles, granting him Magnesia, Myus, and Lampsacus; one account says he sought to renew war and asked Themistocles to lead it. He was father of Darius and Artaxerxes. Records say he was murdered through Artabanus and Mithridates, after which Egypt revolted, and give conflicting succession and reign accounts.

Xerxes in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · -0471

Xerxes is a Persian king, son of Darius and Atossa, and royal heir. Young and reckless, he leads the Persian invasion of Greece and Hellas against Athens, commanding its army and fleet. He builds a bridge across the Hellespont to move his army, but his fleet is defeated at Salamis. Xerxes survives while witnessing the fleet’s destruction and the slaughter of chosen troops, then tears his royal robe, orders retreat, and flees in shame. He leaves Mardonius with three hundred thousand men; the army left behind is later destroyed near Plataea. Returning toward Susa in torn clothing with a nearly empty quiver, he mourns his dead commanders and soldiers and joins the Chorus in lamenting Persia’s defeat before entering the palace.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Xerxes is portrayed in The Persians as an undignified and insufficiently manly returning ruler after the defeat at Salamis. He reappears at the end of the play, and the drama closes with his melancholy procession into the palace of Susa.

THE PERSIANS

Xerxes, son of Darius and Atossa, leads the invasion of Hellas to avenge Darius’ defeat at Marathon. His fleet is defeated at Salamis, he returns toward Susa in a sorry state, and his army left behind is destroyed near Plataea. Atossa dreams of him being thrown from a chariot and mourning after seeing Darius.

Enter a MESSENGER.

Xerxes survives the battle but sees the destruction of his fleet and the slaughter of his chosen troops on the islet near Salamis. He rends his robe, cries out at the disaster, orders the land force to retreat, and flees in shame.

The GHOST OF DARIUS rises.

Xerxes returns in disarray, wearing torn remnants of his royal clothing and carrying a nearly empty quiver. He mourns the destruction of his army and names many commanders and soldiers who died, especially in the fighting and wreck at Salamis. He leads the Chorus in repeated cries, breast-beating, and lamentation for Persia’s defeat.
