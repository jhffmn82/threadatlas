England in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

England is the narrator’s Motherland, a country rooted in native landscape, national life, language, the English Church, and Constitution. Its countryside, woodlands, pastures, fields, rivers, villages, markets, hedgerows, and midland plains, formed the narrator’s early understanding of human life and remains affectionately significant. England is marked by stability, historical continuity, and a conservative spirit, while also changing gradually in institutions, technology, labor, and political attitudes without great convulsions. Its central shared principle is refusal of foreign rule, with national life defended even through improvised resistance. At the same time, hospitality and freedom are presented as world-wide blessings, making exclusion of peaceful foreigners inconsistent with its interests.

England in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

England is the place that supplied the works, reputation, social conditions, and literary responses through which Sterne became known elsewhere. After the first volumes of Tristram Shandy appeared in 1760, Sterne’s fame rose rapidly there. German commentators later presented England as a leading source of literary models, valuing conditions thought favorable to individual development, humor, eccentricity, and novelistic material. German periodicals initially treated Sterne’s popularity as British taste, using English notices and translations; England also produced investigations, debates over his unacknowledged borrowings, and imitations, continuations, explanations, and protests that reached Germany. Yet England’s enthusiasm began to be reconsidered, with Sturz encountering an early reaction in 1768; after Sterne’s death, opinion became more critical or forgetful while his German reputation reached its height.
