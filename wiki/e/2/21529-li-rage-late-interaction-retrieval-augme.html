“LI-RAGE: Late Interaction Retrieval Augmented Generation with Explicit Signals for Open-Domain Table Question Answering” is an ACL 2023 short paper by Weizhe Lin, Rexhina Blloshmi, Bill Byrne, Adrià de Gispert, and Gonzalo Iglesias. It presents LI-RAGE, an end-to-end open-domain TableQA framework that retrieves tables from a corpus and generates answers grounded in their cells. The paper begins by identifying weaknesses in standard retriever-reader pipelines: dense retrievers often reduce an entire question or heterogeneous table to one vector, RAG-style training gives weak implicit guidance to retrieval, and readers may poorly rank answers even when tables are gold. LI-RAGE addresses these issues with a ColBERT-based late-interaction retriever, which retains token-level question-table interactions rather than relying only on single-vector comparisons. It jointly trains retrieval and answer generation with RAGE loss to provide explicit table-level relevance signals. The reader also prefixes generated answers with binary “yes” or “no” relevance tokens, allowing unreliable retrieved tables to be filtered at inference time. The methodology explains table linearization for retrieval and reading, token-level question and table encodings, relevance scoring, answer generation for retrieved tables, and joint refinement from both table relevance and answer prediction. Experiments on NQ-TABLES and E2E-WTQ compare LI-RAGE with DPR-RAGE, RAG, BM25, and literature baselines, using Recall@K for retrieval and Token F1, exact match, or accuracy for answer quality. LI-RAGE reports the strongest evaluated end-to-end results on both datasets. Results attribute much of the gain to late interaction; joint training improves retrieval and TableQA, while removing relevance tokens substantially reduces performance. The revised RAGE loss, which omits a penalty for documents without gold answers, also improves stability and performance. Supplementary material details datasets, hyperparameters, indexing with FAISS and PLAID, dynamic retrieval, comparisons involving CLTR and T-RAG, and computational costs. LI indexing and joint training are slower than DPR alternatives, though inference rises only from 1.22 to 1.40 seconds per iteration. The paper closes by noting limits: evaluation covers only two datasets, especially a relatively small E2E-WTQ; questions are mainly look-ups rather than multi-cell operations such as SUM or MAX; and Transformer token limits constrain long tables. The responsible-NLP checklist notes public datasets, no human participants, and reporting of limitations, statistics, parameters, and infrastructure.

Abstract

Recent open-domain TableQA models are typically built as retriever-reader pipelines, with retrieval often based on Dense Passage Retriever variants that compare single representations of questions and tables. The text proposes late interaction models to capture finer-grained interactions between question and table embeddings during retrieval. It also introduces joint training of the retriever and reader using explicit table-level signals, and a binary relevance token prefixed to the reader’s generated answer to identify unreliable tables at inference time. Together, these strategies achieve state-of-the-art performance on two public open-domain TableQA datasets.

Introduction

The introduction concerns Open-domain Table Question Answering and presents LI-RAGE, an end-to-end framework for answering questions grounded in tables. It identifies information loss from encoding large, heterogeneous tables into single vectors, weak implicit retriever guidance from RAG loss, and imperfect reader ranking of answers from gold tables. The proposed approach uses ColBERT as a Latent Interaction retriever, RAGE loss for explicit table-level signals, and binary relevance tokens in reader training. LI-RAGE achieves state-of-the-art results on NQ-TABLES and E2E-WTQ.

Related Work

This unit reviews prior work on table retrieval, TableQA, and open-domain TableQA. Table retrieval methods use Transformers to model tabular structure or linearize tables, while TableQA systems have evolved from executable logical forms to classification and autoregressive answer generation. Open-domain TableQA systems commonly use retriever-reader pipelines, including DTR, T-RAG, and CLTR. The unit introduces an approach centered on late interaction retrievers that retrieve whole tables and explicitly refine the retriever during end-to-end TableQA training.

Methodology / Table Retrieval .. Learned Table Relevance

The methodology concerns retrieving a gold table from a table corpus and generating answer denotations from one or more cells. It uses ColBERT to encode questions and tables at the token level and rank retrieved tables with a relevance score. A TableQA model generates answers from each retrieved table, while joint training adjusts retrieval using both table relevance and answer prediction. Learned table relevance adds a binary “yes” or “no” token to help prioritize reliable answer sources.

Experimental Setup

The unit describes experiments comparing table retrieval and TableQA systems on NQ-TABLES and E2E-WTQ. It evaluates answer quality with Token F1, exact match or accuracy, and retrieval with Recall@K, using K=5 for the main retrieval setting. The systems include DPR-RAGE, LI-RAGE, RAG, BM25, and literature baselines, with LI-RAGE achieving the strongest reported end-to-end results among the evaluated systems. Ablations show the effects of joint training and relevance tokens, while LI-RAGE generally outperforms DPR-RAGE in both end-to-end performance and retrieval.

Results and Discussions / Main Results .. Remarks of Design Rationale

LI-RAGE achieves the best reported performance on NQ-TABLES and E2E-WTQ, with gains attributed largely to the LI retriever. Joint training improves both retrieval and end-to-end TableQA performance, while removing binary relevance tokens substantially harms results. Relevance tokens help filter irrelevant tables when more tables are retrieved, outperforming strategies based on retrieval or combined retrieval and answer confidence scores. The modified RAGE loss removes a term that penalizes documents lacking gold answers, which improves retriever stability and TableQA performance.

Conclusion

The text introduces LI-RAGE, a novel open-domain TableQA framework. LI-RAGE uses late interaction retrievers to support finer-grained interaction between questions and tables. It also incorporates the RAGE loss and binary relevance tokens. These components enable significant improvements over the state of the art in two challenging TableQA tasks.

Limitations

The limitations concern the proposed system and its evaluation on two open-domain TableQA datasets, including the relatively small E2E-WTQ. The datasets mainly contain look-up questions and do not adequately cover complex operations involving multiple cells, such as SUM, MAX, MIN, and SUBTRACT. The proposed system is also constrained by the token length limit of modern Transformer models, especially as tables become longer. Future improvements may involve sampling relevant rows and columns or increasing Transformer capacity.

CLTR and T-RAG Evaluation

The unit evaluates retrieval and end-to-end performance for CLTR and T-RAG on open-domain TableQA datasets, including E2E-WTQ. Because each question has one gold table, Recall@K is considered more appropriate than Precision@K, whose upper bound is 1/K under table-level retrieval. CLTR and T-RAG decomposed tables into individual rows and columns, and their reported Precision@K values are not directly comparable with other setups. The unit also reports computational costs for DPR, LI, DPR-RAGE, and LI-RAGE, along with fine-tuning hyperparameters for tapex-large on WikiTableQuestions.

Indexing and Dynamic Retrieval

The unit describes indexing and dynamic retrieval for DPR and LI-RAGE. One-dimensional table embeddings are pre-extracted with a DPR model fine-tuned for retrieval and indexed in FAISS for fast nearest-neighbor search. During LI-RAGE training, question embeddings are dynamically extracted and used to retrieve high-scoring tables. It also explains that ColBERTv2 is integrated into the training framework, with PLAID enabling fast dynamic retrieval during end-to-end joint training.

Computational Cost

The unit reports the computational cost of LI, DPR, LI-RAGE, and DPR-RAGE. LI training time is not significantly higher than DPR training because both use contrastive learning, but LI index building takes around five minutes compared with 40 seconds for DPR. LI-RAGE has longer end-to-end training due to slower dynamic retrieval and refinement using larger multi-dimensional embeddings. Its inference speed increases only slightly, from 1.22 to 1.40 seconds per iteration, suggesting limited additional computation in real-world deployment.
