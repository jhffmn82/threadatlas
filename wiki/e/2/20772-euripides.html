Euripides

person · 8 works · 24 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Enripides, Eurip., author, his, its author, our author, our poet, the most tragic of the poets, the poet

Abstract written by a model

In The Rhesus of Euripides, Euripides is presented as the author and playwright associated with Rhesus, Alcestis, and Peliades. In Alcestis, Euripides is the poet who wrote Alcestis and reshaped its traditional story through psychological analysis into refined, pathetic comedy. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Euripides is the author whose plays and poetry are examined through textual, linguistic, stylistic, and authenticity notes. In Medea, Euripides is the poet, sceptic, and devotee of truth whose austere realism exposes tragedy beneath heroic romance. In The Electra of Euripides, Euripides is the playwright who wrote Electra, intellectually extended Aeschylus, and portrayed Apollo and mother-murder negatively. In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Euripides is the author of Troaedes who shaped it around conquered women, later went to Macedonia, wrote Bacchae, and died there. In Iphigenia in Tauris, Euripides is the writer of Iphigenia in Tauris and Iphigenia in Aulis. In Description of Greece, Euripides is regarded as one of the great lights of Tragedy, with a statue among tragic and comic dramatists in the theatre.

Euripides in The Rhesus of Euripides

Euripides · 454 to 439 BC · the work’s page · read it where Euripides first appears

Abstract written by a model

Euripides is presented as an author and playwright associated with Rhesus, Alcestis, and Peliades, the last credited with his first victory. He is identified in front matter and by manuscripts, performance records, and early Alexandrian quotations as author of a Rhesus, though its style has prompted doubts. An introduction proposes an early pro-satyric work later reproduced or adapted after his death, possibly involving his son Euripides the Younger. Notes associate his work with forceful speeches, psychological conflict, lyricism, and daring, sometimes crude technique. Later listings represent him through several plays and lost tragedies.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

front matter

Euripides is identified as the author of The Rhesus of Euripides.

INTRODUCTION

Euripides is identified by the manuscripts, performance records, and early Alexandrian quotations as the author of a Rhesus, although the play's style has led to doubts about its authorship. The introduction proposes that Rhesus may be an early, pro-satyric work by Euripides, later reproduced or adapted after his death. Euripides the Younger, his son, is mentioned as a possible reviser or producer and as someone believed to have written parts of Iphigenia in Aulis.

NOTES

Euripides is associated with the play's didactic speeches, psychological controversies, sudden ferocity, barbaric characterization, lyrical passages, and daring but sometimes crude technique. The notes compare the play's scenes and ending with works including Trojan Women, Cyclops, Medea, Alcestis, Heracles, and Iphigenia among the Taurians.

front matter

Euripides is represented by Hippolytus, Bacchae, The Trojan Women, Electra, Medea, and Iphigenia in Tauris in the separately listed English rhyming-verse editions. The larger volume also includes an appendix on The Lost Tragedies of Euripides.

Euripides in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Euripides first appears

Abstract written by a model

Euripides is an author of plays and poetry whose works are preserved and examined through notes on language, readings, style, characterization, and authenticity. The records associate him with Hecuba, Medea, Bacchae, Iphigenia in Aulis, Iphigenia in Tauris, Andromache, Helen, Alcestis, Hippolytus, Orestes, and Electra. He writes poetry that can depart from received mythology, assigns Hecuba’s father as Cisseus in the version followed by Virgil, and omits Macaria’s sacrifice to heighten her noble-mindedness. His diction includes δόρυ for chief military command and highest civil authority, and he is described as fond of slight alterations to proverbs. Across the records, annotators propose textual readings, interpret Greek expressions, compare his plays, and debate whether some Bacchae passages are authentic or interpolated.

notes

Euripides is the author whose Hecuba is being annotated. The notes discuss his use of δορυ, κεκλημαι, λιμνη, and other Greek expressions, and propose several textual readings for passages in Hecuba.

notes

Euripides changed the phrase ελπιδος λεπτης to ασθενους ‛ρωμης, although the original phrase would also have suited the metre. The notes state that he was fond of making slight alterations in proverbs.

notes

Euripides is the author of the Medea passages under discussion. The notes attribute to him poetic departures from received mythology and comment on what readings or expressions are more likely to have come from his text.

notes

Euripides is identified as the author whose Bacchae is being annotated. The notes sometimes defend or question the authenticity and interpretation of passages in the play, including claims that certain sections sound more like Proclus or may be interpolations.

notes

His style and wording are discussed through comparisons of readings, expressions, and dramatic characterization in Iphigenia in Aulis. The notes also compare the play with other works attributed to Euripides, including Hippolytus, Alcestis, and Orestes.

notes

Euripides is identified as the author whose Iphigenia in Tauris is being annotated. The notes defend or question various lines attributed to him and compare the play with other works by him, including Orestes, Electra, Hecuba, and Iphigenia in Aulis.

Euripides in Medea

Euripides · 430 BC · the work’s page · read it where Euripides first appears

Abstract written by a model

Euripides is a poet, sceptic, and devotee of truth. In the early preserved Medea, his youthful, austere, passionate realism exposes tragedy beneath heroic romance, repeatedly examining oppression, revenge, and cruelty’s damage to victims and avengers.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Euripides is presented as a poet who uses the Medea to expose the tragedy beneath heroic romance and to examine how cruelty damages both its victim and the eventual avenger. His treatment is described as youthful, austere, passionate, skeptical, and intensely realistic, while still allowing poetry to break through at moments of greatest emotional force.

INTRODUCTORY NOTE

Euripides built the Troaedes around the conquered women and named the play after them. He wrote under the influence of the indignant pity and foreboding associated with Athens’ actions in 416 B.C., and later left for the remote valleys of Macedon, where he wrote the Bacchae and died.

Euripides in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · 413 to 411 BC · the work’s page · read it where Euripides first appears

Abstract written by a model

Euripides wrote Iphigenia in Tauris, a subtle, mobile, poignant romantic play whose recognition scene modeled Aristotle, and Iphigenia in Aulis, probably unfinished at his death.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

PREFACE

Euripides wrote Iphigenia in Tauris and is credited with making it subtle, mobile, poignant, and full of reflection and reality. His treatment of the recognition scene became a model for Aristotle, while his romantic form is distinguished from the deeper tragedy of The Trojan Women.
