  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Athenians defend Greece against Persia, expand their naval power, contend with other states, suffer defeat in Sicily and at Aegos Potamos, and later restore democracy.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Athenians manage changing alliances, oppose Lacedaemonian and Macedonian power, suffer defeat and political restrictions, and seek to preserve their laws and liberties.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, the Athenians are portrayed as a free people who honor the gods, protect the children of Hercules, and prepare to resist Argos.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Athenians under Theseus defend Athens, aid the children of Hercules, prepare for battle, and refuse to execute captured enemies.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, the Athenians protect Oedipus from being taken by force, as he seeks their aid against Creon’s violence.
  • In The Rhesus of Euripides, the Athenians establish dangerous settlements in Thrace, transfer Rhesus’s bones to their colony, and found Amphipolis after the massacre of settlers.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, the Athenians help defeat the Persian fleet at Salamis and army at Plataea while their city becomes a warning against pride.
  • In The Library, the Athenians consult an oracle, fight beside Erechtheus, sacrifice to secure victory, and send youths and maidens to the Minotaur.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, the Athenians are defeated by Minos and required to send young men and women as tribute.
  • In The Library, the Athenians protect the children of Hercules, fight Eurystheus, and kill his sons.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Athenians fight wars, command allied Greeks, make religious dedications, aid other communities, and eventually endure defeat, dependence, and poverty.
  • In Description of Greece, the Athenians are a politically and religiously distinguished community that fights many enemies, expands and loses territory, resists Macedonian rule, and recovers Athens with outside aid.
  • In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, the Athenian people are invoked to defend the maidens and the State, while Theseus enforces their laws.

Athenians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Athenians are the inhabitants of Athens, organized through an Athenian assembly and repeatedly concerned with their laws, liberties, territory, alliances, and political order. They suffer oppression under Hipparchus and Hippias, later disagree over government and have the Thirty established. After the Peloponnesian War they are at peace with the Lacedaemonians, yet their relations repeatedly change: they declare war after Sphodrias’s attempted seizure of the Piraeus, build a confederacy of seventy cities, aid Thebes, defeat the Lacedaemonians near Naxos, and later assist the Lacedaemonians against Thebes. Their actions unfold through continual military and diplomatic commitments. They recall Chabrias from Egypt and send Iphicrates to assist the Persians; join Arcadians against Elians; aid Mantineans and Lacedaemonians at Mantinea; support Megalopolis under Pammenes; intervene in Euboea, the Social War, and the Phocian cause; and support Phayllus with troops under Nausicles. Internal decisions include replacing Leosthenes with Chares, fining Iphicrates and Timotheus, and making peace when Persian support for enemies is feared. A central later concern is opposition to Philip’s growing power. They compete for Olynthian alliance, aid cities Philip attacks, deny him passage toward Pylos, proclaim open war, sell gold-and-ivory statues sent by Dionysius to fund their army, and seek Boeotian aid after Elatea is captured. Their defeat at Chaeronea brings heavy losses and the execution of Lysicles. They then resist Alexander’s order restoring exiles rather than surrender land received from Samians. After Alexander’s death, they organize a Greek alliance against the Macedonians, but are forced to surrender to Antipater; their political power is surrendered, poorer citizens are removed to Thessaly, and a garrison is imposed. They later restore democratic magistrates while resisting Nicanor and Cassander, condemn Phocion and others, but finally accept peace with Cassander, pay ten minas, and accept his control of Munychia during the war. Under an edict restoring Greek liberties, they seek Athenian laws and liberties.

The Athenians are a people of Athens, also described as a Greek people and city-state. They are said to be kindred with the Saites, with three social orders resembling Egyptian divisions. Early accounts emphasize generosity toward the Heraclidae, hardship from drought and famine, tribute sent to Crete, and Theseus’s gathering of scattered settlements into Athens. They later honor him after his death. They become major defenders against Persia: they evacuate Athens to Salamis, provide 140 ships, fight Phoenician and Cyprian forces, reject Persian proposals, fight at Plataea, and rebuild walls and harbor works. Their naval strength, treasure, allies, and confederates then expand greatly. Under leaders including Cimon and Pericles, they free or aid cities, defeat Persians, gain sovereignty at sea, transfer wealth from Delos to Athens, subdue or control many cities, and contend repeatedly with Lacedaemonians and other opponents. Their power is accompanied by harsh rule over allies and conquered places, including Mitylene, Scione, Melos, Lesbos, and Delos, while plague and famine cause severe losses. Their greatest reverse comes from the expedition against Syracuse. Seeking Sicily while assisting the Leontines, they decree war, fortify Epipolae, besiege Syracuse, and initially win engagements. Narrow waters, plague, defeats, and a failed retreat destroy much of their army; survivors are captured and some are sent to quarries. Afterward they lose allies, face internal rule by the four hundred, restore democracy, and resume naval warfare, recalling Alcibiades and winning or contesting positions around the Hellespont and elsewhere. They refuse a Spartan peace proposal, later defeat the Lacedaemonians at Arginusae, but condemn their commanders after a storm prevents burial of the dead. Defeat at Aegos Potamos brings famine, surrender, and severe conditions. Citizens later overthrow the Thirty Tyrants and reconcile with exiles. The records conclude with the Athenians acting as confederates of the Boeotians at Haliartus, while also recording their compelled acceptance of the Peace of Antalcidas.

Athenians in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Athenians are a political and religious community centered on Athens, with older resources and institutions including silver mines at Laurium, Phalerum as their ancient port, Piraeus as their principal port under Themistocles, a temple common to all gods, and government by the code of Draco. They are distinguished above all by exceptional religious zeal: they are said to surpass other Greeks in it, first called Athene the worker, first worshipped the mutilated Hermæ, kept the God of the Zealous with them, treated the Ilissus as sacred, and preserved shrines, monuments, heroes, and divine honours for Ajax and Eurysaces. Their history unfolds through warfare, alliances, civic expansion, reversals, and recovery. They fought Persians, Macedonians, Galati, and others; defended Thermopylæ against the Galati; abandoned Athens before a Persian land attack while sending women and children to Troezen; and commemorated Marathon with tombs, honours, and a trophy. They fought Eleusinians, then incorporated Eleusis politically while leaving its Mysteries privately managed; expelled Æginetans from Ægina; claimed Megara and lost it to the Peloponnesians; and at times were surpassed by Æginetans at sea. They resisted Cleomenes and Isagoras’ dominion, fought with the Thebans at Haliartus, were listed second among the states at Platæa, and made a thirty-year peace with the Lacedæmonians after reducing Eubœa. Their relations with the Lacedæmonians alternated between aid and distrust: they assisted at Ithome but were dismissed as suspected revolutionaries, became friendly with the Argives, aided Messenians, gave them Naupactus, and commemorated Sphacteria with a brazen Victory. After Alexander’s death they initiated rebellion against Macedonian rule, urged other cities to join, and chose Leosthenes. They later endured garrisons and imposed rulers, but under Olympiodorus defeated the Macedonians and recovered Athens, Piraeus, and Munychia. They also received aid from Ptolemy I Soter and an Egyptian fleet, honoured benefactors and leaders with statues, and remained divided when Aristion preferred Mithridates while more respectable Athenians joined the Romans.

Athenians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Athenians are the people of Athens, portrayed through their wars, alliances, religious practices, benefactions, and eventual hardship. Early records place them in an Ionian expedition, disputes over kingship, colonization, and traditions connected with their Acropolis. They are repeatedly associated with naval strength: they possess and use triremes, evacuate Greeks from Thermopylæ by sea, fight under Themistocles off Eubœa and at Salamis, and later attack the Galati from ships. Their renowned actions win Greece's goodwill, and their ancient renown gives them command of the allied Greeks; they show exceptional valour at Thermopylæ and against the Galati. Their successes include fighting with the Platæans at Marathon and against Mardonius, defeating Thebans in border fighting, and victories at the Eurymedon. They commemorate victories with treasuries, spoils, shields, statues, a bronze palm, and offerings to Athene, Theseus, and Poseidon. They guard the temple at Delos, preserve the offerings in the temple of Olympian Zeus after taking it at Syracuse, honour Auxo and Hegemone, and pray to the Graces. They also shelter or restore others: they receive the Platæans after expulsions, allow Lacedæmonian suppliants to depart safely, aid the restoration of Thebes, and help restore Phocian towns. The account also stresses reversals and errors. They suffer defeat at Delium, a reverse at Aegospotami, survive the Peloponnesian War and plague, and undertake a fatal war against Syracuse after misreading an oracle. After Macedonian power subdues them, raids and weak allies lead them to depend on Rome. Reduced to extreme poverty, they plunder Oropus, leave a fine unpaid, resist Oropian demands, and plunder it again. Thus celebrated naval courage and public dedications give way to defeat, dependence, and poverty.
