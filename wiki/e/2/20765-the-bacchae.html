The Bacchae of Euripides in The Bacchae of Euripides

Euripides · 406 to 405 BC · the work’s page · read it where The Bacchae of Euripides first appears

Abstract written by a model

The records summarize The Bacchae. Dionysus comes to Thebes in human form to establish his worship and punish denial of his divine birth. He has already driven Theban women, including Cadmus’s daughters, into Bacchic ecstasy on Kithaeron. Cadmus and Teiresias honor the god and warn Pentheus, but Pentheus rejects their counsel, suppresses the rites, and arrests Dionysus. The Chorus praises Dionysus as a heavenly god who delights in Earth, gives wine and joy without regard for rank, and may grow angry at those who reject joy. Though bound and imprisoned, Dionysus calmly foretells punishment. The women escape, and divine power wrecks Pentheus’s house with earthquake and fire; Dionysus returns free. A Messenger then reports the Bacchae’s miraculous and violent power. Pentheus prepares to attack them, but Dionysus persuades him instead to spy on them disguised as a Bacchanal. Mad with Bacchic influence, Pentheus is led to Kithaeron, placed in a pine tree, and exposed to the Maenads. Agave, Ino, Autonoe, and the other women tear him apart, despite his recognition of Agave as his mother. Agave returns carrying Pentheus’s head, mistaking it for a lion’s. Cadmus reveals the truth, and Agave recognizes her son’s remains. Dionysus declares that this destruction answers Thebes’s refusal to honor him, condemning Cadmus, Harmonia, Agave, and her sisters to exile and suffering. The Chorus closes by affirming obedience to God’s laws and the unexpected ends of divine events.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

The Bacchae

Dionysus arrives in Thebes in human form to establish his worship and punish those who have denied his divine birth. He has driven the women of Thebes, including Cadmus's daughters, into ecstatic rites on the mountain, while Pentheus seeks to suppress the worship and capture Dionysus. Cadmus and Teiresias honor Dionysus, defend his rites, and warn Pentheus against opposing a god. The Chorus of inspired maidens praises Dionysus, his birth, his powers, and the freedom found in Bacchic worship.

A God of Heaven is he,

The unit concerns a God of Heaven who is born in majesty but rejoices in the joy of the Earth. He loves the Earth, described as the one who brings increase and feeds children, and gives his wine to every living thing without grief or blemish. He shows no grudge toward the great or scorn toward the lowly, but may show anger toward those who reject joy. The text praises the simple, nameless herd of Humanity, whose deeds and faith are considered truthful enough.

Pentheus has Dionysus arrested, bound, shorn, stripped of his wand, and taken to a dungeon, while the imprisoned women escape into the woods. Dionysus remains calm, warns Pentheus that the God he denies will bring recompense, and later frees himself through divine power. An earthquake shakes the Castle, fire rises from Semele's tomb, and the Voice announces the God's presence. Dionysus returns unbound as Pentheus's house lies wrecked, and the Chorus celebrates the deliverance.

_Enter_ PENTHEUS _in fury_.

Pentheus confronts Dionysus after Dionysus escapes prison, but a Messenger reports that Agave and the other Bacchae have performed miraculous and violent deeds on Kithaeron. Pentheus rejects the Messenger's counsel to honor Dionysus and orders a military campaign against the women. Dionysus then manipulates Pentheus into abandoning the campaign and agreeing to observe the Bacchae while disguised in women's clothing. Dionysus declares that Pentheus is walking into a net and prepares to make him a sacrifice to Dionysus.

CHORUS.

Dionysus disguises Pentheus as a Bacchanal and leads him to the mountain to spy on the Maenads, while Pentheus is overcome by Bacchic madness. On Kithaeron, Dionysus places Pentheus in a pine tree and calls the Maenads to punish him. Agave, Ino, Autonoe, and the other Maenads tear Pentheus apart after he recognizes his mother. The Messenger reports Pentheus's death and dismemberment, while the Chorus rejoices in Dionysus's power and declares that obedience to God's laws is man's best treasure.

CHORUS.

Agave returns from Kithaeron carrying the severed head of her son Pentheus, believing it to be a lion's head taken in the Bacchic hunt. Cadmus reveals that Agave and her sisters, driven mad, killed Pentheus, and Agave gradually recognizes both the head and the body brought to the palace. Dionysus declares that the punishment followed the Thebans' refusal to honor him and condemns Cadmus, Harmonia, Agave, and her sisters to exile and further suffering. Agave leaves Thebes, while the Chorus reflects that divine events often end in ways no one expects.
