Tantalid family curse in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

A hereditary fate repeatedly leaves Tantalid women vulnerable to abduction, causing grief, loss, and disrupted marriages; Hermione believes it has pursued the family through her generation.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

VIII

Hermione wonders whether a hereditary fate has pursued the Tantalid women, making them ready victims for abductors. She connects this suffering with the grief, loss, and disrupted marriages experienced by the women of her family, including herself.

family curse of the Tantalids in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

The family curse of the Tantalids is a history of recurring divine and familial crimes. It begins with Tantalus and continues through Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon, Clytæmnestra, and Orestes, marked by the golden lamb, Thyestes' feast, murders, slaughter, grief, and bloodshed answered by bloodshed. Its unstable prosperity culminates in Orestes fearing he will bring further curses unless Iphigenia rescues him.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

ORESTES.

The history of the Tantalids is presented as a succession of divine and familial crimes, beginning with Tantalus and continuing through Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon, Clytæmnestra, and Orestes. The chorus treats this lineage as marked by recurring slaughter, grief, and unstable prosperity.

PYLADES, ORESTES, CHORUS.

The Chorus traces the present catastrophe to the ancient calamities of the house of Atreus, including the golden lamb, Thyestes' feast, and successive murders. It presents the family as repeatedly suffering bloodshed in response to bloodshed.

CHOR. I deplore thee now destined to the gory streams of the lustral

The unit recalls the conflict between Atreus and Thyestes concerning the golden lamb. Orestes also says that he will become a source of curses upon his house if Iphigenia does not remove him from the barbarian land.

the curse of the house of Atreus in The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies

Aeschylus · -0457

The curse of the house of Atreus is an inborn, hereditary doom caused among other ancestral crimes by Thyestes’ feast, ancient kin-murder, incest, cannibalism, and repeated household bloodshed. After Thyestes learned he had eaten his children, he cursed the race of Pleisthenes; the curse is linked to Agamemnon’s death. It persists by summoning revenge through successive murders, grief, and guilt, until Orestes and Electra avenge Agamemnon through the deaths of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, which the Chorus hopes will end it.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

[_Exeunt all but Cassandra and the Chorus._

The curse is associated with ancient kin-murder, incest, cannibalism, and repeated bloodshed within the household. Cassandra says it continues to summon revenge and will not be satisfied by the blood already shed.

[_The scene opens, disclosing Clytemnestra, who comes forward. The body

The curse of Thyestes was pronounced after he learned that he had eaten his children. He cursed the race of Pleisthenes, and Aegisthus connects that curse with Agamemnon’s death.

ORESTES (_approaching suddenly_)

The curse of the house of Atreus appears as an inherited cycle of murder, vengeance, grief, and doom. Orestes and Electra seek to end or answer its latest bloodshed by avenging Agamemnon.

[_Enter Orestes and Pylades, in guise of travellers_.

The Chorus describes the deaths of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra as the third great blast of woe upon Atreus’ line, following Thyestes’ feast and Agamemnon’s murder. It hopes that the old curse will finally end without producing further blood guilt.
