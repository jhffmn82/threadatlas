Justice in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Justice is a divine and universal law requiring punishment for transgression and restoration for violated obligations. It unfolds through Hecuba’s and Agamemnon’s demand that Polymestor be punished for murdering Polydore, then through the Chorus’s portrayal of Lycus’s death as repayment for violence, blasphemy, and unjust rule. Finally, it guides Theonoe to protect Menelaus and Helen, uphold her father’s honor, and respect plighted trust despite her brother’s demand.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

AGAMEMNON, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Hecuba argues that the gods and justice require punishment for those who murder guests or violate sacred obligations. Agamemnon says he wishes to act according to the gods and justice, and the Chorus reflects that those who transgress should suffer.

CHORUS.

Hecuba presents the killing of Polymestor’s sons and his blinding as a just atonement for his murder of Polydore. Agamemnon concludes that Polymestor deserves punishment because he murdered a protected guest for financial gain, and the Chorus associates his downfall with divine and human justice.

HERCULES, MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

The Chorus presents Lycus’s death as repayment for his violence, blasphemies, and unjust rule. It says that the gods recognize broken trust and restore neglected laws through vindictive punishment.

CHOR. My sentiments on prophets well accord

Justice governs Theonoe’s decision to protect Menelaus and Helen rather than obey her brother’s demand. She says that justice has its shrine in her heart, that she must preserve her father’s honor, and that plighted trust must be respected.

Justice in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Justice is a principle of punishment, protection, equality, and proper judgment. It requires vengeance against the wicked, as Hecuba and Agamemnon affirm, yet it forbids private retaliatory killing: Tyndarus says murderers should face formal accusation, removal, or banishment instead. Orestes defends his killing as vengeance for Agamemnon and as deterrence. Justice links friends through equal shares, protects suppliants from violent removal at sacred altars, and supports hearing charges through a just process. Medea says it is absent when people judge strangers by appearances, while divine witnesses may judge broken oaths. In the chorus's image of streams flowing upward, Justice itself is reversed.

Hecuba appeals to the gods, law, and Agamemnon's sense of justice against Polymestor. Agamemnon agrees that the wicked should suffer vengeance and that Hecuba may seek punishment for the murder.

Tyndarus argues that justice required Orestes to bring a formal charge against Clytæmnestra or remove her from the house rather than kill her. Orestes answers that he acted to avenge Agamemnon and prevent women from treating the murder of their husbands as permissible, while Menelaus says that necessity must be considered by the wise.

MEDEA.

Medea says that justice is not apparent in human judgment, especially when strangers are hated without being known. The Chorus agrees that Medea will act justly in avenging the wrongs done to her, while Medea invokes divine witnesses to judge Jason's broken oaths.

Justice in The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies

Aeschylus · -0457

Justice is a force of divine judgment, retribution, and repayment for unlawful deeds, especially bloodshed, violated hospitality, and broken marriage bonds. It punishes sinners, governs mortals, and makes guilty households bear consequences. It first appears in punishment of Paris and Troy and in the destruction associated with their violation of hospitality. It then drives vengeance within Agamemnon’s household: Agamemnon’s death is presented as repayment for earlier crimes, but Justice is also expected to punish Aegisthus. Orestes, Electra, and the Chorus call on it to avenge Agamemnon, and Justice stands with Agamemnon’s children, preparing repayment at the appointed time. Its greatest conflict arises when Apollo and the Furies each claim Justice in the case of Orestes. Rather than leave vengeance to either side, Athena founds a sworn council-court in Athens to judge bloodshed. The settlement preserves the Furies’ power within civic order, making Justice a foundation of Athens’ stability and a law administered through impartial judgment rather than private revenge.

Orestes asks Zeus for help in avenging Agamemnon’s murder. Electra and the Chorus pray that an avenger will come and that those who shed the blood will themselves be slain.

ORESTES (_approaching suddenly_)

Justice is presented as demanding repayment for bloodshed and as standing with Agamemnon’s children. The Chorus says that Justice prepares the sword and restores the child at the appointed time.

THE FURIES

Justice is invoked by both Apollo and the Furies. The Furies claim an ancient right to avenge bloodshed, while Apollo argues that their unequal treatment of a murdered husband and a murdered wife is unjust and says Athena will guard the right.

_The scene changes to Athens. In the foreground, the Temple of Athena

Justice is described as requiring punishment for bloodshed, respect for parents and guests, and obedience to the gods. Athena seeks to preserve justice by creating a court rather than allowing either Orestes or the Furies to decide the matter alone.

[_Re-enter Athena, with twelve Athenian citizens_.

Athena establishes the council-court to administer the law of bloodshed. She commands that the judges act under oath and that the court remain pure, unsullied by gain, and ready to punish wrongdoing.

CHORUS

Justice is invoked by the Furies as they call for vengeance and protest the loss of their ancient rights. Athena’s settlement places their power alongside a civic order founded on justice.

divine justice in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Divine justice is a divine authority, enthroned among the gods and observing humanity. It punishes the unjust: Heaven sees both just and unjust people, and no godless sinner escapes. It stands above human authority, though Antigone is said to oppose it, while Love is imagined enthroned beside it.

OEDIPUS AT COLONUS

Oedipus says that Heaven sees both just and unjust people and that no godless sinner escapes. He urges the Chorus to stand on Heaven’s side by refusing to wrong him.

Justice is presented as something that stands above human authority, although the Chorus says that Antigone has boldly set herself against it. The Chorus also imagines Love enthroned with Justice and bending human beings to its will.
