Christianity in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Christianity, a religion in the Highlands, Iona, and adjacent regions, arrived with St. Columba, overturned Druids’ superstitions, and was established through Columba’s monastery and the Culdees, leaving ecclesiastical remains at Iona.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter XXXIV

Christianity overturned the Druids' superstitions and supplanted their religious system in the Highlands, Iona, and adjacent regions after the arrival of St. Columba.

IONA

Christianity was established at Iona through Columba's monastery and the work of the Culdees. Iona later contained ecclesiastical remains, including a cathedral or abbey church and a nunnery chapel, while older cairns indicated that other forms of worship had existed before Christianity.

Christianity in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

Christianity is an expanded religion, born through Jewish separateness, with a substantial genealogy and an asserted claim to surpass the spiritual doctrine of the Hebrew prophets. It is presented as having determined the religion of half the world, though modern praise often disowns its Jewish origin. The records condemn its use to justify vengeance, persecution, greed, and cruelty against the Jews. In Mixtus's theory, Christianity spreads through commercial intercourse, even with war as a possible pioneer.

Christianity in An Astronomer's Wife

Angelo Hall · 1908

Christianity is presented as a source of divine enthusiasm for advancing knowledge, supported by arguments, symbolized by mountains, and considered a desirable condition shared by marriage. It begins in intense faith centered on prayer meetings, the Savior’s blessing, salvation, cleansing, and the Lamb’s Book of Life. It then emphasizes tolerance: human, theistic, moral, Christian, and Protestant identities outrank denominational division in marriage. Faith broadens beyond superstition and narrow theology through Unitarianism and searching in philosophy, history, and science, ending in a broad religious outlook that finds no necessary conflict between religion and nature study.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER IV.

Angeline Stickney’s religious faith was intense and centered on prayer meetings, the Savior’s blessing, and salvation. In her diary she prayed to be cleansed and prepared for life or death, valuing a name in the Lamb’s Book of Life above being remembered on earth.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER IX.

The unit argues for tolerance in marriage, ranking broader identities such as being human, theistic, moral, Christian, and Protestant before narrower denominational identities. Angeline Stickney was a sincere Baptist, while Asaph Hall stood outside religious sects, and their differing beliefs did not prevent a fortunate marriage.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XV.

Religious faith was the motive power of Angeline Hall’s life, although her faith outgrew superstition and the narrow theology of her mother’s church. She became a Unitarian, later searched for a renewed faith through philosophy, history, and science, and retained a poetic religious feeling centered on truth, love, and the mystery of God.

------------------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER XVII.

The unnamed woman held a broad faith and saw no necessary conflict between religion and the study of nature.
