Rudolph Hackh in Dragon's Blood

Henry Milner Rideout · 1909 · the work’s page · read it where Rudolph Hackh first appears

Abstract written by a model

Rudolph Hackh is a young German, a Baltic native who had lived in Bremen and worked as a clerk at twenty-one before becoming a shipping agent in China for Fliegelman and Sons. On his first journey to the Far East, aboard a German mail-boat and bound for China, he is homesick, timid, and romantically absorbed in Mrs. Forrester; he is crushed to learn she is married. He arrives at Stink-Chau to replace Zimmerman as manager, a new foreign station employee facing an unfamiliar Chinese city, plague fears, and chaotic office accounts. Friendship with Maurice Heywood draws him into local social life, while Dr. Earle's chapel address changes his perception of Chinese townspeople as human beings rather than devils or images. He survives the pestilence, studies Chinese, and settles into a former Portuguese nunnery assigned to him. His impulsiveness and emerging conscience appear when he rescues an abandoned infant girl from an island, takes her to the hospital, and promises funds for the child. His attachment to Mrs. Forrester and anger at Dr. Chantel lead him into a duel despite being a novice who knows nothing of swords; practice leaves his arm wounded, and Heywood substitutes for him. Later, called the "spare man," Rudolph escorts Mrs. Forrester home, protects her from a buffalo, and is pleased to be recognized as brave and honest. The records end with his return home to find most of the Mausers disabled by missing side-bolts.

Rudolph Hackh is a young German agent traveling from Port Said toward China to take up a new appointment. He is homesick and timid at first, becomes absorbed in Mrs. Forrester during the voyage, and is crushed when he learns that she is married.

CHAPTER II THE PIED PIPER A gray smudge trailing northward showed where the Fa-Hien--Scottish Oriental, sixteen hundred tons--was disappearing from the pale expanse of ocean.

Rudolph arrives at Stink-Chau as the new agent for Fliegelman and Sons, having traveled there aboard the Fa-Hien and then by sampan. Heywood takes him to his house and later to the club, where he meets Gilly, Wutzler, and Doctor Chantel. Rudolph learns that the rats' behavior signals the plague.

" CHAPTER III UNDER FIRE The desert was sometimes Gobi, sometimes Sahara, but always an infinite stretch of sand that floated up and up in a stifling layer, like the tide.

Rudolph wakes from a nightmare, is unsettled by the unfamiliar city and its smells, and fears the plague. He begins work at the Fliegelman and Sons office, gradually finding relief in sorting its chaotic accounts. At the club, he learns of Monsieur Jolivet's death, recognizes Mrs. Forrester, and awkwardly joins the group singing.

CHAPTER IV THE SWORD-PEN "Wutzler was missing last night," said Heywood, lazily.

Rudolph accompanies Heywood on the excursion and visits Captain Kneebone's steamer. He learns that a former Portuguese nunnery and its grounds have been assigned to him, though Heywood urges him to continue staying at Heywood's house. The shop stabbing and the crowd's hostility leave him disturbed and bewildered.

CHAPTER V IN TOWN Night, in that maze of alleys, was but a more sinister day.

Rudolph attends the Christian chapel service with Heywood and is moved by Dr. Earle’s sincere address to the Chinese congregation. He tells Dr. Earle that the sermon made him perceive the Chinese as men rather than as devils or images, and he feels briefly that a person might work through the troubled present toward a better future.

" CHAPTER VI THE PAGODA He was spared that inconvenience.

Rudolph is relieved when the pestilence ends and grows increasingly tranquil as he studies Chinese, moves into the Portuguese nunnery, and adjusts to local life. He is disturbed by the notice forbidding girls to drown, becomes jealous and angry when he sees Chantel riding with a woman, and is drawn back into emotional confusion by Mrs. Forrester's attention at the pagoda. At the tiffin he performs brilliantly, especially for Miss Drake, but later risks being happy with Mrs. Forrester before Maurice Heywood interrupts them.

CHAPTER VII IPHIGENIA "Don't chop off a hen's head with a battle-axe.

Rudolph rides with Mrs. Forrester and accepts her challenge to investigate an island. After his pony becomes trapped in the mud, he swims to the island, finds an abandoned infant girl among the remains of other babies, and rescues her. He carries the child to the hospital and, when told it lacks funds, declares that he will replenish his nunnery.

CHAPTER VIII THE HOT NIGHT Rudolph paced his long chamber like a wolf,--a wolf in summer, with too thick a coat.

Rudolph spends the hot night pacing in distress over whether he can believe in the lady and whether he came too late. At the club, he strikes Dr. Chantel after Chantel insults her, then learns that Chantel is an expert swordsman; although he claims not to be afraid, he admits that he does not know a sword's point from its handle and concludes that the duel may be a good way out.

" CHAPTER IX PASSAGE AT ARMS "Boy.

Rudolph is frightened of appearing absurd because he cannot fence, though he refuses to withdraw from the duel. During practice with Heywood, he is cut in the arm and watches Heywood substitute for him; he later tends his wounded arm during the fight and riot.

" CHAPTER X THREE PORTALS Not till after dinner, that evening, did Rudolph rouse from his stupor.

Rudolph lies wearily in the upper chamber of Heywood's house after rousing from his stupor. When Heywood admits that he caused the accident, Rudolph reacts with shock and clutches his bandaged arm.

CHAPTER XI WHITE LOTUS He was swarming up, quiet as a thief, when his fingers clawed the bare plaster.

Rudolph waits at Heywood's house and says that he nearly was killed that afternoon. He responds emotionally to Heywood's return and discusses the possibility that neither of them may see another night.

" CHAPTER XIII THE SPARE MAN Beyond the scant greenery of Heywood's garden--a ropy little banyan, a low rank of glossy whampee leaves, and the dusty sage-green tops of stunted olives--glared the river.

Rudolph is sent to escort Mrs. Forrester home after the others decide he is the man they can spare. He protects her from a buffalo, explains that he did not fail to stand up to Chantel, and is pleased when she calls him different, brave, and honest. Returning home, he finds that almost all of the Mausers have been crippled by missing side-bolts.

Rudolph in Dragon's Blood

Henry Milner Rideout · 1909 · the work’s page · read it where Rudolph first appears

Abstract written by a model

Rudolph is a young white man, a runner who brings urgent news from the river, a host and defender at the nunnery, and owner of his own go-down. He is Heywood’s longstanding friend and comrade, is associated with Miss Drake through shared grief and understanding, and has Bertha as a forsaken lover. Initially perplexed and comparatively raw, he is changed by danger, loss, and Heywood’s example into a more resilient man ready to defend others. At the nunnery, Rudolph receives the arriving party with awkward ceremony, helps make dinner a banquet, notices Mrs. Forrester’s angry signal, and is sent to find Chantel without alarming the women. Amid the uprising he runs to Bertha’s house, helps Chantel with Bertha, carries Bertha to the river, and refuses the boat so that he can return toward the fires and those left behind. Struck by Chantel’s pole, he is revived and aided by Wutzler. He follows Wutzler through smoke, reaches his go-down, hides from rioters, witnesses the Christian captive’s martyrdom, and, with Heywood’s help, escapes over a wall. Taking a rifle, he runs to the northeast corner to join the defense. There he obeys Captain Kneebone, fights at a loophole, learns Bertha has departed by boat, and finds the enemy digging beneath the defenses. Though initially resigned to this threat, he regains courage through Heywood, enters the mine tunnel, carries powder, restrains a coolie, and retreats before the explosion. During the escape preparations, Rudolph learns not to mistake one exploit for victory, helps oppose Gilbert Forrester, clears the stairs, and boards the Hakka boat. He mourns presumed-dead Heywood, warns of hostile boats, recognizes Mrs. Wu as the Pretty Lily, and anxiously watches pursuit at sea. Heywood’s return confirms the lesson of Rudolph’s ordeal: grief and experience have made him more seasoned, courageous, and seemingly invulnerable.

CHAPTER XV KAU FAI At the top of the nunnery stairs, Rudolph met them with awkward ceremony, and with that smiling air of encouragement which a nurse might use in trying cheerfully to deceive a sick man.

Rudolph welcomes the arriving party at the nunnery and helps Captain Kneebone turn the dinner into a banquet. During the celebration, he notices Mrs. Forrester signal angrily and is instructed by Heywood to find Chantel without frightening the women.

CHAPTER XVI THE GUNWALE Rudolph's mission began quietly, with a glimpse which he afterward recalled as incredibly peaceful.

Rudolph runs from Heywood's house through the uprising to Bertha's house, where he helps Chantel try to manage her. He carries Bertha to the river but refuses to board Chantel's boat, choosing instead to return toward the fires and those left behind; after being struck by Chantel's pole, he is revived and aided by Wutzler.

CHAPTER XVII LAMP OF HEAVEN The white men peered from among the oil-jars, like two of the Forty Thieves.

Rudolph circles behind a motionless musketeer and discovers that the man is already dead. He follows Wutzler through the smoke, reaches his own go-down, hides while rioters search it, and witnesses the Christian captive's martyrdom. After Heywood helps him over the pony-shed wall, Rudolph takes a rifle and runs to the northeast corner, ready to join his friends in danger.

CHAPTER XVIII SIEGE He never believed that they could hold the northeast corner for a minute, so loud and unceasing was the uproar.

Rudolph joins the defense of the northeast corner and crawls to a loophole under Captain Kneebone's orders. During the assault, he strikes a Chinese climber with his gun, learns that Bertha Forrester has left by boat, and later discovers digging in the clay-pit beneath the defenses with Heywood.

CHAPTER XIX BROTHER MOLES This new danger, working below in the solid earth, had thrown Rudolph into a state of sullen resignation.

Rudolph initially feels resigned because the enemy is working beneath the solid earth, but Heywood's confidence restores his courage. He follows Heywood into the mine tunnel, helps carry powder sacks, restrains a coolie, and joins the retreat before the explosion.

CHAPTER XX THE HAKKA BOAT "Pretty fair," Captain Kneebone said.

Rudolph initially resents Captain Kneebone's grudging praise but later understands its warning that one exploit does not make a victory. He helps argue with Gilbert Forrester, assists in preparing the escape, helps kick the bonfire from the stairs, and boards the Hakka boat.

CHAPTER XXI THE DRAGON'S SHADOW The lowdah would have set his dirty sails without delay, for the fair wind was already drooping; but at the first motion he found himself deposed, and a usurper in command, at the big steering-paddle.

Rudolph mourns Heywood and recalls first hearing of him on the same river where he now believes he has heard the last of him. He warns the others about the approaching hostile boat, recognizes Mrs. Wu as the Pretty Lily, and later watches anxiously as another boat follows them at sea. After Heywood returns, Rudolph reflects that his grief and experience have helped turn him from a raw boy into a more seasoned and invulnerable man.
