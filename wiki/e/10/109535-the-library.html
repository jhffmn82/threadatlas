The Library

Uranus was the first ruler of the entire universe. After marrying Earth, he fathered the Hecatoncheires: Briareus, Gyges, and Cottus. They were extraordinarily large and powerful, with one hundred hands and fifty heads each. The text also discusses the marriage of Sky and Earth as a widespread myth.

BOOK I

Sky first ruled the whole world and fathered the Hundred-handed, the Cyclopes, and the Titans with Earth. Sky cast the Cyclopes into Tartarus, while Earth persuaded the Titans to attack him, and Cronus overthrew him with an adamantine sickle. Cronus then ruled, but he swallowed his children because of a prophecy that he would be dethroned by his own son. Rhea secretly gave birth to Zeus in a cave of Dicte and arranged for him to be raised in Crete while Cronus swallowed a stone in his place.

II. ᾿Ἐ ππειδὴ δὲ Ζεὺς ἐγενήθη τέλειος, λαμβάνει

When Zeus becomes full-grown, he takes Metis, daughter of Ocean, as his helper, and she gives Cronus a drug that makes him disgorge the stone and the children he had swallowed. With these children Zeus wages a ten-year war against Cronus and the Titans, following Earth’s prophecy that victory will come if he allies with those imprisoned in Tartarus. Zeus frees the Cyclopes, who provide divine weapons, and the gods defeat and imprison the Titans in Tartarus under the guard of the Hundred-handers. Zeus, Poseidon, and Pluto then divide sovereignty among the sky, sea, and Hades.

III. Ζεὺς δὲ γαμεῖ μὲν “Ἥραν, καὶ τεκνοῖ

The unit concerns Zeus’s offspring and unions, including his children by Hera, Themis, Dione, Eurynome, Styx, and Mnemosyne. It recounts the stories of Orpheus and Eurydice, Hyacinth and Apollo, Thamyris and the Muses, and several children born from the Muses. It also describes Hera’s birth of Hephaestus and Zeus’s swallowing of Metis, after which Athena emerged from his head fully armed.

IV. Τῶν δὲ Κοίου θυγατέρων ᾿Αστερία μὲν

Asteria fled Zeus by taking the form of a quail and throwing herself into the sea, after which the island was first called Asteria and later Delos. Hera pursued Leto across the earth until Leto reached Delos, where Artemis was born first and later helped deliver Apollo. Apollo took over the oracle at Delphi after killing Python, and he also killed Tityus and Marsyas. Artemis killed Orion, whose stories include his blinding by Oenopion, his restoration of sight, and different accounts of his death.

V. Πλούτων δὲ Περσεφόνης ἐρασθεὶς Διὸς

Pluto, with Zeus’s help, secretly abducts Persephone. Demeter searches for Persephone throughout the earth and, after learning that Pluto took her, comes to Eleusis in the guise of a woman. Persephone eats a pomegranate seed given by Pluto and is required to spend one-third of each year with him and the rest with the gods.

VI. Περὶ μὲν οὖν Δήμητρος ταῦτα λέγεται"

Earth, angered over the Titans, gives birth to the Giants, who attack the sky and are defeated by the gods with the aid of Heracles. Zeus learns that the Giants can be killed only with the help of a mortal, obtains a herb that would protect them, and summons Heracles through Athena. After the Giants are overcome, Earth unites with Tartarus and gives birth to Typhon, whom Zeus eventually defeats by casting Mount Etna upon him.

VII. Προμηθεὺς δὲ ἐξ ὕδατος καὶ γῆς ἀνθρώ-

Prometheus moulds human beings from water and earth and secretly gives them fire carried in a fennel stalk. Zeus punishes Prometheus by having him nailed to Mount Caucasus, where an eagle eats his liver daily, until Hercules later releases him. Prometheus's son Deucalion survives Zeus's Flood with Pyrrha in a chest, lands on Mount Parnassus, and restores humankind by throwing stones that become men and women.

Ἕλλην μὲν πρῶτος, ὃν ἐκ Atos γεγεννῆσθαι ϊ

Hellen, said by some to be the son of Zeus, had Dorus, Xuthus, and Aeolus by Orseis and divided the country among them. He named the Greeks Hellenes after himself, while his sons gave their names to the Dorians, Achaeans, Ionians, and Aeolians. Poseidon fathered Otus and Ephialtes, the Aloads, who tried to attack the gods but were killed by Artemis through deception. Endymion became ruler of Elis, and his son Aetolus later gave his name to Aetolia.

The unit traces the descendants of Porthaon and Thestius and describes the family of Oeneus, king of Calydon. Oeneus neglected Artemis in a sacrifice, prompting her to send a destructive boar and leading to the Calydonian boar hunt, in which Meleager killed the boar and awarded its skin to Atalanta. Meleager later died after Althaea burned the brand on which his life depended, though another account says he died fighting after defending Calydon. The narrative then follows Tydeus and Diomedes, including Oeneus’s overthrow, restoration, and burial.

IX. Τῶν δὲ Αἰόλον παίδων ᾿Αθάμας, Βοιωτίας

Athamas rules Boeotia, has Phrixus and Helle with Nephele, and later has Learchus and Melicertes with Ino. Ino causes a famine and contrives for Phrixus to be sacrificed to Zeus, but Nephele rescues Phrixus and Helle with a golden-fleeced ram; Helle falls into the sea, while Phrixus reaches the Colchians and gives the ram’s fleece to Aeetes. Athamas later loses Learchus and Melicertes, while Sisyphus is punished in Hades and Salmoneus is destroyed by Zeus for claiming to be Zeus.

Now Tyro, daughter of Salmoneus and Alcidice,

Tyro, daughter of Salmoneus, secretly bore twin sons, Pelias and Neleus, after Poseidon approached her in the likeness of Enipeus. The twins were exposed, rescued and raised by a horse-keeper, later recognized Tyro, and killed their stepmother Sidero; Pelias ruled in Thessaly, while Neleus founded Pylus and fathered Nestor and other children. Neleus’s daughter Pero was won for Bias after Melampus obtained the kine of Phylacus, and Melampus later settled in Argos with Bias after healing the women driven mad by Dionysus. The genealogy continues through Bias’s son Adrastus, and through Cretheus’s son Pheres to Admetus, who reigned at Pherae while Apollo served him.

Πελίον θυγατέρα Αλκηστιν. ἐκείνου 1 δὲ δώσειν

Pelias promised Alcestis to the man who could yoke a lion and a boar, and Apollo enabled Admetus to win her. After Admetus forgot Artemis at his marriage sacrifice, he was granted release from death if someone voluntarily died for him, and Alcestis did so; she was later restored according to one account by the Maiden and according to another by Hercules. Pelias later learned from an oracle to beware the one-sandaled man, and Jason became that man while crossing the Anaurus; Pelias therefore sent him to fetch the Golden Fleece from Colchis. Jason gathered the Argonauts aboard the Argo, whose voyage included Lemnos and Mysia, where Hercules and Polyphemus were left behind after Hylas disappeared.

The Argonauts reach Salmydessus, where Phineus tells them how to escape the Harpies and pass the Clashing Rocks. They pass the rocks with the help of a dove and Hera, then travel to Colchis and ask Aeetes for the Golden Fleece. Medea promises Jason assistance after he swears to take her as his wife to Greece, and Jason uses her drug to yoke the brazen-footed bulls and defeat the armed men who rise from the dragon’s teeth.

τῶν ταύρων οὐκ ἐδίδου τὸ δέρας Αἰήτης, ἐβούλετο

Medea helps Jason obtain the fleece, escapes with him and Apsyrtus, and kills Apsyrtus when Aeetes pursues them. Zeus sends a storm because of Apsyrtus’s murder, and the Argonauts are purified by Circe before continuing their voyage through various dangers to Iolcus. Medea helps Jason bring about Pelias’s death, after which Jason and Medea are expelled from Iolcus and settle in Corinth. There, Jason marries Glauce, and Medea kills Glauce and her own children Mermerus and Pheres before fleeing to Athens.

Medea came to Athens, married Aegeus, and bore a son named Medus. She later plotted against Theseus and was driven from Athens with Medus. Medus conquered many barbarians, named the territory under him Media, and died while campaigning against the Indians. Medea returned to Colchis, killed Perses after finding that he had deposed Aeetes, and restored the kingdom to Aeetes.

BOOK ITI

The unit traces the Inachean family from Inachus, son of Ocean and Tethys, through Phoroneus, Apis, Niobe, and their descendants. Phoroneus ruled the land later called the Peloponnese and fathered Apis and Niobe, while Apis was killed after ruling as a stern tyrant and was regarded as the god Sarapis. Niobe had Argos by Zeus, and Argos later ruled the kingdom, naming the Peloponnese after himself and fathering several children. The lineage also includes Argus the All-seeing, who possessed eyes throughout his body, performed heroic deeds, and avenged the killing of Apis.

THE LIBRARY, II. 1. 1-3

This unit concerns Io’s transformation into a cow, her wanderings under Hera’s persecution, and her return to human form in Egypt, where she gives birth to Epaphus. It traces the descendants of Epaphus through Libya, Agenor, and Belus to Egyptus and Danaus, whose descendants become involved in a forced marriage alliance. Danaus’s daughters kill the sons of Egyptus on their wedding night, except for Hypermnestra, who spares Lynceus. The account also describes Amymone’s encounter with Poseidon and the origins of the springs at Lerna.

THE LIBRARY, 11. 1. 5—u. 1

Proetus occupied Tiryns after his father-in-law restored him, while he and Acrisius divided the Argive territory, with Acrisius ruling Argos and Proetus ruling Tiryns. Proetus's daughters and the other Argive women were seized by madness, and Melampus cured the surviving daughters after Proetus agreed to his demands for land. Bellerophon came to Proetus for purification after accidentally killing a relative, but Stheneboea falsely accused him of making improper advances. Proetus sent Bellerophon to Iobates with a letter ordering his death, but Iobates instead assigned him dangerous campaigns, which Bellerophon completed with the aid of Pegasus and ultimately won Iobates's admiration and kingdom.

Acrisius learned from an oracle that Danae would bear a son who would kill him, so he confined Danae, but she gave birth to Perseus. Acrisius cast Danae and Perseus into the sea in a chest, and Dictys rescued and raised Perseus on Seriphus, where Polydectes later sent Perseus to obtain the Gorgon’s head. Guided by Hermes and Athena, Perseus obtained magical equipment from the nymphs, decapitated Medusa, and traveled to Ethiopia, where he found Andromeda exposed to a sea monster.

THE LIBRARY, 1]. 1v. 2-3

Perseus beheaded Medusa with Athena’s guidance, rescued Andromeda from a sea monster, and turned Phineus and his fellow conspirators to stone with the Gorgon’s head. He returned to Seriphus, turned Polydectes and his gathered friends to stone, made Dictys king, and gave the Gorgon’s head to Athena. Perseus later caused the death of Acrisius by accidentally striking him with a quoit, exchanged Argos with Megapenthes, and ruled Tiryns while fortifying Midea and Mycenae. The unit also recounts the lineage of Perseus, including Electryon, Amphitryon, Alcmena, and the events leading to Electryon’s accidental death.

THE LIBRARY, II. tv. 6-7

Amphitryon went to Thebes with Alcmena and Licymnius, was purified by Creon, and undertook to avenge Alcmena’s brothers so that he could marry her. Creon agreed to assist Amphitryon against the Teleboans if Amphitryon first rid the Cadmea of the Teumessian vixen, but the vixen was fated never to be caught. Amphitryon brought Cephalus and the dog Laelaps to hunt the vixen, and Zeus turned both animals into stones. Amphitryon then conquered the Tafian islands after Comaetho removed the golden lock from Pterelaus’s head, causing Pterelaus to die.

THE LIBRARY, II. tv. 7-8

Amphitryon’s campaigns and death are followed by Hercules’ marriage to Megara and his violent madness, during which he kills his children and two children of Iphicles. Hercules is purified and goes to Delphi, where the Delphic oracle gives him the name Hercules and orders him to serve Eurystheus at Tiryns for twelve years and perform ten labours. The first labour requires Hercules to bring back the hide of the invulnerable Nemean lion, which he goes to hunt.

V. When Hercules heard that, he went to Tiryns

Hercules carries out the first five labours ordered by Eurystheus, including killing the Nemean lion and the Lernaean hydra, capturing the Cerynitian hind and the Erymanthian boar, and undertaking the cleaning of the Augean cattle’s stalls. Eurystheus forbids Hercules from entering Mycenae and orders him to display his labours before the gates. Iolaus assists Hercules against the hydra, so Eurystheus refuses to count that labour among the ten. The unit also recounts Hercules’ encounters with Artemis, the centaurs, and Augeias.

THE LIBRARY, II. v. 4-5

Hercules completes the fifth through eighth labours by cleansing Augeas's cattle-yard, driving away the Stymphalian birds, bringing the Cretan Bull to Eurystheus, and capturing Diomedes's man-eating mares. Eurystheus rejects the fifth labour because Hercules performed it for a promised reward, and he also rejects an additional deed involving Eurytion for the same reason. Hercules then undertakes the ninth labour, traveling to the Amazons to obtain Hippolyte's belt of Ares. At Troy, Hercules encounters a city suffering under the wrath of Apollo and Poseidon.

THE LIBRARY, II. v. 9

Apollo and Poseidon assumed human form and fortified Pergamum for Laomedon in return for wages, but Laomedon refused to pay them. Apollo sent a pestilence and Poseidon sent a sea monster that carried away people from the plain, and Laomedon exposed his daughter Hesione to it after oracles promised deliverance. Hercules offered to rescue Hesione in exchange for the horses Zeus had given to Tros, killed the sea monster, and saved her. When Laomedon again refused payment, Hercules threatened to wage war on Troy.

THE LIBRARY, II. v. 9

Hercules undertakes his tenth labour by fetching the red kine of Geryon from Erythia. He crosses the ocean in a golden goblet given to him by the Sun, kills Geryon, Eurytion, and Orthus, and drives the kine through Europe and Italy. After recovering an escaped bull from Eryx and overcoming Hera's dispersal of the herd, Hercules brings the kine to Eurystheus. Eurystheus sacrifices the kine to Hera.

When the labours had been performed in eight

After Hercules had completed his labours in eight years and a month, Eurystheus assigned him an eleventh labour: fetching golden apples from the Hesperides. The apples were said to be located by Atlas in Hyperborea rather than in Libya, and to have been given by Earth to Zeus when he married Hera. An immortal, hundred-headed dragon born of Typhon and Echidna guarded the apples, together with the Hesperides. On his journey, Hercules reached the river Echedorus, where Cycnus challenged him to single combat, but a thunderbolt separated them.

THE LIBRARY, 11. v. 11

Hercules travels through Illyria, Libya, Egypt, Asia, Arabia, and beyond in pursuit of the golden apples. He defeats Antaeus, kills Busiris and his son, rescues Prometheus, and obtains the apples through a trick involving Atlas. Hercules gives the apples to Eurystheus, though another tradition says he plucked them himself after killing the guardian snake.

The twelfth labor required Hercules to bring Cerberus from Hades. Cerberus had three dog heads, a serpent’s tail, and many snake heads along his back. Before going to him, Hercules went to Eumolpus at Eleusis to be initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries, but first had to be purified because he had not been cleansed from the killing of the Centaurs. After Eumolpus purified him, he was initiated, and he later came to Tainaron in Laconia.

THE LIBRARY, IIT. v. r1-12

Hercules descends into Hades through Taenarum to fetch Cerberus, encounters Theseus and Pirithous, and brings Cerberus to Eurystheus before returning him to Hades. After his labours, Hercules gives Megara to Iolaus, seeks to marry Iole, and kills Iphitus after a fit of madness. To be cured, he is sold by Hermes to Omphale for three years as compensation for the murder. During his servitude, Hercules performs various deeds, including seizing the Cercopes, killing Syleus, burying Icarus, and participating in the voyage to Colchis and the Calydonian hunt.

Μετὰ δὲ τὴν λατρείαν ἀπαλλαγεὶς τῆς νόσου

After recovering from his illness following the sacrifice, Heracles sailed to Ilium with eighteen penteconters and an army of willing distinguished men. He left the ships under Oicles and advanced on the city with the other nobles, but Laomedon killed Oicles while fighting. Heracles's forces besieged Laomedon, and Telamon broke through the wall and entered first, followed by Heracles. Heracles captured the city, shot Laomedon and his sons except Podarces, and awarded Hesione, Laomedon's daughter, to Telamon.

THE LIBRARY, II. vr. 3-4

Hercules captures Troy, Cos, Elis, and Pylus, defeats several enemies, and establishes or celebrates religious rites at Olympia. He later attacks Lacedaemon to avenge the death of Licymnius’s son, restores Tyndareus to power, and encounters Auge and her infant son Telephus. At Calydon, Hercules wins Deianira as his wife after defeating Achelous and receives the horn of Amaltheia in exchange for Achelous’s broken horn.

THE LIBRARY, II. vis. 4-5

Hercules came to Calydon and married Deianira after wrestling Achelous, who had taken the form of a bull. Hercules broke off one of Achelous’s horns, and Achelous recovered it by receiving Amalthea’s horn, which could provide abundant meat or drink. Hercules later campaigned with the Calydonians against Thesprotous, captured a city, and fathered Tlepolemus by Astyoche.

THE LIBRARY, II. vir. 6

Hercules marched with the Calydonians against the Thesprotians, took Ephyra, and fathered Tlepolemus with Astyoche. While feasting with Oeneus, he accidentally killed Eunomus, then accepted exile and set out toward Trachis with Deianeira. At the river Evenus, Nessus attempted to assault Deianeira while ferrying her, so Heracles shot him in the heart. Dying, Nessus gave Deianeira instructions for making a love charm from his blood and the substance from his wound.

THE LIBRARY, II. vu. 6-7

Hercules travels with Deianira to Trachis, defeats the Dryopes and other opponents, and later captures Oechalia from Eurytus, taking Iole captive. Deianira, fearing that Hercules will love Iole more than her, treats a tunic with what she believes is a love charm made from Nessus's blood. The Hydra's poison in the tunic corrodes Hercules's body, leading Deianira to commit suicide and Hercules to order a pyre built on Mount Oeta. Hercules dies on the pyre and is taken to heaven, where he becomes immortal and reconciles with Hera.

THE LIBRARY, IIL. vir. 7

Hercules went to Oeta in the Trachinian territory, built a pyre, and was carried to heaven when a cloud and thunder lifted him from the burning pyre. He became immortal, married Hebe, and had sons, including Alexiares and Anicetus, as well as many sons by the daughters of Thespius and other women. After Hercules was translated to the gods, his sons, also called the Heraclids, fled from Eurystheus to Athens, where the Athenians protected them and fought Eurystheus. The Athenians killed Eurystheus's sons, and Hyllus pursued and killed Eurystheus, giving his head to Alcmena.

THE LIBRARY, II. vir. 2

The Heraclids repeatedly attempt to return to the Peloponnese, interpreting an oracle about the third crop and the narrows before finally conquering the region under Oxylus's guidance. Aristodemus dies at Naupactus, and his sons Eurysthenes and Procles later share Lacedaemon after the drawing of lots, while Temenus receives Argos and Cresphontes receives Messene. The rulers establish signs for their territories, but Temenus's sons murder him because he favors Hyrnetho and Deiphontes. Cresphontes and two of his sons are murdered by Polyphontes, but his surviving son Aepytus returns secretly, kills Polyphontes, and recovers the kingdom.

BOOK III

The unit concludes the account of the family of Inachus from Belus to the Heraclids and begins the account of the house of Agenor. Libya and Poseidon had Belus and Agenor; Agenor went to Phoenicia, married Telephassa, and had Europa, Cadmus, Phoenix, and Cilix. Zeus carried Europa to Crete in the form of a tame bull, where she bore Minos, Sarpedon, and Rhadamanthys. After Europa disappeared, Agenor sent his sons to search for her and ordered them not to return until they found her, while Telephassa accompanied them.

THE LIBRARY, 111. 10

The unit recounts the descendants of Europa, including the careers of Minos, Sarpedon, Rhadamanthys, and their families. It describes Minos’s rise to power in Crete, the birth and confinement of the Minotaur, and the construction of the Labyrinth. It tells how Cadmus founded Thebes after killing a dragon and sowing its teeth, then married Harmonia. It also relates the death and resurrection of Glaucus and the death of Actaeon after he saw Artemis bathing.

V. Διόνυσος δὲ εὑρετὴς ἀμπέλου γενόμενος,

Dionysus, after discovering the vine and being driven mad by Hera, wanders through Egypt and Syria before traveling through Phrygia, Thrace, India, and Greece. Lycurgus, king of the Edonians, expels Dionysus and is later driven mad and killed by the will of Dionysus. In Thebes, Dionysus causes the women to engage in Bacchic frenzy, and Pentheus is torn apart by his mother Agave on Cithaeron. Later, Tyrrhenian pirates attempt to take Dionysus past Naxos, but he turns their ship's mast and oars into snakes, causing the pirates to leap into the sea and become dolphins.

Cadmus and Harmonia leave Thebes and go to the Encheleans, who make them leaders against the Illyrians; Cadmus then reigns over the Illyrians, and later Zeus sends the pair to the Elysian Fields after transforming them into a serpent. Polydorus becomes king of Thebes, and after Labdacus dies, Lycus rules while Labdacus's son Laius is still a child. Zethus and Amphion, the sons of Antiope, kill Lycus and Dirce, fortify Thebes, and expel Laius. Laius later stays with Pelops and carries off Chrysippus while teaching him to drive a chariot.

Γαμεῖ δὲ Ζῆθος μὲν Θήβην, ad ἧς ἡ πόλιες

Zethus married Thebe, while Amphion married Niobe, who bore seven sons and seven daughters according to this account, though other traditions gave different numbers. Niobe claimed to be more blessed with children than Latona, provoking Artemis and Apollo to kill her children, after which Niobe became a stone at Sipylus. After Amphion’s death, Laius ruled Thebes, but Oedipus unknowingly killed Laius, solved the Sphinx’s riddle, became king, and married Jocasta. When the truth was revealed, Jocasta hanged herself, Oedipus blinded himself, was expelled from Thebes, and later died at Colonus.

VI. ᾿Ἑτεοκλῆς δὲ καὶ ἸΠολυνείκης περὶ τῆς

Eteocles and Polynices agree that they will rule the kingdom of Thebes in alternate years, but Eteocles refuses to surrender the kingdom after his year, so Polynices goes to Argos. Adrastus reconciles Polynices and Tydeus, marries them to his daughters, and promises to restore them to their homelands; he then gathers the Seven Champions for the war against Thebes. Eriphyle accepts Polynices' necklace and persuades Amphiaraus to join the expedition, despite his foreknowledge that the champions will die. At Nemea, Hypsipyle leads the champions to water while leaving Opheltes behind, and a serpent kills the child; the champions kill the serpent, bury Opheltes, and establish the Nemean Games in his honor.

6 ᾿Αργεῖοε δὲ καθοπλισθέντες προσήεσαν τοῖς

The Argives armed themselves and approached the walls of Thebes, with seven leaders stationed at the seven gates. Eteocles armed the Thebans, appointed matching leaders, arranged the battle, and sought through divination to learn how the Thebans could defeat the enemy. The Theban seer Tiresias was blind but possessed prophetic abilities, and different accounts explained his blindness and powers. One account says Athena blinded him after he saw her naked, while another says Hera blinded him after he judged that women experience greater pleasure in intercourse, and Zeus gave him prophetic power.

Οὗτος οὖν Θηβαίοις μαντενομένοις 3 εἶπε νική-

Tiresias tells the Thebans that they will win if Menoeceus, son of Creon, sacrifices himself to Ares, and Menoeceus kills himself before the gates. During the battle at Thebes, the Cadmeans are driven back, Zeus strikes Capaneus with a thunderbolt, and the Argives begin to flee. Eteocles and Polynices fight a single combat over the kingdom of Thebes and kill each other. Melanippus wounds Tydeus, Athena attempts to make Tydeus immortal, but withholds the gift after Tydeus consumes Melanippus's brains; Zeus later makes Amphiaraus immortal after the earth swallows him with his chariot and charioteer.

VII. Κρέων δὲ τὴν Θηβαίων βασιλείαν παραλα-

Creon of Thebes refused burial to the Argive dead, but Antigone buried Polynices and was buried alive, after which Adrastus appealed to Athens and Theseus led the Athenians to recover the dead. Ten years later, the Epigoni attacked Thebes under Alcmaeon, defeated its defenders, and caused the Thebans to flee; the Argives then destroyed the city and dedicated spoils, including Manto, to Apollo. Alcmaeon later killed Eriphyle, was pursued for the killing, and after successive purifications received Callirrhoe from Achelous. His sons eventually avenged his death, dedicated the robe and necklace of Eriphyle at Delphi, and colonized Acarnania.

VIII. ᾿Επανάγωμεν δὲ νῦν πάλιν ἐπὶ τὸν Πε-

The unit recounts the lineage of Pelasgus through his son Lycaon and Lycaon’s fifty sons, including Nyctimus and Maenalus. Zeus tests the sons’ impiety by appearing as a day-labourer, but they serve him a sacrifice mixed with the entrails of a murdered child. Zeus overturns the table, destroys Lycaon and his sons except Nyctimus, and the flood of Deucalion occurs during Nyctimus’s reign. The unit also relates the story of Callisto, whom Zeus seduces and transforms into a bear before placing her among the stars.

IX. ᾿Αρκάδος δὲ καὶ Λεανείρας τῆς ᾿Αμύκλου

Arcas had two sons, Elatus and Aphidas, who divided the land, though Elatus held all the power. Elatus and Aphidas had descendants including Aleus, whose daughter Auge was seduced by Heracles and later married Teuthras, ruler of Mysia. Auge's exposed infant was named Telephus, was raised by herdsmen, and eventually became Teuthras's adopted son and successor. The unit also recounts the upbringing, contests, race, marriage, and later fate of Atalanta, including her pursuit by Melanion with golden apples from Aphrodite.
