  • In Dragon's Blood: Heywood is a young English hunter and foreigner in China, living in Stink-Chau in a house with dogs, debt, limited arms, and a cynical, increasingly taciturn outlook. He first meets Rudolph Hackh while shooting by the river, brings him into the settlement, hosts him, and becomes his close friend, companion, protector, and eventually proxy. He introduces Rudolph to the club and local figures, recognizes the rats as warning of plague, investigates killings and agitation connected with Fang the Sword-Pen, and admits that the town's terror and the likelihood of dying there trouble him. His loyalty and practical courage become central. Outraged by Doctor Chantel's insult, he fears Rudolph's duel will be fatal because Rudolph cannot fence; after trying to prevent it, he fights Chantel in Rudolph's place and helps repel rioters. Warned by Wutzler of coming violence, he infiltrates the White Lotus lodge, witnesses its ritual, survives discovery and attack, and returns with evidence of danger. As attack approaches, Heywood presides over the council while deferring to older men, presses for the nunnery as a fortified refuge, refuses to abandon native converts, and organizes scaffolding, loopholes, arms, and defenses. He warns Miss Drake to leave, yet remains beside her. Captain Kneebone's lottery win brings Heywood half-shares and an invitation Home, but the Black Dog's assault interrupts those hopes. During the siege, Heywood commands from the pony-shed roof, conserves ammunition, welcomes Rudolph back into the compound, directs resistance, investigates the Hakka boat and enemy tunneling, enters the mine, captures a coolie, and arranges an explosion in the enemy powder. As supplies fail, he helps prepare the women's escape. Seeing Wutzler's displayed body, he attacks Fang and vanishes, apparently dead. He then returns alive after escaping the attackers, killing pursuers, and reaching the boat by fisherman. Wounded only by an arrow scratch, he rejoins the group, sits quietly with Miss Drake, and proposes that Rudolph go Home with them.

Heywood in Dragon's Blood

Henry Milner Rideout · 1909

Heywood is a young English hunter and foreigner in China, living in Stink-Chau in a house with dogs, debt, limited arms, and a cynical, increasingly taciturn outlook. He first meets Rudolph Hackh while shooting by the river, brings him into the settlement, hosts him, and becomes his close friend, companion, protector, and eventually proxy. He introduces Rudolph to the club and local figures, recognizes the rats as warning of plague, investigates killings and agitation connected with Fang the Sword-Pen, and admits that the town's terror and the likelihood of dying there trouble him. His loyalty and practical courage become central. Outraged by Doctor Chantel's insult, he fears Rudolph's duel will be fatal because Rudolph cannot fence; after trying to prevent it, he fights Chantel in Rudolph's place and helps repel rioters. Warned by Wutzler of coming violence, he infiltrates the White Lotus lodge, witnesses its ritual, survives discovery and attack, and returns with evidence of danger. As attack approaches, Heywood presides over the council while deferring to older men, presses for the nunnery as a fortified refuge, refuses to abandon native converts, and organizes scaffolding, loopholes, arms, and defenses. He warns Miss Drake to leave, yet remains beside her. Captain Kneebone's lottery win brings Heywood half-shares and an invitation Home, but the Black Dog's assault interrupts those hopes. During the siege, Heywood commands from the pony-shed roof, conserves ammunition, welcomes Rudolph back into the compound, directs resistance, investigates the Hakka boat and enemy tunneling, enters the mine, captures a coolie, and arranges an explosion in the enemy powder. As supplies fail, he helps prepare the women's escape. Seeing Wutzler's displayed body, he attacks Fang and vanishes, apparently dead. He then returns alive after escaping the attackers, killing pursuers, and reaching the boat by fisherman. Wounded only by an arrow scratch, he rejoins the group, sits quietly with Miss Drake, and proposes that Rudolph go Home with them.

Narrative, section by section

18 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II THE PIED PIPER A gray smudge trailing northward showed where the Fa-Hien--Scottish Oriental, sixteen hundred tons--was disappearing from the pale expanse of ocean.

Heywood meets Rudolph while shooting near the river and escorts him to Stink-Chau. He hosts Rudolph for dinner, brings him to the club, and explains the isolated colony's harsh conditions. When the rats appear, he identifies them as the first notice of the plague.

" CHAPTER III UNDER FIRE The desert was sometimes Gobi, sometimes Sahara, but always an infinite stretch of sand that floated up and up in a stifling layer, like the tide.

Heywood jokes about the French school, Rudolph's bath, and their dangerous situation, then escorts Rudolph to the office. He brings Rudolph to the club and encourages him to participate in the entertainment. After hearing of Monsieur Jolivet's death, he reacts angrily and bitterly, but then leads the group in singing.

CHAPTER IV THE SWORD-PEN "Wutzler was missing last night," said Heywood, lazily.

Heywood declares a holiday and takes Rudolph to visit Captain Kneebone. He enjoys the river excursion, discusses illusions and Kneebone's hopes of winning a fortune, and later identifies Fang as the Sword-Pen and the Reverend James Earle as another dangerous man. After a self-stabbing in a Chinese shop, he recognizes that the crowd's hostility has been deliberately provoked and decides to find Earle and report.

CHAPTER V IN TOWN Night, in that maze of alleys, was but a more sinister day.

Heywood brings Rudolph to the Jesus Religion Chapel, listens skeptically but with some attention to Dr. Earle, and reports the killing of Chok Chung and the role of Fang the Sword-Pen. He then takes Rudolph to Wutzler’s house, where he eats, jokes, and later admits that the town’s terror and the prospect of dying there disturb him.

" CHAPTER VI THE PAGODA He was spared that inconvenience.

Heywood accompanies Rudolph on a twilight walk and explains that the placard by the pond says, "Girls May Not be Drowned in This Pond." He attends the tiffin at the ancient pagoda, interrupts Rudolph and Mrs. Forrester on the tower, and later mocks Rudolph by calling him the star after his performance and asking what price sympathy has on a pagoda.

CHAPTER VIII THE HOT NIGHT Rudolph paced his long chamber like a wolf,--a wolf in summer, with too thick a coat.

Heywood tries to calm Rudolph by attributing the men's behavior to the heat, but he becomes furious at Dr. Chantel's insult and considers fighting him himself. He supports Rudolph after the blow, worries that the duel may amount to murder because Rudolph cannot fence, and tells Rudolph that Chantel's criticism of the lady is probably justified.

" CHAPTER IX PASSAGE AT ARMS "Boy.

Heywood worries that Rudolph cannot fence and tries to prevent the duel, but he devises an accident that wounds Rudolph during practice. He takes Rudolph's place against Doctor Chantel, fights desperately, and helps drive off the rioters after the attack.

" CHAPTER X THREE PORTALS Not till after dinner, that evening, did Rudolph rouse from his stupor.

Heywood receives Wutzler's warning about impending riots and believes his account of the White Lotus. He follows Wutzler to the secret lodge, passes its three guarded portals using passwords, and enters its hidden passages alone.

CHAPTER XI WHITE LOTUS He was swarming up, quiet as a thief, when his fingers clawed the bare plaster.

Heywood climbs onto the long roof and spies through a chink into the rival lodge building. He witnesses the initiation ritual, is nearly discovered twice, and escapes after a lodge member attacks him with a spear. Wutzler guides him out of the town, after which Heywood washes the blood from his hair and returns to his house.

" CHAPTER XII THE WAR BOARD "Rigmarole?

Heywood presides over the council meeting and argues that the nunnery is the strongest place to fortify. He insists that the native converts must not be abandoned and plans bamboo scaffolding, sand-bags, loopholes, and a mound or platform for defense. He receives the magistrate, interprets the visit as a warning to look after themselves, and rebukes Chantel for mocking him.

" CHAPTER XIII THE SPARE MAN Beyond the scant greenery of Heywood's garden--a ropy little banyan, a low rank of glossy whampee leaves, and the dusty sage-green tops of stunted olives--glared the river.

Heywood watches Chok Chung's pursuit and shooting from the window and says that the event is only the first blood. He sends Rudolph to accompany Mrs. Forrester, later visits Rudolph for tiffin, and discovers that all but two of the Mausers have lost their side-bolts.

" CHAPTER XIV OFF DUTY The last of the sunlight streamed level through a gap in the western ridges.

Heywood tells Miss Drake that he discharged Rudolph's gatekeeper, installed his own trusted man, hired a diver, and recovered six of the stolen guns. He warns her repeatedly about the danger and urges her to leave when the next ship arrives, but he also remains with her as they approach the nunnery and read the threatening placards.

CHAPTER XV KAU FAI At the top of the nunnery stairs, Rudolph met them with awkward ceremony, and with that smiling air of encouragement which a nurse might use in trying cheerfully to deceive a sick man.

Maurice Heywood recognizes Captain Kneebone's arrival and encourages him to proceed with the dinner. He learns that Kneebone has won the Derby Sweepstake Lottery and intends to give him half of the fortune and take him Home. When the attack begins, Heywood identifies it as the Black Dog's attack and organizes the defense of the nunnery.

CHAPTER XVII LAMP OF HEAVEN The white men peered from among the oil-jars, like two of the Forty Thieves.

Heywood is stationed on the roof of the pony-shed and sharply stops Nesbit from wasting ammunition on a shadow. He recognizes Rudolph, has a rope lowered to him, and helps him enter the defended compound.

CHAPTER XVIII SIEGE He never believed that they could hold the northeast corner for a minute, so loud and unceasing was the uproar.

Heywood directs the defense with cheerful confidence, identifies the incoming projectiles as copper cash, and helps repel the Chinese assault. He helps carry Kempner's body to the nunnery, gives Gilly Forrester Kempner's rifle and belt, investigates the Hakka boat, and later confirms with Rudolph that digging is taking place beneath the defenses.

CHAPTER XIX BROTHER MOLES This new danger, working below in the solid earth, had thrown Rudolph into a state of sullen resignation.

Heywood interprets the enemy's digging as preparation for a single destructive attack and organizes an investigation of the tunnel. He enters the mine, discovers the powder, captures a returning coolie with Rudolph's help, and places a makeshift fuse in the enemy's larger powder supply. After the delayed explosion, he celebrates and calls Rudolph his brother mole.

CHAPTER XX THE HAKKA BOAT "Pretty fair," Captain Kneebone said.

Heywood becomes surly and contemptuous as the siege continues and ammunition runs low. After seeing what appears to be Wutzler's head above the wall, he orders preparations to get the women out and silently carries supplies to the water gate. Once aboard the Hakka boat, he sees Wutzler's body displayed on a bier, takes a halberd, leaps ashore, and strikes down the rider identified as Fang the Sword-Pen before disappearing over the crest.

CHAPTER XXI THE DRAGON'S SHADOW The lowdah would have set his dirty sails without delay, for the fair wind was already drooping; but at the first motion he found himself deposed, and a usurper in command, at the big steering-paddle.

Heywood unexpectedly boards the Hakka boat alive after escaping from the attackers and traveling by fisherman. He explains that he ran from the burning hospital, shot three pursuers with his Webley, and suffered only a scratch from an arrow in the shoulder. He quietly joins Miss Drake while the others sleep, then later calls to Rudolph and proposes that Rudolph go Home with them.
