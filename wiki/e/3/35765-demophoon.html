Demophoon

person · 4 works · 20 quoted facts · 11 connections
Also called: DEM., DEMOPHOON, Demophoonta, Theseus’ son, he, king, monarch of this realm, you, your

Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Demophoon, king of Athens, protects the Heraclidae as suppliants and refuses to surrender them. In Hecuba and other plays, Demophoon rules Athens, protects the Heraclidae as suppliants, and seeks to defend them without sacrificing his daughter or an Athenian citizen. In Heroides and Amores, Demophoon accepts Phyllis’s hospitality, aid, gifts, love, and trust, promises marriage and return, then remains absent, apparently having another wife. In The Fall of Troy, Demophoon recognizes Aethra, promises to rescue her with Acamas, and vows to bring her safely to Greece.

Demophoon in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Demophoon first appears

Abstract written by a model

Demophoon, son of Theseus and king of free Athens, rules the land and protects the Heraclidae as suppliants. When asked to surrender them, he refuses, invoking the altar’s sanctity, kinship, debt to Hercules, and the dishonor of forcible seizure.

Demophoon in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Demophoon first appears

Abstract written by a model

Demophoon is the constrained king and ruler of Athens, including Marathon, and a protector of suppliants. When Copreus seeks to remove the children of Hercules from the altar, he refuses, citing reverence, kinship, Hercules’ past services, and Athens’ honor. As Eurystheus and Argos approach, he summons citizens, arms Athens, and prepares sacrifices and lustrations. He will defend the foreign suppliants but refuses to sacrifice his daughter or any Athenian citizen, seeking a solution that saves Athens without public blame.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

THE CHILDREN OF HERCULES.

Demophoon hears the claims of Copreus and Iolaus and refuses to permit the children of Hercules to be dragged from the altar. He cites reverence for Jupiter, family ties, past services from Hercules, and the dishonor of surrendering suppliants through fear. He summons the citizens, prepares troops and seers, and promises to defend the suppliants.

DEMOPHOON, IOLAUS, CHORUS.

Demophoon reports that Argos, led by Eurystheus, is approaching with numerous troops. He has armed the city, arranged sacrifices and lustrations, and refuses to kill his daughter or compel an Athenian citizen to serve as the victim. He asks for a way to save both the country and the foreign suppliants without losing the citizens’ respect.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

II

Demophoon promised Phyllis that he would return, but he remains absent and has not kept his word. He accepted her hospitality, aid, gifts, love, and trust before departing, and Phyllis says he may now have another wife.
