Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, anger and revenge arise as dreadful, irremediable conflict between friends, as Medea seeks punishment for Jason and her enemies and lets revenge overcome affection. In Medea, vengeance kills the princess, king, and children, worsens Jason’s suffering, threatens him after the killings, and leaves him lonely while Medea escapes. In Hecuba and other plays, revenge and retaliation is used to justify punishment and sought by Hecuba against Polymestor for murdering her son and violating guest obligations. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, revenge is presented as justified punishment for the Athenian invasion, intended enslavement, and destruction of other cities, resulting in the execution of the generals and confederates and the imprisonment of the Athenian citizens. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, revenge motivates Progne against Tereus, as grief and anger lead her to murder Itys and feed his remains to Tereus. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, revenge drives Medea to send a poisoned robe, kill her children, and burn the palace; leads Althea to burn the fatal brand that kills Meleager; and gives Achilles consolation after Patroclus’s death before he abandons his refusal of mercy and returns Hector’s body.

Anger and revenge arise as dreadful, irremediable conflict between friends: Medea seeks punishment for Jason and her enemies, recognizes rage ruling her judgment, and lets revenge overcome affection, deciding against sparing her sons.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

The Chorus calls anger between friends dreadful and irremediable. Medea embraces revenge against Jason, declaring that her enemies will suffer punishment and that she will not tolerate being mocked by them.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Medea recognizes that her rage governs her judgment and drives her toward acts she knows are dreadful. She briefly considers sparing her sons, but her desire to punish her enemies overcomes her affection for them.

vengeance in Medea

Euripides · 430 BC · the work’s page · read it where vengeance first appears

Abstract written by a model

Vengeance in the Medea is a powerful yet morally and emotionally unsatisfying force. Born from Medea’s injuries and cruelty’s pain, it kills the princess, king, and children, worsens Jason’s suffering, threatens him after the killings, and leaves him lonely while Medea escapes.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Revenge in the Medea is presented as powerful but morally and emotionally unsatisfying. Medea’s injuries give her superhuman force, yet her punishment of Jason comes through a victim transformed into something like a devil, leaving the audience longing for explanation, forgiveness, or consolation.

Medea's vengeance reaches Jason through the deaths of the princess, the king, and their children. She tells Jason that he will live and die lonely, while she escapes and leaves him to grieve.

revenge and retaliation in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where revenge and retaliation first appears

Abstract written by a model

Revenge and retaliation is a principle used to justify punishment and sought by Hecuba against Polymestor for murdering her son and violating guest obligations. It unfolds through Hecuba’s secretly supported plan for Trojan women to act, while Menelaus calls retaliation just and Andromache condemns punishment of the uninvolved.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

AGAMEMNON, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Hecuba seeks vengeance against Polymestor for murdering her son and violating the obligations owed to a guest. She proposes that the captive Trojan women execute the plan, while Agamemnon secretly supports her and restrains opposition.

MENELAUS, MOLOSSUS, ANDROMACHE, CHORUS.

Menelaus says that it is just for the injured to retaliate, both at Troy and in the present conflict. Andromache condemns this reasoning as the punishment of her for a crime committed by another.

Revenge motivates Progne against Tereus: grief and anger after Philomela’s woven account lead her to consider violence, then murder Itys and feed his remains to Tereus.

opening

After reading Philomela's woven account, Progne becomes silent from grief and anger. She rushes to devise revenge, intending to overturn the ordinary boundaries of right and wrong.

opening

Revenge against Tereus drives Progne’s actions. She considers burning him, mutilating him, or killing him, and ultimately takes revenge by murdering Itys and feeding his remains to Tereus.

revenge in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where revenge first appears

Abstract written by a model

Revenge drives Medea after Jason puts her away to marry Creusa, leading her to send a poisoned robe, kill her own children, and burn the palace. Althea also seeks revenge for the deaths of her brothers and burns the fatal brand that kills Meleager. Achilles's principal consolation after Patroclus's death is revenge against Hector. His rage makes him irresistible in battle, but after Priam appeals to his compassion, he abandons his refusal of mercy and returns Hector's body.
