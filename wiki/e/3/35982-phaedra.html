  • In Heroides and Amores, Phaedra is the Cretan daughter of Minos and Pasiphae who falls in love with Hippolytus, writes to him, and begs him to accept her.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Phaedra, Theseus’s wife, falls in love with Hippolytus, falsely accuses him of attempting to ravish her, and hangs herself when she fears discovery.
  • In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Phaedra is Theseus’s Cretan wife who, overcome by love for Hippolytus, hangs herself and leaves a false accusation to protect her honour.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Phaedra is the Cretan queen and wife of Theseus who, consumed by forbidden love for Hippolytus, hangs herself and leaves a false letter accusing him.
  • In The Library, Phaedra is Minos’s daughter, Theseus’s wife, and Hippolytus’s stepmother who falsely accuses him after he rejects her and then hangs herself.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Phædra falls in love with Hippolytus, builds the Hippolyteum to see him, accuses him in a letter after his rejection, and dies by suicide.
  • In Description of Greece, Phædra becomes desperately enamoured of Hippolytus at Træzen, watches him train, and contrives his death after he rejects her.

Phaedra in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Phaedra, a Cretan maid, Minos and Pasiphae’s daughter, Ariadne’s sister, and Theseus’s wife, is seized by late, intense love for Hippolytus. Love wounds her, strips her modesty, and draws her to hunting. She writes and confesses, invoking her lineage, criticizing Theseus, and begging Hippolytus to accept her.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

IV

Phaedra confesses her love for Hippolytus and says that Love has made her write what modesty prevented her from saying. She describes herself as consumed by an unseen wound, adopts interests associated with Hippolytus's hunting life, and begs him to accept her. She invokes her family history, criticizes Theseus, and pleads with Hippolytus to soften his hard heart.

Phaedra in Hippolytus; The Bacchae

Euripides · -0427

Phaedra is a Cretan princess, daughter of Minos, queen and wife of Theseus, mother of children, and a woman in Troezen whose deepest concern is honour amid shame. Overwhelmed by a forbidden, grievous love for Hippolytus, said to come through Cypris, she becomes ill, fasts, and hides her inward grief. She first tries silence and endurance, but after the Nurse reveals her secret and Hippolytus rejects it, she chooses death to protect her honour, her children, and the Cretan house. She hangs herself and leaves a tablet, a false written accusation that Theseus treats as proof against Hippolytus. Artemis says Phaedra resisted the imposed love until the betrayal, then wrote the accusation and died.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS

Phaedra is brought from the castle in a weakened condition after fasting for three days. She speaks feverishly of springs, mountains, hunting, horses, and Artemis before admitting that she loves Hippolytus and that this love is the source of her grief and shame.

CHORUS

Phaedra tells the Chorus that, after discovering her love, she first tried to remain silent, then to endure it with purity, and finally chose death rather than live in shame. She rejects the Nurse's proposed cure after Hippolytus overhears the secret, blames the Nurse for betraying her, and resolves to die while preserving some honour for herself, her children, and the Cretan house.

Phaedra is found dead on a bier after hanging herself in the Castle. Her tablet is treated by Theseus as proof that Hippolytus violated her, while Hippolytus says that she cast away her life because there was no right in her.

Phaedra in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Phaedra is a Cretan queen, daughter of Minos and Pasiphaë, descended from Jove, and wife of Theseus. She is mother of Theseus’s children and comes from Crete before her marriage. What most determines her life is a fierce, unwilling, forbidden love for Hippolytus, imposed through Venus’s influence. Though she values chastity, honor, and her reputation, and fears disgrace for Theseus, her children, and the house of Crete, she cannot master the passion. In Trœzene she hides her condition from her attendants, remains indoors, abstains from food for three days, and wastes away in an illness identified as unlawful love. Her madness and restless longings contrast with her shame; she says she has attempted silence, chastity, and death rather than yield. After the Nurse reveals her love to Hippolytus, Phaedra fears exposure to Theseus. To escape disgrace and preserve honor, she hangs herself. Before dying, she leaves a false letter accusing Hippolytus. Found as a corpse, she becomes Theseus’s deceased wife whose letter and death persuade him of Hippolytus’s guilt. Later it is said that she wrote the accusation because she feared her conduct would be examined, while the truth remains unresolved until after her death.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS.

Phaedra has fallen desperately in love with Hippolytus through Venus’s design. She is wasting away secretly in Trœzene, keeps herself from food, and remains confined to the house while her illness is unknown to her attendants.

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

Phædra is severely ill, wastes away after abstaining from food for three days, and alternates between longing for the mountains, woods, fountains, and horses and shame at her own words. She confesses that she loves Hippolytus and says that she has tried silence, chastity, and death to resist the passion. She fears disgrace to Theseus, her children, and her household.

CHORUS, PHÆDRA.

Phædra learns that the Nurse has revealed her love for Hippolytus and fears that Hippolytus will report it to Theseus. She resolves to die rather than disgrace the house of Crete, hangs herself, and leaves a letter accusing Hippolytus.

HIPPOLYTUS, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Phaedra is identified as Theseus's deceased wife, whose letter accuses Hippolytus. Theseus treats her letter and death as decisive proof of Hippolytus's guilt.

DIANA, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Phaedra became enamored of Hippolytus and, fearing that her conduct would be examined, wrote a false letter accusing him. She dies before the truth can be fully established, but her death helps persuade Theseus of the accusation.

notes

Phaedra is described as struggling with shame and uncertainty before naming the man connected with her love. The notes discuss her illness, her assertions of chastity, and the double meaning in her claim that she had acted properly, a meaning Theseus does not understand.

Phædra in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Phædra is sent to Athens by Deucalion, falls in love with Hippolytus, and builds the Hippolyteum near Trœzen to see him. After his rejection, she accuses him in a letter to Theseus and dies by suicide, though one account places her death after learning of his death.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

EXPLANATION.

Phædra fell in love with Hippolytus and built a temple to Venus near Trœzen to see him more often. After Hippolytus rejected her, she either hanged herself after falsely accusing him in a letter, as several authors report, or died by stabbing herself after learning of his death, as Seneca relates.
