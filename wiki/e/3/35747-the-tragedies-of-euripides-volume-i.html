Narrative, section by section

HECUBA.

The Ghost of Polydore explains that Priam secretly sent him, with gold, to Polymestor in Thrace for protection, but Polymestor murdered him for the gold and cast his body into the sea. Achilles has appeared above his tomb and demanded Polyxena as a sacrifice, so the Greeks will take her from Hecuba. Polydore’s body is being carried by the waves toward the shore, where Hecuba will see it along with Polyxena’s corpse. Hecuba, once a queen of Troy, is now enslaved and suffers the destruction of her family and city.

HECUBA. CHORUS.

Hecuba learns that the Greeks have decreed the sacrifice of her daughter Polyxena at Achilles's tomb. Ulysses arrives to take Polyxena away, while Hecuba appeals to him by recalling that she once saved his life. Polyxena accepts death rather than slavery and indignity, and she is led away despite Hecuba's grief. The Chorus laments its enslavement and the destruction of Troy, wondering where the Trojan women will be taken.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Talthybius tells Hecuba how Polyxena was sacrificed at Achilles' tomb by the Greeks, describing her courage, dignity, and death. Hecuba, now a captive and bereft of her children, mourns Polyxena and asks to prepare her body for burial. The Chorus attributes Troy's destruction to the judgment of Paris and the war that followed Helen's abduction. The unit contrasts Hecuba's former royal prosperity with her present misery and reflects on the instability of human fortune.

FEMALE ATTENDANT, CHORUS, HECUBA.

Hecuba learns that her son Polydore has been murdered by Polymestor, who cast his body into the sea, after Polyxena was sacrificed by the Greeks. She appeals to Agamemnon to punish Polymestor, invoking hospitality, the gods, and justice. Agamemnon agrees to help if he can avoid opposition from the Greek army, and Hecuba plans to use the captive Trojan women to take revenge. The Chorus mourns the destruction of Troy and the enslavement and exile of its women.

POLYMESTOR, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Hecuba lures Polymestor into the tents by claiming that Polydore's gold and other treasures are hidden there. Hecuba and the Trojan women kill Polymestor's children and blind Polymestor in revenge for his murder of Polydore. Before Agamemnon, Polymestor claims that he killed Polydore to protect the Greeks and Thrace, while Hecuba argues that greed caused the murder. Agamemnon judges Polymestor guilty and orders him removed, while the Greeks prepare to sail from Troy.

notes

The unit consists of notes on Euripides’ Hecuba, including textual readings, translations, grammar, and mythological references. It discusses Hecuba’s parentage, her physical dependence in old age, and several passages involving Polymestor, Agamemnon, and the Chorus. Polymestor is described as having committed both injustice and impiety by murdering and leaving his victim unburied. The notes also identify Cynosema as Hecuba’s burial place and mention a naval battle fought there.

ORESTES.

Electra describes the cursed history of the Tantalids, the killing of Agamemnon by Clytæmnestra, and Orestes' subsequent murder of his mother at Apollo's command. Orestes has been afflicted with madness, weakened by hunger and illness, and persecuted by the Eumenides while Electra cares for him. Argos has decreed that Orestes and Electra must be excluded, and that the Argives will decide whether Orestes should die, but Electra hopes that Menelaus's return from Troy will bring help. Helen sends Hermione to offer libations at Clytæmnestra's tomb, and Menelaus then arrives at the palace.

MENELAUS, ORESTES, CHORUS.

Menelaus returns from Troy and finds Orestes living in extreme misery after killing his mother, Clytæmnestra, in revenge for Agamemnon's death. Tyndarus condemns Orestes and urges Menelaus not to save him, arguing that Orestes violated Greek law by committing matricide instead of pursuing a legal charge or accepting banishment. Orestes defends himself by claiming that Apollo commanded the killing and that he acted to avenge Agamemnon, while asking Menelaus to repay Agamemnon's aid by saving him and Electra. Menelaus promises to seek help through persuasion, but Orestes believes he has been betrayed when Menelaus leaves, and Pylades arrives from Phocis.

PYLADES, ORESTES, CHORUS.

Pylades joins Orestes and learns that Menelaus has refused to save Orestes and Electra, while Tyndarus has supported their condemnation. Orestes and Electra are tried by the Argive citizens for the murder of Clytemnestra and sentenced to die, though Orestes persuades the assembly to let them avoid stoning by promising self-slaughter. A Messenger reports the arguments made at the Argive assembly and describes Pylades supporting Orestes. The Chorus and Electra lament the madness, inherited violence, and declining fortune of the house of Pelops.

ELECTRA, ORESTES, PYLADES, CHORUS.

Orestes, Electra, and Pylades, condemned to die, resolve to avenge themselves on Menelaus by murdering Helen and, if necessary, setting the palace on fire. Electra proposes seizing Hermione as a hostage so that Menelaus will spare them, or killing Hermione if he refuses. Helen is attacked inside Orestes' house, while Hermione arrives from Clytemnestra's tomb and is seized at sword point. The Chorus guards the approaches and hears a Phrygian servant emerge from the palace to report what has happened.

PHRYGIAN, CHORUS.

The Phrygian recounts how Orestes and Pylades entered the palace, killed Clytemnestra, and attempted to kill Helen before Helen vanished and the Phrygian attendants fled. Orestes then threatens to kill Hermione and burn the palace unless Menelaus secures a judgment that will save him, Electra, and Pylades. Apollo appears, declares that Helen has been preserved as an immortal daughter of Zeus, and orders that Orestes stand trial at Athens, marry Hermione, and allow Pylades to marry Electra. Apollo also commands Menelaus to let Orestes rule Argos and to return to Sparta.

notes

The notes concern Euripides’ Orestes and explain textual readings, grammar, mythology, and editorial variants. Orestes is described as having avenged his father, Agamemnon, whose murder was committed by Clytæmnestra. Pylades is identified as Orestes’ friend, the son of Strophius, and one who voluntarily shares Orestes’ misfortunes.

THE PHŒNICIAN VIRGINS.

Jocasta recounts the history of Laius, Oedipus, and their children, including Oedipus's patricide and incest, his self-blinding, and the curse laid upon Eteocles and Polynices. She explains that Eteocles and Polynices agreed that Polynices would first go into exile while Eteocles ruled Thebes for a year, but Eteocles refused to surrender the throne. Polynices allied himself with Adrastus at Argos and brought an Argive army against Thebes, while Jocasta arranged a truce so the brothers could meet. Antigone and the Tutor view the approaching Argive leaders, and the Chorus of Phoenician Virgins describes its origin and fears the threatened war.

POLYNICES, CHORUS.

Polynices enters Thebes under a truce to meet his mother, Jocasta, and seek restoration of his share of the rule from his brother, Eteocles. Jocasta questions Polynices about exile, marriage to a daughter of Adrastus, and his leadership of the Argive army, then attempts to reconcile the brothers. Polynices argues that Eteocles broke their agreement to alternate rule, while Eteocles openly declares that he will not surrender kingship and would rather risk war. The meeting ends with mutual threats, as Polynices returns to the Argive forces determined to fight Eteocles, despite the curse of Œdipus and the danger to Thebes.

CHORUS.

Cadmus came from Tyre and founded a settlement in this land after a heifer guided him to the plains of the Aonians, as foretold by an oracle. He slew the dragon of Mars, and, following Pallas's counsel, scattered its teeth, from which an armed host sprang from the earth before its members killed one another. The chorus invokes Epaphus, descendant of Jove and ancestor of the founders, and calls on Proserpine and Ceres to send the fire-bearing Goddesses to defend the land.

ETEOCLES, CHORUS, MESSENGER.

Eteocles and Creon prepare Thebes for the Argive army by assigning seven commanders to defend the seven gates. Tiresias declares that Thebes can be saved only if Menoeceus, Creon's son, is sacrificed in the cave where the earth-born dragon died. Creon tries to send Menoeceus away, but Menoeceus secretly chooses to die for his country. The unit also recalls the curses and pollution arising from Oedipus and the conflict between his sons.

MESSENGER, JOCASTA, CHORUS.

A Messenger reports that Thebes has repelled the Argive attack, though both armies suffered heavy losses. Eteocles and Polynices agree to settle the succession by single combat, but each kills the other. Jocasta reaches her dying sons, laments over them, and kills herself, while Antigone accompanies her. The Messenger reports that the deaths of the brothers lead to a renewed clash in which the Thebans defeat the Argives and bring the spoils into the city.

CHOR. No longer the misfortunes of the house come to our ears, we may also

The Chorus says that the misfortunes of the house are no longer merely heard. It sees three fallen corpses before the palace, all of whom have entered the dark realms through a united death. The dead bodies are borne away.

ANTIGONE, CREON, CHORUS.

Antigone mourns Jocasta, Eteocles, and Polynices, recounting the destruction of the house of Oedipus and the deaths of the two brothers in their battle. Creon orders Oedipus into exile, forbids the burial of Polynices, and commands Antigone to prepare for marriage to Haemon. Antigone defies Creon by declaring that she will bury Polynices and refuses to marry Haemon or abandon Oedipus. Oedipus leaves Thebes with Antigone as his guide, destined to die in exile at Colonus near Athens.

notes

The unit consists of explanatory and textual notes on the Phœnician Virgins, covering mythology, geography, literary parallels, translation, and disputed readings. It identifies proposed emendations and omissions by Porson, Dindorf, Valckenaer, Musgrave, and others. It explains several Greek grammatical constructions, including ellipses, unusual syntax, omitted prepositions, and active or passive meanings. Additional notes address textual punctuation, conjectures, interpretations, and passages considered interpolated or corrupt.

MEDEA.

Medea is devastated because Jason has abandoned her and their children to marry Glauce, the daughter of Creon, king of Corinth. Creon orders Medea and her sons into exile, but grants her one additional day to remain in Corinth. Medea uses this delay to plan the deaths of Creon, Glauce, and Jason, considering fire, the sword, and poison. The Nurse, the Tutor, and the Chorus of Corinthian women fear Medea's anger, while the Chorus sympathizes with her suffering and her account of women's hardships.

CHOR. The waters of the hallowed streams flow upward to their sources, and

The Chorus says that justice and the order of nature are being reversed, while men’s counsels and the faith of heaven have become treacherous. It declares that fame and honor will come to the female race, and that the Muses will no longer celebrate women’s perfidy. Addressing Medea, the Chorus describes her as having left her father’s house, crossed the ocean, and settled in a foreign land after losing the protection of her widowed marriage bed. Medea is now dishonored and exiled, without a father’s house to shelter her, while a more powerful queen rules the nuptial bed and the house.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Jason defends his marriage to Creon's daughter and claims that it will secure prosperity and royal status for himself, Medea, and their children, while Medea condemns him for betraying her after she aided him. Medea, banished from Corinth, obtains from Aegeus a promise of refuge in his country and then develops a plan to kill Creon's daughter with poisoned gifts and to murder her own children. The Chorus condemns Medea's intended violence, while Aegeus's oath gives her a place of safety after she leaves Corinth.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Medea pretends to reconcile with Jason and sends their sons with poisoned gifts for Jason's new bride, asking that the children remain in Corinth. The gifts kill Creon's daughter and Creon, while Medea struggles between affection for her sons and her determination to take revenge on Jason. Despite the Chorus's alarm and the sons' cries for help, Medea kills them and prepares to flee. Jason's marriage, Medea's banishment, and the deaths caused by her gifts bring disaster upon the royal household.

JASON, CHORUS.

Jason arrives at the house intending to save his children from Creon’s relatives, but the Chorus tells him that Medea has killed them. Medea appears above the house in a chariot given to her by the Sun, refuses Jason access to the bodies, and declares that she will bury the children at Juno’s shrine. Jason and Medea bitterly accuse one another over the children’s deaths, their broken marriage, and Jason’s new marriage to the princess. Medea says she will go to Aegeus in the land of Erectheus, while the Chorus concludes that the Gods have brought about an unexpected end.

notes

The notes explain linguistic, mythological, textual, and interpretive issues in Euripides' Medea. They discuss Medea's marriage to Jason, her exile, her children, and her final appearance bearing their bodies in a dragon-drawn chariot. Elmsley, Porson, Dindorf, the Scholiast, and other commentators propose readings and interpretations of particular lines. The notes also compare passages from other ancient authors and works, including Homer, Virgil, Ovid, Plato, and Juvenal.

HIPPOLYTUS.

Venus declares that she will punish Hippolytus because he rejects marriage and honors Diana above her. She has caused Phaedra to fall desperately in love with Hippolytus, and she intends for Theseus to learn of it so that he will destroy his son. Hippolytus praises Diana, offers her a wreath, and openly rejects Venus. The Chorus of Trœzenian dames describes Phaedra’s mysterious illness and observes the Nurse bringing her from the palace.

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

Phædra lies sick, refuses food, and longs for hunting grounds and water before admitting to the Nurse that she loves Hippolytus. She explains that she has tried silence, chastity, and finally death to resist the love, fearing disgrace to Theseus, her children, and her household. The Nurse argues that Venus causes such passions and proposes a love potion requiring a token from Hippolytus. The Chorus observes Phædra’s suffering and warns that Venus’s power has brought misfortune upon the house.

CHORUS, PHÆDRA.

Phædra's love for Hippolytus is exposed by the Nurse, and Hippolytus angrily condemns women before leaving in silence because of his oath. Phædra resolves to die, hangs herself in her bridal chamber, and leaves a letter accusing Hippolytus of forcing himself upon her. Theseus returns, discovers her death and accusation, and invokes one of Neptune's promised curses against Hippolytus. The Chorus warns Theseus to withdraw the curse just as Hippolytus arrives.

HIPPOLYTUS, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Hippolytus denies Theseus's accusation that he defiled Theseus's wife and swears that he has remained sexually chaste. Theseus refuses to believe him, treats the letter left by the deceased woman as proof, and banishes Hippolytus from Athens and Troezen. After Theseus invokes his father, the ruler of the ocean, Hippolytus's horses are terrified by a bull from the sea, overturn the chariot, and drag Hippolytus to his death. The Messenger reports that Hippolytus was virtuous, while the Chorus reflects on the power of the Gods and Venus.

DIANA, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Diana reveals to Theseus that Phaedra falsely accused Hippolytus after falling in love with him, and that Theseus's curse caused his son's destruction. Hippolytus is brought in mortally injured, forgives Theseus, and dies after Diana promises him lasting honors at Troezene. Diana explains that Venus arranged the catastrophe because Hippolytus was chaste, while the Gods do not thwart one another's purposes. Theseus mourns his son, and the Chorus laments the disaster that has come upon the citizens.

notes

The notes explain textual, grammatical, and interpretive difficulties in Euripides’ Hippolytus, often proposing alternative readings or emendations. They discuss Phaedra’s illness, shame, love, and assertions of chastity, including her struggle to name the man involved. They also clarify the account of Hippolytus’s chariot accident, including his horses, reins, clothing, and the bull from the sea. Theseus’s interpretation of Phaedra’s conduct and innocence is noted as involving a double meaning that he does not understand.

ALCESTIS.

Apollo explains that he saved Admetus from death by persuading the Fates to accept a substitute, and that Alcestis has agreed to die for her husband when neither of Admetus's parents would do so. Death comes to claim Alcestis, while Apollo predicts that Hercules will later take her away from Death. Alcestis prepares for death, says farewell to her children and husband, and asks Admetus not to remarry or bring a stepmother into the household. Admetus promises to remain faithful to her and mourns the life they will no longer share.

CHOR. And I indeed with thee, as a friend with a friend, will bear this

Alcestis dies after sacrificing herself for Admetus, entrusting their children to him and asking him not to remarry. Admetus mourns her, orders extensive funeral observances, and condemns his parents for refusing to die in his place. Hercules arrives while Alcestis is being mourned, and Admetus nevertheless receives him as a guest without revealing the full extent of his grief. The Chorus praises Alcestis's devotion and Admetus's hospitality, while the children lament their mother's death.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Pheres and Admetus quarrel over Pheres's refusal to die for his son, and Pheres condemns Admetus for allowing Alcestis to die in his place. The servant of Admetus reproaches Hercules for feasting and singing while Alcestis is being mourned, then tells him that Alcestis has died. Hercules, shocked by the news and grateful for Admetus's hospitality, resolves to rescue Alcestis from Death and return her to the house of Admetus. The scene reflects on mortality, the uncertainty of life, and the obligations of hospitality.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

Admetus mourns Alcestis, who died in his place, and says that returning to his house and living without her is unbearable. The Chorus tells him to endure necessity and honor Alcestis, while Heracles arrives unaware of her death and later returns her after wrestling with Death at the tomb. Admetus initially refuses to accept the veiled woman Heracles brings, but recognizes her as Alcestis after taking her by the hand. Heracles says she must remain silent until the third day, and Admetus orders celebrations and sacrifices for the unexpected restoration of his happiness.

notes

These notes explain passages and translation issues in Euripides’ Alcestis, especially Alcestis’ approaching death, funeral preparations, and return from Death. They discuss Death as the masculine figure corresponding to Θάνατος, who cuts Alcestis’ hair and consecrates her to the dead. They also describe Greek customs concerning corpses, mourning, burial, purification, tombs, and funeral rites. Several notes compare the play with other Greek and Latin authors, Shakespeare, and ancient inscriptions.

THE BACCHÆ.

Bacchus arrives in Thebes in mortal form to vindicate his mother Semele and establish his worship after Pentheus rejects him and persecutes the Bacchæ. Bacchus has driven the women of Cadmus's house, including Agave, Autonoe, and Ino, into Bacchic madness on the mountain and directs his Asian Chorus in sacred dances. Tiresias and Cadmus prepare to honor Bacchus, but Pentheus condemns the rites, orders the arrest of the stranger identified with Bacchus, and threatens punishment for his followers. Tiresias and Cadmus warn Pentheus that his impiety and resistance to the god will bring grief upon his house.

SERVANT. Pentheus, we are here; having caught this prey, for which you sent

Pentheus's servant brings the stranger to Pentheus, reporting that the stranger submitted calmly to arrest while the Bacchae escaped from prison when their chains and doors were miraculously released. Pentheus questions and threatens the stranger, orders him imprisoned, and plans to enslave or sell the Bacchae, while the stranger warns that Bacchus will punish him. Bacchus frees himself through divine power, causes Pentheus's palace to shake and fall into ruin, and returns to the Chorus. When Pentheus reappears, Bacchus tells him that the god who produces the vine delivered him and announces that a messenger from the mountain is coming.

MESSENGER. Pentheus, ruler o'er this Theban land, I come, having left

A messenger reports to Pentheus that the Bacchae on Cithaeron perform marvelous rites, summon water, milk, wine, and honey, and overpower men and cattle. Pentheus plans an armed assault on the Bacchae, but Bacchus persuades him to disguise himself as a woman and go secretly to observe them. Bacchus then leads the deluded Pentheus toward the mountain, while the Chorus celebrates Bacchus and anticipates Pentheus's punishment by the Maenads. The unit presents divine power, madness, and reverence toward the gods as forces that overcome Pentheus's resistance.

MESS. O house, which wast formerly prosperous in Greece! house of the

The Messenger reports that Pentheus was lured onto a pine on Cithaeron and torn apart by Agave, Ino, Autonoe, and the Mænads after Bacchus commanded their attack. Agave returns to Thebes carrying Pentheus's head, believing it to be the head of a lion, but Cadmus leads her to recognize what she has done. Bacchus declares that the family is being punished for rejecting his divinity and decrees exile for Cadmus and Agave, while Cadmus and Harmonia are destined to become dragons and rule over barbarians. The Chorus concludes that the gods bring about unexpected events.

notes

The notes explain passages, textual difficulties, and mythological parallels in the Bacchae, a play attributed to Euripides. They discuss Bacchanalian rites, including drums, cymbals, ivy, animal skins, dancing, and initiation, as well as Dionysus’s associations with prophecy, night, caves, and human sacrifice. The central catastrophe concerns Pentheus, whose madness and delusion lead to his death and dismemberment by Agave and the Mænads. The notes compare the story with accounts by Hyginus, Ovid, Oppian, Nonnus, and other ancient and later authors.

THE HERACLIDÆ.

Iolaus brings the sons of Hercules to the altars of the Gods in the land of Athens after Eurystheus has driven them from Argos and other parts of Greece. Copreus, herald of Eurystheus, tries to drag the suppliants away, but Demophoon protects them and refuses to surrender them. Iolaus appeals to Athenian kinship, Theseus's debt to Hercules, the protection owed to suppliants, and the honor of a free city. Copreus threatens to return with an Argive army, while Demophoon declares that Athens will not yield the Heraclids.

CHOR. This land is ever willing to aid in a just cause those in difficulty;

Demophon prepares Athens to receive the approaching Argive army, but reports that the oracle requires the sacrifice of a noble-born virgin to save the city and the Heraclids. Iolaus and the children of Hercules remain as suppliants, while Macaria freely offers herself for sacrifice so that her brothers and their protectors may live. A servant announces that Hyllus has arrived with an Athenian army, and Iolaus insists on joining the battle despite his age. The Argive and Athenian forces are about to engage under the opposing divine favor of Juno and Minerva.

CHOR. O earth, and moon that shinest through the night, and most brilliant

The Chorus invokes the earth, moon, divine rays, and Minerva for news and protection. The speaker says that, for the sake of the country and house, the speaker has received suppliants and is prepared to face danger rather than surrender them to Argos. Mycenae is described as a prosperous and warlike city that secretly resents the speaker's land. The speaker calls on Minerva and Jupiter to defend the land and prevent the hostile force from Argos from driving the speaker away.

SERV. O mistress, I bring news most concise for you to hear, and to myself

A servant reports to Alcmena that the Athenians and the descendants of Hercules have defeated the Argives, while Iolaus has been rejuvenated and has captured Eurystheus. Eurystheus is brought before Alcmena, who demands his death despite the Athenian law against executing enemies captured alive. Eurystheus reveals Apollo's prophecy that his tomb near the temple of the divine virgin of Pallene will protect Athens and harm its descendants' enemies. Alcmena orders the servants to kill him and give his body to his friends, while the Chorus approves her decision.

notes

The unit consists of notes on the Heraclidae, especially their exile, military arrangements, religious observances, and related tragic passages. It discusses Macaria's voluntary sacrifice after an oracle required that a descendant of Hercules be devoted. The notes also compare passages from Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and other authors, while addressing disputed readings, emendations, and grammatical constructions.

IPHIGENIA IN AULIS.

Agamemnon tells the Old Man that Calchas has declared that Iphigenia must be sacrificed to Diana so that the Greeks can sail to Troy, but he has changed his mind and writes to Clytaemnestra ordering her not to send their daughter to Aulis. Menelaus intercepts the letter and accuses Agamemnon of abandoning Greece after previously arranging Iphigenia’s supposed marriage to Achilles as a pretext for bringing her to the army. Agamemnon defends his decision by insisting that he will not kill his daughter for Menelaus’s sake or to recover Helen. The brothers quarrel over leadership, popular opinion, loyalty, and Menelaus’s demand that Agamemnon support the expedition against Troy.

[_Enter a Messenger_.[29]]

A Messenger announces that Clytaemnestra, Iphigenia, and Orestes have arrived at Aulis, believing Iphigenia is to marry Achilles. Agamemnon struggles with the necessity of sacrificing his daughter to secure the Greek expedition, while Menelaus reverses his earlier demand and urges him not to kill her. Clytaemnestra questions Agamemnon about the wedding and refuses to return to Argos, while Iphigenia joyfully greets her father without knowing his plan. The Chorus describes Paris and Helen's union, the coming Greek attack on Troy, and the suffering that will follow.

Achilles meets Clytæmnestra and learns from the Old Man that Agamemnon has deceived her by presenting Iphigenia’s journey as a marriage to Achilles. The Greek army intends to sacrifice Iphigenia to Diana so that the expedition can sail to Troy and Menelaus can recover Helen. Achilles promises to oppose the sacrifice and urges Clytæmnestra first to persuade Agamemnon by words. The chorus recalls the wedding of Peleus and Thetis and contrasts it with the fatal marriage-like preparation of Iphigenia.

Iphigenia resolves to die willingly so that the Greek army can sail to Troy and Greece can be protected from barbarian rule. She rejects Achilles' offer to defend her and asks Clytemnestra not to mourn her with funeral rites, identifying Diana's altar as her memorial. Achilles promises to resist the sacrifice, but Iphigenia is led toward the altar while the Chorus prepares songs, offerings, and ritual observances. Her sacrifice is presented as the source of Greek victory, Troy's destruction, and Iphigenia's lasting renown.

[_Enter a_ MESSENGER.[102]]

The Messenger tells Clytæmnestra that her daughter willingly offered herself for sacrifice on behalf of Greece, but Diana replaced her with a stag at the altar. Calchas declares that the divine substitution promises the Greek army a safe voyage and victory against Troy. Agamemnon confirms the report, says that the girl now dwells with the Gods, and urges Clytæmnestra to return home with Orestes while the army prepares to sail.

IPHIGENIA IN TAURIS.

Iphigenia explains that Diana rescued her from sacrifice at Aulis and made her priestess in Tauri, where Grecian strangers are sacrificed. Orestes and Pylades arrive from Argos seeking Diana's image because Phœbus's oracle promised Orestes relief from the Furies. Iphigenia mourns a dream that she interprets as Orestes' death, while a herdsman reports that two Greek strangers have been captured after Orestes became mad and attacked cattle. The strangers are brought to the king and sent to Iphigenia for lustral waters and sacrifice.

Iphigenia, serving Artemis in a land where Greeks are sacrificed, prepares to conduct the rites for Orestes and Pylades, who have come from Argos in chains. She plans to fetch the letter and prepare Orestes for sacrifice, while the Chorus reflects on the Greeks’ sea voyage and Iphigenia’s longing for home.

notes

The unit consists of critical and explanatory notes on Euripides’ Iphigenia in Tauris, including the play’s plot, textual problems, mythological references, and proposed emendations. It discusses Iphigenia’s intended sacrifice at Aulis, her removal through the air by divine agency, and her service as a priestess in Tauris. It also treats Orestes’ wanderings, his encounter with Iphigenia, and the escape from Tauris, while repeatedly evaluating the Cambridge editor’s readings. The notes compare passages in the play with works by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Ovid, Virgil, and other authors.
