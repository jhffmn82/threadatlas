  • In Hecuba and other plays, Creon is the former king of Thebes and father of Megara, whom Lycus kills before seizing Thebes and fearing vengeance from Hercules’ children.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Creon serves as Theban envoy, regent, king, and guardian of the royal family; he seeks to control Oedipus, forbids Polynices’ burial, condemns Antigone, and recognizes too late that his decree caused the disaster.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Creon rules Thebes after Eteocles and Polynices die, honors Eteocles’ burial, forbids Polynices’ burial, and sentences Antigone to be buried alive.
  • In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Creon serves as Theban royal figure, guardian and later ruler who places state authority above family, condemns Antigone, and recognizes too late that his decisions caused the deaths of Haemon and Eurydice.

Creon in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Creon is a Theban prince, son of Menoeceus, brother of Jocasta, husband of Eurydicè, father of Haemon, and uncle of Antigone. Through Jocasta he is Oedipus’s brother-in-law, kinsman, trusted friend, and a third partner in rule. He first appears as an envoy from Thebes: he returns from Delphi with Apollo’s message that Laius’s murderer must be expelled or answered with blood, and he advises Oedipus to summon Tiresias. Oedipus later suspects him of plotting for the throne, but Creon denies it, saying he already shares rule’s benefits without its dangers; Jocasta and the Chorus prevent Oedipus from punishing him. After Oedipus’s fall, Creon serves as regent, succeeds to Oedipus’s former kingship, cares for Oedipus’s children, and commands Oedipus’s banishment with the sons’ consent. Later, sent by Thebans, he tries to bring Oedipus from Colonus to Thebes in order to control his burial. When refused, he has Antigone taken by force, threatens to seize Oedipus, and admits capturing Oedipus’s two children in retaliation for Oedipus’s curses; Theseus compels him to lead the way toward them. As newly ruling king of Thebes after Polynices and Eteocles die, Creon makes state loyalty his highest principle. He grants Eteocles burial honors but forbids burial and mourning for Polynices, threatens disobedience with death, and treats the unauthorized burial as bribery or greed. He interrogates Antigone, rejects Ismene’s shared guilt and Haemon’s objections, and orders Antigone confined in a tomb. Tiresias accuses him of divine pollution. Only after prophecy and the Chorus’s urging does he reverse the decree, resolve to bury Polynices, and free Antigone. The reversal comes too late: Haemon dies, Eurydicè kills herself, and Creon acknowledges that his decree and folly caused the disaster. He calls himself his son’s killer, recognizes his folly too late, and asks to be taken away.

Narrative, section by section

11 sections; each heading opens the text

ANTIGONE

Creon, newly ruling Thebes, declares that Eteocles will receive full burial honors while Polynices will remain unburied. He insists that loyalty to the state must come before friendship and threatens death for anyone who disobeys his order.

CHORUS.

Creon interrogates Antigone, condemns her defiance, and insists that his authority and the State's commands must be obeyed. He rejects Ismene's attempt to share Antigone's guilt and refuses Haemon's arguments that Antigone's punishment is unjust. He orders Antigone's death by confinement in a cave-like tomb.

CHORUS

Creon orders Antigone to be sealed in the tomb and continues to refuse burial for Polynices. After Tiresias warns him of divine punishment and the Chorus urges him to act, Creon decides to release Antigone and bury Polynices himself.

CHORUS.

Creon enters carrying Haemon’s corpse and admits that his folly and decree caused the disaster. After learning of Eurydicè’s death, he calls himself the killer of his son and asks to be taken away.

KING OEDIPUS

Creon, Jocasta's brother, returns from Delphi with Apollo's message. He reports that Laius's murderer must be driven from the land or answered with blood.

[OEDIPUS _has entered during the Choral song_

Oedipus suspects that Creon has suborned Tiresias to undermine and remove him from the throne. Tiresias denies that Creon is responsible and says that Oedipus is his own sole enemy.

CHORUS.

Creon denies conspiring against Oedipus and argues that he has no reason to seek kingship because he already shares the benefits of rule without its dangers. Oedipus continues to call him a villain and threatens him with death or exile, but Jocasta and the Chorus persuade Oedipus to spare him. Creon leaves, declaring that he is untainted in the eyes of others even though Oedipus is blind to the truth.

CHORUS.

Creon arrives as regent in Oedipus's place and refuses to act solely on Oedipus's request until the god has been consulted. He agrees to care for Oedipus's daughters and leads Oedipus into the palace.

OEDIPUS AT COLONOS

Creon is identified in the introductory account as having consented to Oedipus's banishment and as having supported the exclusion of Polynices from Thebes.

_Enter_ THESEUS.

Creon comes from Thebes claiming that he wants to bring Oedipus back to his ancestral home. When Oedipus refuses, Creon orders Antigone taken by force and threatens to seize Oedipus, provoking resistance from the Chorus.

Creon in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Creon is a Theban royal figure: Jocasta’s brother, Menoeceus’s son, uncle and later guardian to Oedipus’s children, husband of Eurydice, and father of Haemon. In the time of Oedipus, he shares royal power yet values the security of influence over a troubled reign. He consults Apollo at Delphi for Thebes and reports that Laius’s murder must be purged; when Oedipus accuses him of conspiracy, he denies wanting the throne and offers verification. After Oedipus’s fall, he becomes guardian of the state, arranges Jocasta’s burial, consults the gods about Oedipus’s exile, and promises care for Antigone and Ismene. Later, as Thebes’s ruler, he tries to keep Oedipus under Theban control, even seizing Antigone and threatening Oedipus and Ismene before Theseus forces their restoration. As king after Eteocles and Polyneices die, Creon makes the state supreme: he honors Eteocles, forbids Polyneices’ burial, threatens dissenters, and punishes Antigone for defying his decree. Rejecting family ties and Haemon’s counsel, he orders Antigone sealed alive and believes himself free of blame. He initially dismisses Teiresias, then reverses himself too late. Haemon dies, Eurydice kills herself, and Creon recognizes that his own decisions caused these deaths; he bears guilt and punishment for his errors.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

OEDIPUS THE KING

Creon returns from consulting Apollo at Delphi and reports that Thebes must expel the pollution caused by Laius’s murder. Oedipus later suspects Creon of conspiring with Teiresias to seize the throne.

CREON.

Creon denies conspiring against Oedipus and argues that he has no desire to be king because he already enjoys influence and security through Oedipus. He offers to have his account tested at Delphi and is allowed to leave after Jocasta and the Chorus intervene.

[Enter SECOND MESSENGER.]

Creon becomes the sole guardian of the state in Oedipus’s place and enters without mocking or reproaching him. He says that the gods must be consulted about exile, promises to arrange Jocasta’s burial, brings Antigone and Ismene to Oedipus, and agrees to care for them.

OEDIPUS AT COLONUS

Creon is identified in the argument as Jocasta’s brother and the ruler then reigning at Thebes. He does not appear in this unit’s scene.

[Enter ISMENE]

Creon is coming to Oedipus with the intention of placing him near Theban territory while preventing him from passing beyond their boundaries. Ismene says that Creon seeks to keep Oedipus within Thebes's grasp because his tomb could bring a curse upon Thebes if he dies elsewhere.

[Enter CREON with attendants]

Creon comes as a representative of Thebes, claiming that he mourns Oedipus and wants him to return home. After Oedipus refuses, Creon has Antigone seized and threatens to take Oedipus's other daughter and Oedipus himself; Theseus condemns his actions and orders him to restore the maidens.

ANTIGONE

Creon claims the throne after Eteocles and Polyneices die and declares that the state must come before private friendship. He orders Eteocles to receive burial honors and Polyneices to remain unburied, threatens death for anyone who disobeys, and accuses the guards of taking bribes when Polyneices’ corpse is covered with dust.

[Enter GUARD bringing ANTIGONE]

Creon questions the Guard and Antigone about the burial and insists that his interdict must be obeyed. He declares that Antigone’s defiance and pride deserve the utmost penalty, condemns her to death, and orders the guards to take both Antigone and Ismene away. He also refuses to spare Antigone because she is his sister’s child or because she is Haemon’s bride.

[Enter HAEMON]

Creon insists that rulers must be obeyed and that the State cannot tolerate rebellion. He rejects Haemon’s advice, orders Antigone to be sealed alive in a rock-hewn tomb, and remains convinced that he is guiltless of her blood.

[Enter TEIRESIAS and BOY]

Creon rejects Teiresias’s warning and accuses prophets of greed, but he eventually accepts the Chorus’s advice to free Antigone and bury Polyneices. He arrives too late to prevent Haemon’s death, then learns of Eurydice’s suicide and acknowledges that his own decisions caused the deaths.
