Jason's new marriage to the princess in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Jason's new marriage to the princess first appears

Abstract written by a model

Jason’s new marriage joins him to Glauce, Creon’s daughter, in a royal and politically advantageous alliance. It replaces his relationship with Medea, breaks the household’s former bonds, and causes Medea’s exile. Jason claims it will protect Medea and their children, give his sons royal brothers, and secure prosperity, but Medea sees it as a hateful betrayal made without her knowledge. Regarding the marriage as the injury that provokes her revenge, Medea sends wedding gifts that kill the new bride and Creon, leaving Jason unable to enjoy the nuptials.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

MEDEA.

Jason's marriage to Glauce has replaced his relationship with Medea and has caused the household's former bonds to break down. Medea describes marriage as especially dangerous for women because a woman must accept a husband without knowing his character and cannot easily repudiate him.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Jason says that his marriage to the princess is intended to preserve Medea and their children, produce royal brothers for his sons, and secure an honorable and prosperous household. Medea regards the marriage as a betrayal made without her knowledge.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Jason's new marriage to Creon's daughter is presented by Jason as an advantageous alliance that will benefit him and the children. Medea uses its wedding gifts to kill the new bride and her father.

JASON, CHORUS.

Jason’s new marriage to the princess is identified by Medea as the injury that provoked her revenge. Medea says the princess and Creon expected to drive her away with impunity, while Jason laments that he will not enjoy his new nuptials.

the marriage of Jason and Creon's daughter in Medea

Euripides · 430 BC · the work’s page · read it where the marriage of Jason and Creon's daughter first appears

Abstract written by a model

Jason's new marriage to Creon's daughter, arranged by Creon, causes Medea's abandonment and grief; Creon protects his daughter, while Medea curses the bride and pretends acceptance to gain access for revenge.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

MEDEA

Jason's planned marriage to Creon's daughter is the cause of Medea's abandonment and grief. Creon says that he is acting to protect his daughter, while Medea curses the bride and the house connected with the marriage.

CHORUS.

Medea pretends that she now accepts Jason’s marriage to a queen and calls the arrangement wise and beneficial. She uses this apparent acceptance to gain access for her revenge.
