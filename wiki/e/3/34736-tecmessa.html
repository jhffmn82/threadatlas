Tecmessa in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · 449 to 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Tecmessa first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tecmessa is a captive woman of noble Phrygian lineage, daughter of Teleutas, and Aias’ concubine, housemate, and dependent, though she calls him husband and master. Loving Aias and mother of their son Eurysakes, she fears that his death will leave them enslaved and humiliated. She recounts Aias’ raid, cruelty to animals, and grief after his madness ends, then begs him not to abandon her and their child. Sensing his fatal intent, she seeks him after a warning, finds him dead, mourns him, covers his body, summons Teucer, and brings Eurysakes for burial.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

AIAS

Tecmessa describes Aias' night raid, his torture of the animals, and his collapse into grief after learning what he has done. She seeks help from the Salaminian Mariners and explains that Aias' present sorrow is worse for those who care for him because his madness has ended.

Of Aias, and his fortunes as they are. [AIAS _is discovered_

Tecmessa begs Aias not to abandon her and Eurysakes to slavery and humiliation. She brings their son to him, hears his instructions concerning the child, and later recognizes that his apparent change of heart has concealed a fatal purpose. After hearing the messenger's warning, she orders the sailors to search for Aias and goes to seek him herself.

_The scene changes to a lonely wooded spot._

Tecmessa discovers Aias newly dead and mourns him, saying that she and Eurysakes are being driven toward slavish and difficult circumstances. She covers his body, calls for Teucer, and later brings Eurysakes to take part in the burial.

Tecmessa in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where Tecmessa first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tecmessa is Aias’s captive bride, household-queen, and mother of Eurysaces, promised queenship of Salamis. After Aias’s suicide, she laments his corpse, lost return to Salamis, and feared enslavement for herself and her son; Agamemnon promises their protection.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Then Aias saw those sheep upon the earth

Tecmessa, Aias's captive bride and household-queen, falls upon his corpse and laments his suicide. She grieves that Aias will never return with her and their son to Salamis, and fears that their child will become a wretched thrall and that she will again face bondage. Agamemnon promises that neither she nor her son will be enslaved while Teucer or Agamemnon lives.
