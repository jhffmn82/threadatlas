  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, she is Creon's daughter who is courted and married by Jason and dies with Creon in the burning Corinthian palace, or from poisoned plasters in another account.
  • In Medea, she is Jason's new bride, a princess of Corinth and daughter of Creon, who accepts Medea's poisoned robes and crown and dies from them.

  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, she is Creon's daughter and Jason's wife who dies when Medea's burning crown consumes her.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, she is identified as Creüsa, another name for Glauce, Jason's wife who is consumed in the burning of Creon's palace or dies from the burning crown in Apollodorus's version.
  • In The Library, she is Creon's daughter who becomes Jason's wife after his divorce from Medea and dies when Medea's poisoned robe burns her.

Jason's new bride in Medea

Euripides · -0430

Jason's new bride is a maiden princess, daughter of Creon, the king and ruler of Corinth, and queen in Jason's nearer house through his political remarriage. Her elevation above Medea makes her a target of Medea's curse and planned killings. She accepts the robes and golden crown delivered by Medea's children, an act that spares the children from exile. The poisoned gifts burn her in agony, killing her before her father arrives.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

MEDEA.

The bride is the woman who has been married to Jason and placed above Medea in the nearer house. Medea includes her among the three people who will die, and the Chorus describes her as queenlier and dearer in that household.

_Enter from the right_ JASON.

Jason's new bride is described as the daughter of the ruler of Corinth and as the woman whom Jason has taken to rule his house. Medea curses the marriage and predicts that the bride may bring Jason suffering before he kisses her.

CHORUS.

The royal bride accepts Medea’s gifts and thereby spares the children from exile. The Chorus foresees that the poisoned robe and crown will kill her.

[_Enter_ MESSENGER.

The princess receives the robes and golden crown brought by Medea's children. The gifts burn her, and she dies in agony before her father arrives.

Glauce in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Glauce, Creon’s daughter and a princess, becomes Jason’s newly married bride and mistress of the house. Medea marks her for death, sending a fine robe and golden chaplet with deadly charms. Glauce receives and wears the gifts while admiring herself; they burn her body, destroy her flesh, and kill her in the bridal house.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

MEDEA.

Glauce is Creon's daughter and Jason's newly married bride. Medea identifies her as one of the three people she intends to kill.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Creon's daughter becomes Jason's new bride and the mistress of the house. Medea plans to kill her by sending her a fine robe and a golden-twined wreath anointed with deadly charms.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Creon's daughter receives Medea's robe and golden chaplet and puts them on while admiring herself. The poisoned gifts set her body on fire, destroy her flesh, and cause her death.

Glauce, also called Creüsa, was the daughter of Creon and became Jason’s wife. Medea sent a crown to her rival in the account attributed to Apollodorus; when Glauce put it on, it burned her to death.
