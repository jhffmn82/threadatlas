Lentulus in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

Lentulus is a man remembered as fair in society: well-looking, polite, acceptable, sociable at clubs and dinners, and supported by a business in which he does nothing. Benevolent and free from envy, hatred, and malice, he nevertheless has vast, ignorant confidence in his own rambling originality. He dismisses poets, philosophers, and scientific thought as mistaken, knows little of particular poets or doctrines, and defines poetry as artificial language expressing unreal sentiments. He claims to compose superior poetry spontaneously, considers writing on philosophic thinking, and expects his views on electricity to offend the scientific world. Yet he never clearly develops these reformative ideas; his silence leaves his vague, addled conclusions harmless.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

IV.

Lentulus is presented as a well-positioned, polite, sociable, and benevolent man who appears unpretending but privately believes that poets, philosophers, and scientists have largely been mistaken. He claims to compose superior poetry as he goes along and to possess abundant reformative thoughts, though he knows little of particular poets or established systems and never develops his ideas clearly. His silence keeps his addled originalities and vague conclusions from doing much harm.
