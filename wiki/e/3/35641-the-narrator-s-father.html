the narrator's father in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

The narrator’s father was an English country parson, a Tory, and a small-scale pluralist, born about the same time as Scott and Wordsworth. Raised in close contact with aristocracy and experienced among miners, weavers, labourers, farmers, and other parishioners, he believed clergy should share something of every class. He carefully collected tithe and relied on a recurring year’s supply of sermons, yet remained beloved because he kept no lasting quarrel with rich or poor. He revered Government, Church, and Constitution, regarded rebels and revolutionary teachers as evil or foolish, and held that national welfare required strong rule, order, gratitude, and obedient subjects.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

II.

The narrator's father was a country parson who knew miners, weavers, field-labourers, farmers, aristocrats, and other parishioners through close daily contact. He was a Tory, collected his tithe carefully, composed or reused a limited supply of sermons, and was nevertheless beloved because he had no lasting feud with rich or poor. He taught that a clergyman should feel in himself a bit of every class.

That part of my father's prime to which he oftenest referred had fallen on the d

The narrator's father regarded revolutionary teachers as a mixture of fools and scoundrels and believed that national welfare depended on a strong Government maintaining order. He admired England's Church and Constitution, praised rulers from Mr Pitt to the Duke of Wellington before Catholic emancipation, and blamed national troubles less on administrators than on subjects who failed to be grateful and mind their own business.
