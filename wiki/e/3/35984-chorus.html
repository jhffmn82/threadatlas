Chorus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Chorus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Chorus is a group of noble women, daughters of Trœzene. It describes a spring near the sea, reports Phædra’s fasting, illness, and wish for death, and considers divine or domestic causes. It questions the Nurse, reacts to Phædra’s revealed love, praises chastity, and laments Venus’s power. After Phædra’s secret is exposed, it swears secrecy before Diana, mourns her death, and warns Theseus to recall his curse against Hippolytus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS.

The Chorus describes a spring near the sea and reports hearing that Phaedra has been ill, has kept her body untouched by food for three days, and wishes to hasten toward death. The Chorus speculates about divine possession, an offense against the gods, Theseus’s possible concubinage, or a troubling message from Crete.

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

The Chorus asks the Nurse about Phædra’s condition and observes that she is weak, wasted, and refusing food. It reacts with alarm when Phædra’s love is revealed and praises chastity while lamenting the destructive power of Venus.

CHORUS, PHÆDRA.

The Chorus reflects on the destructive power of love and Venus, witnesses the exposure of Phædra's secret, and promises not to reveal it. It laments Phædra's death and warns Theseus to recall his curse against Hippolytus.

the Trozenian women in Hippolytus; The Bacchae

Euripides · 427 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Trozenian women first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Trozenian women are a chorus of fifteen women of Trozên, dwelling in Pelops’ land. They gather outside the castle to seek the cause of Phaedra’s wasting illness, question the Nurse, love Phaedra sympathetically, and swear silence about her griefs. They praise her virtue, sing of Erôs and Cypris’s destructive power, and foresee her hanging. After witnessing Phaedra’s death, Theseus’s accusation, and Hippolytus’s exile, they lament ruined royalty, wrongdoing, uncertain divine justice, Hippolytus’s suffering, and the people’s grief after his death.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS

The Trozenian women gather outside the castle and speculate about the cause of Phaedra's wasting illness. They wonder whether a god, a forgotten sacrifice, Theseus, or news from Crete has caused her suffering, and they question the Nurse about it.

CHORUS

The Chorus responds to Phaedra's suffering with sympathy, praises her concern for virtue and honour, and promises not to reveal her secret. It sings of the destructive power of Erôs and Cypris, then mourns Phaedra's fate and foresees her hanging herself in the bridal room.

The Chorus sings of Cypris's power over gods, humans, and all living things. It later mourns the disaster, Hippolytus's suffering, and the great grief that falls upon the people after his death.
