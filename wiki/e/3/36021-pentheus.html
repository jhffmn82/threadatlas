Pentheus in The Bacchae of Euripides

Euripides · -0406/-0405

Pentheus is a mortal man, son of Agave and Echion and grandson of Cadmus, who becomes king and ruler of Thebes after Cadmus yields the throne. Defined by wrath and by religious and political opposition to Dionysus, he rejects Dionysus's divinity and rites. He orders worshippers arrested, seeks the stranger's capture and execution, commands Teiresias's dwelling destroyed, and has Dionysus bound and imprisoned. Attempting to punish him, Pentheus is deceived, attacks emptiness, and is left exhausted after his house is destroyed. After Dionysus escapes, Pentheus bars the city, rejects reports of the Bacchae's miracles, and plans an armed campaign against them. Dionysus leads him toward Kithaeron, where Pentheus, increasingly overcome and dressed as a Bacchanal, spies on the Maenads from a pine tree. When his mind clears, he recognizes Agave, but she, Ino, Autonoe, and the Maenads, driven mad, tear him apart in the Bacchic hunt. He dies on Kithaeron, and his dismembered body is brought back to the palace.

Pentheus is a mortal king of Thebes, son of Agave and Echion and grandson of Cadmus. Ruling in Cadmus's stead, he rejects Dionysus's worship as impious and dangerous, refuses Dionysus offerings and prayers, and begins war against him. Hearing of women’s secret rites, Pentheus orders guards to seize them and the stranger teaching the worship, mocks Teiresias and Cadmus, and seeks to destroy the sanctuary. Dionysus gradually controls Pentheus’s thoughts; though he resists, Pentheus is transformed and dressed as a Bacchanal in a robe, snood, fawn-skin, and with a wand. He goes to spy on the Bacchae, climbs a pine tree, and is exposed as their enemy. Agave, Ino, Autonoë, and the other Maenads tear him apart despite his plea that Agave recognize him. He dies as a consequence of this spying.

Pentheus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Pentheus is the reigning king and ruler of Thebes, son of Agave and Echion, grandson of Cadmus through Agave, and descended through Echion from the earth-born race. What most defines him is his impious, hostile opposition to Bacchus, the Bacchae, and Bacchic worship, which he judges unlawful, disorderly, and corrupting. He hunts and imprisons the Bacchae, orders the stranger claiming to be Bacchus arrested and confined, threatens him, and seeks to suppress the rites by force. After the stranger escapes amid damage to the palace, Pentheus rejects a messenger’s report and plans an armed assault. Bacchus instead persuades him to disguise himself as a woman, with turban and thyrsus, and to go secretly to Cithaeron to spy on the Bacchae. Driven mad, he participates in revelry and suffers delusions, seeing two suns, twin Thebes, and horns on Bacchus. At Cithaeron he climbs a pine to watch the Mænads. After Bacchus commands the attack, Pentheus falls, begs Agave to spare him, and is killed and dismembered by Agave and the Mænads. In Oppian’s account, he is transformed into a bull.

Pentheus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Pentheus is the son of Echion and Agave, grandson of Cadmus, and king of Thebes after Cadmus. He is described as a contemner of the gods who refuses to honor Bacchus, ridicules Tiresias, forbids Bacchic rites, and orders Bacchus captured. Seeking to suppress or punish the Bacchantes and their mysteries, he goes to Mount Cithæron, in one account climbing a tree to observe secret rites. Discovered there, he is attacked and torn apart by Agave and the other Bacchantes.

Pentheus, son of Echion, ridicules Tiresias and refuses to honor Bacchus. He orders Bacchus to be captured, rejects Acœtes’s account of the god’s power, and goes to Mount Cithæron to disrupt the rites. There, his mother Agave and the other Bacchantes attack and tear him apart.

notes .. notes

Pentheus was the son of Echion and Agave, grandson of Cadmus, and king of Thebes after succeeding his grandfather. He opposed abuses in the mysteries of Bacchus and went to Cithæron to chastise the Bacchantes, who tore him to pieces in their frenzy. Another account says that he was a wicked prince who climbed a tree to observe the secret rites and was killed after the Bacchantes discovered him.

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Pentheus, king of Thebes, rejects and forbids Bacchus’s worship, orders followers seized, and dismisses Acetes. Going to witness the rites, he is mistaken for a wild boar and torn apart by Agave and his aunts.

Chapter XIV

Pentheus is the king of Thebes and rejects the worship of Bacchus. He orders Bacchus’s followers seized, dismisses Acetes’s account, and goes to witness the rites himself. Blinded by the god’s influence, his mother Agave and his aunts mistake him for a wild boar and tear him to pieces.
