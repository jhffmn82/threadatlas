Abstract written by a model

In Alcestis, Pheres is Admetus's father and a free-born Thessalian ruler who refuses to die for him, brings gifts to Alcestis's burial, clashes with Admetus over that refusal, and departs accusing him of letting a woman die for him. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Pheres is Admetus's aged, free Thessalian father, who refuses to die for him, praises Alcestis's sacrifice, defends himself against Admetus, and ends by cursing and disowning him.

Pheres in Alcestis

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Pheres first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pheres is Admetus’s father, a free-born Thessalian of chiefly ancestry who owns Thessaly by inheritance and rule. He refuses to die to save Admetus. At Alcestis’s burial he brings gifts and praise, clashes with Admetus over that refusal, defends each person’s single life, and departs accusing Admetus of letting a woman die for him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

[_As the song ceases there enters a stranger, walking strongly, but

Pheres comes to Alcestis's burial with gifts and praises her for saving Admetus. Admetus attacks him for refusing to die for his son, but Pheres rejects the accusation, arguing that each person has only one life and that no law requires fathers to die for their sons. He leaves after accusing Admetus of allowing a woman to take his place.

Pheres in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Pheres first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pheres is Admetus's aged, free Thessalian father, who raised him and owns a large demesne. Though not legally bound to die for Admetus, he refuses to do so. At Alcestis's funeral he brings adornments and praises her sacrifice, but Admetus rebukes him. Pheres defends himself, condemns Admetus's unwillingness to die, and ends by cursing and disowning him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHOR. And I indeed with thee, as a friend with a friend, will bear this

Pheres comes to Alcestis's funeral with adornments for her body and praises her for saving Admetus and preserving him from childlessness. Admetus rebukes Pheres for refusing to die for his son and rejects his attempt to honor Alcestis.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Pheres argues that he was not bound by paternal or Grecian law to die for Admetus. He says that Admetus, unlike Alcestis, was unwilling to die, and he curses and disowns his son after their quarrel.
