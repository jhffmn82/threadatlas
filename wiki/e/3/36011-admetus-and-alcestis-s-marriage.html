Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Admetus and Alcestis's marriage begins in celebration and ends in grief after Alcestis chooses to die for Admetus. In The Library, the marriage of Admetus and Alcestis involves Admetus's failure to sacrifice to Artemis, leading to snakes in the marriage chamber and Apollo's advice to appease the goddess.

Admetus and Alcestis's marriage in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Admetus and Alcestis's marriage first appears

Abstract written by a model

Admetus and Alcestis’s marriage began with Pelian torches, bridal songs, revelry, and happiness. Alcestis says she rejected another Thessalian marriage and chose to die for Admetus, warning that his remarriage could harm their children; it ends in grief, black clothing, and an empty couch.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

ALCESTIS.

Alcestis says that she could have married another Thessalian and lived in royal comfort, but she chose to die for Admetus instead. She warns Admetus that a second marriage could bring a hostile stepmother to their children.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

Admetus recalls entering the palace with Alcestis amid Pelian torches, bridal songs, and revelry. Their marriage, once associated with happiness and noble birth, is now represented by groans, black clothing, and an empty couch.
