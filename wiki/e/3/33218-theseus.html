Abstract written by a model

In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Theseus is the righteous king of Athens who welcomes and protects Oedipus, rescues his daughters, grants Polynices safe-conduct, and keeps Oedipus’s resting-place secret. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Theseus is the king of Athens who believes Phaedra’s accusation against Hippolytus, invokes Neptune against him, and later repents and mourns after learning the truth. In Hecuba and other plays, Theseus is the king of Athens and Hercules’ kinsman and friend who arrives to comfort Hercules, offers him refuge and aid, and leads him from Thebes. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Theseus is the king of Athens who accepts Phaedra’s accusation, curses and exiles Hippolytus, then repents when Artemis reveals Hippolytus’s innocence. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Theseus kills foes and the Minotaur, abandons Ariadne, defeats the Amazons, unites Athens, is imprisoned in the underworld, dies in exile, and is later honored with a sanctuary. In Heroides and Amores, Theseus is the son of Neptune and Aethra, husband of Phaedra, and father of Hippolytus who kills the Minotaur, abandons Ariadne, abducts Helen, and is reproached by Ariadne and Phaedra. In The Library, Theseus is described as the son of Aegeus and Aethra, a hero who becomes king of Athens, kills the Minotaur and numerous other foes, fights Amazons and centaurs, descends into Hades, and is ultimately killed by Lycomedes. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Theseus arrives in Athens, is saved from Medea by Aegeus’s recognition, and is celebrated for killing the Marathonian bull, the Cromyon boar, robbers, and Pallas with his sons.

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Theseus kills the Minotaur with Ariadne’s assistance, abandons Ariadne, participates in the Calydonian boar hunt and the battle with the Centaurs, and invokes Neptune against Hippolytus. In The Library, Theseus is targeted by Medea, is bound near Hades after joining Pirithous in wooing Persephone, is rescued by Hercules, and leads Athens against Thebes before returning the Argive dead for burial. In Description of Greece, Theseus is presented as an Athenian hero who kills or defeats dangerous figures, fights the Minotaur, battles Amazons, unites Athens, establishes political equality, and is later killed on Scyrus before his bones are taken to Athens. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Theseus is associated with the Panathenaea, institutes games at Delos, and is seated with Pirithous on an enchanted rock while holding both swords. In The Fall of Troy, Theseus is Aethra’s son and the father of Demophoon and Acamas, linked to the fall of Aphidnae and his sons’ recollection of Aethra’s enslavement. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Theseus is a hero and king who defeats robbers and the Minotaur, unites Attica, fights the Amazons, befriends Pirithous, and is killed by Lycomedes at Scyros. In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Theseus is the king of Athens who protects Oedipus, rescues his daughters from Creon, grants sanctuary, and guards the secret of Oedipus’s burial place.

Abstract written by a model

Theseus is the son of Aegeus, king and ruler of Athens, portrayed as righteous in word and deed. When Oedipus arrives as an aged suppliant and exile, Theseus recognizes him, pities him, welcomes him, gives his word to protect him from forcible removal, and accepts his promised boon for Athens. After Creon seizes Oedipus’s two children, Theseus orders pursuit, condemns the seizure of suppliants, and vows to restore them. He returns with Antigone and Ismene unharmed and also grants Polynices safe-conduct. At Oedipus’s summons, he swears to protect the daughters, becomes the sole rightful witness of Oedipus’s disappearance, keeps the resting-place secret, and promises to escort the daughters back to Thebes.

Abstract written by a model

Theseus is a king of Athens, Troezen, and his household; son of Othra and Neptune, and also identified as son of Ægeus. He is husband of Hippolyta and later Phaedra, father of Hippolytus, Phaedra’s children, and Demophoon. After murdering Pallas’s sons, he lives with Phaedra in Troezen during a year of banishment. He possesses three prayers granted by Neptune that cannot fail. Returning from an oracle, he finds Phaedra dead and reads her false accusation that Hippolytus defiled her. Diana reveals that Phaedra deceived Theseus and that he acted without proof. Theseus repents, seeks to die in Hippolytus’s place, and mourns him; Hippolytus forgives him before dying, and Theseus receives pardon because he acted in ignorance under Venus’s will. Elsewhere, he is linked to Iolaus’s expedition for the belt, rescues Demophoon’s father from hell, and is connected to Hercules’s children through Demophoon.

Abstract written by a model

Theseus is a son of Aegeus and Aethra, born in Troezen. Raised with Aegeus’s sword and sandals as tokens of his parentage, he comes of age by moving a stone to recover them and travels on foot to Athens. On that journey he clears the road and the Isthmus of Corinth of evildoers, killing Periphetes, Sinis, Sciron, Phaea, and others; after killing Periphetes, he takes and carries his iron club. Abstract written by a model

Theseus is a son of Ægeus, a grandson of Neptune through Ægeus, and a renowned Neptunian hero. He begins by going to Crete with the Athenian victims, finding and killing the Minotaur with Ariadne’s assistance, escaping the Labyrinth, and carrying Ariadne away before abandoning her on Dia, or Naxos. Celebrated for ending Athens’s mournful tribute, he becomes famous throughout Argive cities and Achaia. He joins Pirithoüs in the Calydonian boar hunt, cautions him after Ancæus’s death, then, delayed by the flooded Acheloüs, rests in Acheloüs’s grotto and hears divine transformation stories. At Hippodame’s wedding, he protects her from attackers, receives Pallas’s warning, and defeats several Centaurs. Later, hoping to marry Phædra, he invokes Neptune against Hippolytus after Phædra’s accusation; Hippolytus dies amid a sea-monster story or a chariot accident.

Theseus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Theseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Theseus is a hero of Athenian tradition, also associated with other places and competing accounts. He was identified as the son of Aegeus through shoes and a sword found beneath Theseus’ rock. His career includes being acquitted at the Delphinium for justifiable homicide, purification at Milichian Zeus after killing the progeny of Phytalus, and the killing or defeat of dangerous figures including Sinis, Polypemon, Cercyon, Sciron, and Timalcus. The account credits him with defeating Cercyon by skill, making wrestling a science, and establishing training schools. His most notable exploit is presented as his return from Crete after killing Asterion amid the hidden dangers of the labyrinth; he is also said to have fought the Minotaur. After returning, he built Artemis the Saviour. He sailed from Phalerum to avenge Androgeos, fought Amazons, and may have carried off Antiope or received her help in taking Themiscyra. He recovered Minos’s ring from the sea and received a golden crown from Amphitrite. In Athens he was portrayed as restoring political equality and was believed by popular tradition to support Demos. When he combined the Athenians into one city, he established worship of Persuasion. Although one tradition called him king, this is rejected as untrue. Later he was imprisoned by the king of Thesprotia at Cichyrus, was killed on Scyrus, and his bones were subsequently conveyed to Athens, where he had a shrine. Temples and paintings preserve his battles and associations.

Theseus was said to have built the temple of Artemis the Saviour after returning from Crete, where he killed Asterion, the son of Minos. The account presents this as his most notable exploit because he escaped the hidden dangers of the labyrinth.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Theseus is associated with a rock where he found the shoes and sword of Ægeus, with a temple of Bridal Aphrodite that he built when he married Helen, and with a place near the harbour called his Natal-place because he was said to have been born there. A temple of Ares stood where Theseus defeated Amazons, and an olive tree was said to have overturned Hippolytus’s chariot. The people of Trœzen also said that Theseus’s name caused the former altar of Sthenian Zeus to be called Theseus’ rock.

Theseus in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912 · the work’s page · read it where Theseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Theseus is Aegeus’s son, a prince, and the sovereign king and ruler of Athens, also called its bold captain and chieftain. Though described as exiled like Oedipus in his upbringing, he rules Athens and acts as Oedipus’s friend, ally, and protector. Hearing Oedipus’s name and later responding to his summons, he recognizes Oedipus, sympathizes with his suffering, grants him citizenship, and promises that he will not be removed by force. When Creon’s actions violate Athens’s laws and customs and Oedipus’s daughters are captured, Theseus orders their pursuit, declares his resolve to restore them, and returns Antigone and Ismene alive and unharmed. He protects Oedipus under his authority, permits Polyneices to speak from sanctuary, and assures Oedipus of safety while he lives. During the thunderstorm, Theseus alone is chosen to receive and guard the secret of Oedipus’s burial place for Athens. Afterward, he remains a constant friend to Antigone and Ismene, pledges their continued protection and care, and refuses their request to see the tomb.
