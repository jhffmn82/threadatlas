thyrsus in Hippolytus; The Bacchae

Euripides · -0427

The thyrsus is a sacred wand of Dionysian worship, belonging to Dionysus and later taken by Pentheus. Dionysus gives Pentheus one and teaches him to carry and swing it like the Bacchae. In the Messenger’s account, wild women use such wands to draw water, produce wine, and defeat armed men.

DIONYSUS

Dionysus gives Pentheus a wand and instructs him to swing it in his right hand in time with his right foot. Pentheus asks how to carry it so that he will resemble the Bacchae.

the thyrsus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

The thyrsus is an ivy-clad wand or weapon, decorated with ivy and carried by Bacchus, Tiresias, Cadmus, and the Bacchants in worship and dance. Sacred to the Chorus and Bacchæ but rejected by Pentheus, it can produce wine, water, and honey; Pentheus is told to carry one in disguise.

THE BACCHÆ.

The thyrsus is the ivy-clad staff carried by Bacchus, Tiresias, Cadmus, and the Bacchants in worship and dance. Pentheus orders it rejected, while the Chorus and Bacchæ treat it as a sacred emblem.

The thyrsus produces miraculous effects when used by the Bacchae: one releases water from a rock, while another yields honey. Pentheus is instructed to carry a thyrsus as part of his woman's disguise.
