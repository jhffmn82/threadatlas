plagiarism in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

Plagiarism is conscious theft of ideas when deliberate reproduction is presented as original. It ranges from obvious copying or false claims to quieter appropriation of philosophical or scientific ideas. Though morally alike, it can evade shame or enforcement and is difficult to prove, since coincidence, forgotten sources, and assumptions can cause false accusations.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

XI.

Plagiarism is distinguished between obvious frauds, such as copying a poem or falsely claiming an invention, and the quieter appropriation of philosophical or scientific ideas. The latter may have the same moral quality even when it escapes public shame or legal enforcement, but accusations require caution because coincidence, forgotten sources, and mistaken assumptions about what someone must have read can produce false conclusions. The unit maintains that borrowing another person’s idea, forgetting its source, and treating it as one’s own reveals weakness in mental grasp and rectitude.
