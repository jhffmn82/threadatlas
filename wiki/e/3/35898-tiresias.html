Abstract written by a model

In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Tiresias is a blind prophet who warns Creon about Thebes’s pollution and condemns Oedipus as Laius’s murderer, foretelling his curse, blindness, exile, and revelation of his identity. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Tiresias is a blind, aged prophet who delivers the oracle requiring Menoeceus’s death, foretells Oedipus’s exile, aids Athens, and defends Bacchus’s worship before Pentheus. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Tiresias advises the defeated Thebans to abandon Thebes, later dies at Tilphossus, and is buried with great funeral pomp and worshiped as a god. In The Library, Tiresias is a blind Theban soothsayer and father of Manto who advises the Thebans to sacrifice a human being and abandon their city, and whom Ulysses consults after death for guidance about propitiating Poseidon. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Tiresias is a prophet and renowned soothsayer who, after living as both man and woman, receives prophetic power from Jupiter, gives unerring answers, and predicts Narcissus’s fate and Pentheus’s death. In The Library, Tiresias is a blind Theban soothsayer who changes sex after striking copulating snakes, receives prophecy from Zeus, advises the Thebans, and dies after drinking at Tilphussa spring. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Tiresias is a prophet whom Odysseus encounters in Hades and who later dies after drinking at Tilphusa and is buried there, though the Thebans display a cenotaph for him.

Tiresias in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · 449 to 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Tiresias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tiresias is a blind prophet and seer, holy, inspired by sacred Truth, and ruled by Apollo in life and prophetic office. Though outwardly blind and guided by his guide, he possesses divine inward knowledge. He tells Creon that pollution in Thebes and failed sacrifices foretell punishment for entombing Antigone and leaving Polynices unburied. Brought before Oedipus, he identifies and accuses him as Laius’s murderer, foretelling divine curse, blindness, exile, poverty, and revelation of his hidden family identity. Oedipus rejects the prophecy and alleges Creon’s manipulation, but Creon denies it.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

CHORUS

Tiresias reports that birds have fought over blood and that sacrifices fail, showing pollution in Thebes. He warns Creon that he will soon pay with a corpse from his own family for entombing Antigone alive and leaving Polynices unburied.

Tiresias is brought before Oedipus because he is believed to perceive what Apollo perceives. He identifies Oedipus as Laius's murderer and foretells that Oedipus will become blind, exiled, beggared, and revealed as both brother and father to his children.

CHORUS.

Tiresias is described as having declared that Oedipus was responsible for Laius's murder. Oedipus accuses Creon of having suborned Tiresias, while Creon maintains that Tiresias's prophecy was not fabricated by him.

Tiresias in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Tiresias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tiresias is a blind, aged and frail prophet and soothsayer, father of a daughter, whose painful truths shape Thebes and others. He delivers an oracle for Thebes’s preservation, telling Creon that Menoeceus must die to satisfy Mars’s ancient wrath, and warns that diviners are hated for such revelations. He foretells Oedipus’s exile, aids Athens against Eumolpus, and supports Bacchus with Cadmus. In Bacchic dress, he defends the god’s worship and warns Pentheus to honor him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

ETEOCLES, CHORUS, MESSENGER.

Tiresias comes from the Athenians with his daughter and tells Creon that Thebes can be saved only by the death of Menoeceus. He explains that Menoeceus must shed his blood in the cave where the earth-born dragon lay, thereby satisfying the ancient wrath of Mars against Cadmus. Tiresias then leaves after warning that diviners are hated when they reveal painful truths.

THE BACCHÆ.

Tiresias comes dressed for Bacchic worship, wearing a deerskin, carrying a thyrsus, and crowned with ivy. He defends Bacchus, explains the benefits of wine and Bacchic excitement, urges Pentheus to honor the god, and warns that Pentheus is speaking and acting foolishly.

Tiresias in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Tiresias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tiresias is a prophet and renowned soothsayer, son of Evenus and Chariclo, who has lived as both man and woman. After striking coupling serpents, he becomes a woman for seven autumns; striking them again in the eighth year restores his former sex and shape. Having known both male and female pleasure, he judges Jupiter and Juno’s dispute over sexual pleasure in Jupiter’s favor. Juno blinds him, and Jupiter compensates him with prophecy and prophetic knowledge. He gives unerring answers to those who consult him, is praised throughout Achaia, and predicts Narcissus’s fate and Pentheus’s violent death if Pentheus will not honor Bacchus, though Pentheus mocks his blindness. He lives to great age and is blind in old age. Other accounts attribute his blindness to Minerva after he sees her bathing, connect it with writings or teachings about sexual differences, and say he dies after drinking from the fountain of Telphusa when overheated.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

front matter

Tiresias, who has been of both sexes, decides a dispute between Jupiter and Juno about the relative pleasures of the sexes. Juno deprives him of sight, while Jupiter gives him the gift of prophecy.

opening

Tiresias becomes a woman after striking two coupling serpents with his staff and remains female for seven autumns. After striking the same serpents again in the eighth year, he regains his former sex and shape. Because he has experienced both forms of pleasure, he is chosen to judge the dispute between Jupiter and Juno.

notes .. notes

Tiresias predicted Narcissus’s untoward fate and was the son of Evenus and Chariclo. He was regarded as the most renowned soothsayer of his time, lived to a great age, and became blind toward the end of his life; different accounts attribute his death or blindness to drinking from a fountain, seeing Minerva bathing, or his teachings about the sexes of animals and stars.

opening

Tiresias is praised throughout the cities of Achaia for his prophetic powers, but Pentheus mocks him for his blindness. He predicts that Pentheus will be torn apart and that his blood will pollute the woods, his mother, and his mother’s sisters if Pentheus refuses to honor Bacchus.

Tiresias in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Tiresias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tiresias is a blind Theban soothsayer, son of Everes and the nymph Chariclo, with prophetic power, long life, a cornel-wood staff, and understanding of every bird note. He became a woman after wounding copulating snakes on Cyllene and a man again after seeing them again. Hera blinded him, while Zeus gave him soothsaying. He told the Thebans they would win if Menoeceus sacrificed himself to Ares, later advised them to send a herald to the Argives and flee, and died after drinking at Tilphussa spring during that flight.

6 ᾿Αργεῖοε δὲ καθοπλισθέντες προσήεσαν τοῖς

Tiresias was a Theban seer, son of Everes and the nymph Chariclo, descended from Udaeus the Spartoi. He was blind but understood the voices of birds, possessed prophetic power, and was granted long life.

Οὗτος οὖν Θηβαίοις μαντενομένοις 3 εἶπε νική-

Tiresias tells the Thebans that they will be victorious if Menoeceus offers himself as a sacrifice to Ares. Zeus also gives Tiresias the art of soothsaying, while Hera blinds him.

VII. Κρέων δὲ τὴν Θηβαίων βασιλείαν παραλα-

Tiresias advised the Thebans to send a herald to the Argives and flee themselves. He died after drinking from the spring called Tilphussa while the Thebans were fleeing.

Tiresias in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Tiresias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tiresias is a prophet, encountered by Odysseus in Hades for counsel about returning home. He beheads a serpent with his sword. Taken by Argives and Polynices’ sons toward Apollo at Delphi, he drinks at Tilphusa, dies, and is buried there; Thebans nevertheless display a cenotaph, admitting he died in Haliartia.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Thebans show a tomb of Tiresias about 15 stades from the tombs of the children of Œdipus. They admit that Tiresias died in Haliartia, so they regard the tomb as a cenotaph.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Tiresias was being taken with the spoil to Apollo at Delphi by the Argives and the sons of Polynices when he became thirsty and drank from the fountain Tilphusa. He died there and was buried on the spot.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Tiresias is the soul whom Odysseus visits in Hades to consult about his return home.
