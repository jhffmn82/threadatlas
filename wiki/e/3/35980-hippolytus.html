Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Hippolytus is Theseus’s chaste, pious son who rejects Venus, is falsely accused by Phædra, cursed by Theseus, fatally wrecked by a sea-born bull, and dies forgiving his father. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Hippolytus is Theseus’s chaste prince who serves Artemis, rejects Aphrodite, is falsely accused by Phaedra, fatally wrecked by Poseidon’s Sea-Bull, and dies forgiving Theseus. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Hippolytus is Theseus’s Amazon-born son who rejects Phaedra, dies after being dragged by his horses, and is later adored by the Troezenians as a god for his chastity. In The Aeneid, Hippolytus is a hunter killed by wild horses through another’s crime, restored by Diana, struck down by Jove, and concealed by Trivia as Virbius. In Heroides and Amores, Hippolytus is an Amazon-born, vigorous and stern hunter devoted to Diana, renowned for riding and weapons and resistant to Phaedra’s love. In The Library, Hippolytus is Theseus’s Amazon-born son who rejects Phaedra’s advances, is falsely accused, and dies when Poseidon’s bull frightens his horses. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Hippolytus is Theseus’s wise and chaste son whose rejection of Phædra leads to his death, restoration as Virbius, and possible translation as Auriga. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Virbius is Hippolytus renamed after restoration to life and made Diana’s devoted lower God, living concealed in her grove.

Hippolytus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Hippolytus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Hippolytus is the son of Theseus and an Amazon, a pupil of Pittheus, inhabitant of Trœzene, hunter and servant of Diana, and follower of Artemis. Beautiful, notably chaste, and pious, he prizes an undefiled body, a virgin mind, hunting with swift dogs, and worship of the Gods. He honors Diana as greatest and rejects Venus, marriage, sexual desire, and women, whom he calls evil. Returning from a hunt, he offers Diana a wreath from a pure meadow. Phædra loves him, but he rejects the Nurse’s disclosure and, bound by oath, keeps silent. After Phædra’s death, he tells Theseus that he neither touched nor desired his wife and swears innocence by Zeus. Theseus believes Phædra’s accusation, banishes him from Athens and his native land, and curses him. The curse is fulfilled when a sea-born bull causes a chariot disaster: while driving his own horses, Hippolytus is dragged and mangled. Brought before Theseus in extreme pain, he forgives his father at Diana’s request, frees him from guilt, asks him to cover his face, and dies.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS.

Hippolytus returns from the hunt praising Diana and carries her a wreath gathered from a pure meadow. He says that he is chaste and refuses to honor Venus, whom he regards from a distance, while declaring that he will have nothing to do with marriage.

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

Hippolytus is identified as the son of Theseus and the man whom Phædra loves. Phædra begs the Nurse not to speak further about him.

CHORUS, PHÆDRA.

Hippolytus hears the Nurse's revelation, rejects her as a friend, and condemns women as a great evil. He keeps silent because of his oath, leaves the house while Theseus is away, and later appears before Theseus after Phædra's death.

HIPPOLYTUS, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Hippolytus denies having touched or desired Theseus's wife and swears by Zeus that he is innocent. He is banished by Theseus from Athens and the surrounding land, then suffers a fatal chariot accident after Theseus's curse is fulfilled.

DIANA, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Hippolytus is brought in severely mangled after his horses destroyed him, suffering intense pain from the injuries. He forgives Theseus at Diana's request, releases him from guilt for the killing, and dies while asking his father to cover his face.

notes

The notes clarify details of Hippolytus’s chariot accident: he does not appear to change his dress, leans back while pulling the reins, and is compared to a sailor. The commentary also discusses proposed readings concerning his horse, clothing, and the sea-born bull that causes the disaster.

Abstract written by a model

Hippolytus is a prince, son of Theseus and Hippolyte, reared strictly by Pittheus. Devoted above all to Artemis, whom he serves and who loves him, he values chastity, purity, hunting, and a life apart from sexual desire. He rejects Aphrodite, hates Love and women as sources of corruption, and returns from hunting to praise Artemis with a garland from an untouched meadow. After overhearing the Nurse's disclosure, he reacts with horror but keeps his oath of silence. When Phaedra dies and accuses him through her tablet, he protests his innocence yet will not break that oath. Theseus exiles and curses him through Poseidon. His horses panic at a supernatural Sea-Bull, wrecking his chariot and fatally injuring him. Before dying, he learns the truth, forgives Theseus, and is later honored in Trozên.
