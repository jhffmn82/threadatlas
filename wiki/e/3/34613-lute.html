Abstract written by a model

The lute is a stringed musical instrument, called el-oud in Arabic. It was brought to Mekka by Nadr Ben el-Hares Ben Kelde after he learned Persian singing and lute performance at Khosroo Purviz’s court. Before the tenth century, the Arabian lute had four paired strings; around the tenth century a fifth tone was added, with silk strings and frets regulated by seventeen one-third-tones. It later used catgut strings and movable catgut frets, six-line notation, and sizes from treble-lute to bass-lute. It developed to twelve pairs of strings in the seventeenth-century English lute, was prized for mellow tone and decoration, then fell into oblivion.

THE PERSIANS AND ARABS.

The lute was brought to Mekka through the Arab musician Nadr Ben el-Hares Ben Kelde, who learned Persian singing and lute performance at the court of Khosroo Purviz. Before the tenth century the Arabian lute had four strings or pairs of strings, and a fifth tone was added around the tenth century; its silk strings and frets were carefully constructed according to the seventeen-interval system.

CHAPTER X.

The lute was once nearly as popular as the pianoforte. It developed from four pairs of catgut strings to instruments with many more strings, used frets made of tightly fastened catgut, and varied in size from treble-lutes to bass-lutes. Lutes were valued for their rich, mellow tone and elegant decoration, although they later fell into oblivion.
