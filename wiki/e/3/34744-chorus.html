Chorus of Theban Elders in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

The Chorus of Theban Elders is a group of Theban citizens witnessing the conflict between Antigone and Creon. It begins by celebrating victory and mourning Polynices and Eteocles, acknowledges Creon’s authority while allowing for divine action, then reflects on human limits and inherited suffering. As Antigone’s fate darkens, it questions Creon, counsels reason, mourns her, and urgently urges him to heed Tiresias, free Antigone, and bury Polynices.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

ANTIGONE

The Chorus celebrates Thebes’ victory over the Argive attack and laments the mutual deaths of Polynices and Eteocles. It acknowledges Creon’s authority but suggests that a divine power may have caused Polynices’ body to receive burial.

CHORUS.

The Chorus reflects on man's power and limitations, the inherited suffering of the house of Cadmus, and the uncertainty caused by Antigone's arrest. It recognizes the force of Antigone's character, questions Creon's decision, and advises both Creon and Haemon to heed reason.

CHORUS

The Chorus mourns Antigone and compares her fate with the punishments of Danaë, Lycurgus, and the sons of Phineus. It interprets Tiresias' words as terrifying and advises Creon to release Antigone and bury Polynices without delay.

Chorus in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Chorus is the Theban civic body of elders: a collective commentator, counselor, and public voice that supports Oedipus as Thebes’ protector and later serves as Creon’s trusted council. In Oedipus’s search for Laius’s killer, it appeals to Apollo and other gods to end the plague, identifies Teiresias as the needed prophet, and urges Oedipus and Teiresias to stop quarreling. Perplexed by the seer’s accusation, it knows no blood-feud involving the Labdacidan house and Polybus’ son and refuses to accept Oedipus’s guilt without proof. It also tries to restrain conflict between Oedipus and Creon, praises divine law, and fears that Apollo and the oracle are being ignored. As the truth emerges, it encourages the search for Laius’s Herdsman, recognizes him, laments Oedipus’s fall, Jocasta’s death, and Oedipus’s blinding, and concludes that no one is happy until life ends. Under Creon, it acknowledges his authority while questioning its role in enforcing his order. It witnesses Antigone’s conflict with Creon, sings of inherited suffering, divine power, fate, and punishment, and urges Creon and Haemon to listen to each other. Trusting Teiresias’s unfailing warnings, it finally presses Creon to free Antigone and bury Polyneices, affirming wisdom and warning that those who defraud the gods face danger.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

OEDIPUS THE KING

The Theban elders form the Chorus and support Oedipus’s oath to investigate Laius’s murder. They appeal to Apollo and the other gods to save Thebes from plague and identify Teiresias as the prophet most likely to guide the search.

CHORUS.

The Chorus says that both Teiresias and Oedipus have spoken angrily and urges them to stop quarreling and determine how best to fulfill the oracle.

CHORUS.

The Chorus describes the doer of the murder as fleeing under pursuit by Apollo and the Fates. It is perplexed by the master seer’s words, knows of no feud involving the Labdacidan house and the ruler, Polybus’ son, and cannot accept the charge laid against Oedipus without proof.

CREON.

The Chorus attempts to calm the quarrel between Oedipus and Creon and asks Oedipus not to condemn Creon on suspicion. It later praises divine law, condemns insolence and impiety, and laments that Apollo and the oracle are no longer heeded.

[Enter JOCASTA.]

The Chorus observes Jocasta’s distress and encourages the search for the Herdsman. After Oedipus learns the truth, it sings of his fall from power and happiness, declaring that his birth and marriage have brought together son and father in one bed.

[Enter SECOND MESSENGER.]

The Chorus reacts with grief and horror to Jocasta’s death and Oedipus’s blinding. It questions Oedipus, condemns the dreadful condition of his life, and concludes that no one should be considered happy until life has ended.

ANTIGONE

The Theban elders form the Chorus and are summoned by Creon as trusted counselors. They acknowledge Creon’s authority, question their responsibility for enforcing his order, and sing of the Argive invasion and the limits imposed by law.

[Enter GUARD bringing ANTIGONE]

The Chorus observes the conflict between Creon, Antigone, and Ismene and reports that Antigone’s death-warrant appears sealed. It sings of the inherited suffering of the Labdacidae and the power of Zeus, Fate, and divine punishment. It then announces Haemon’s angry arrival.

[Enter HAEMON]

The Chorus initially finds Creon’s words reasonable but later urges both Creon and Haemon to heed one another. It reflects on love, fate, Antigone’s suffering, and the dangers of defying authority, recalling other figures overcome by divine or destined power.

[Enter TEIRESIAS and BOY]

The Chorus confirms that Teiresias’s warnings have never failed and urges Creon to free Antigone and bury Polyneices immediately. It later declares that wisdom is the chief part of happiness and that mortals who defraud the gods face danger and punishment.
