Aias's madness in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · 449 to 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Aias's madness first appears

Abstract written by a model

Aias's madness, attributed to Athena and Pallas, diverts his intended attack on the Greek generals, Odysseus, and the host onto livestock. Deluded, he mistakes bulls, rams, sheep, and other animals for his enemies, raids the army's animals, and slaughters the flocks. When the madness ends, he recognizes the disaster, remains self-alienated and overwhelmed by shame and grief, and the condition is said to bring his ruin and death.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

AIAS

Aias' madness makes him mistake bulls, rams, sheep, and other animals for the Greek generals and Odysseus. When the madness passes, he understands the disaster and is consumed by shame and grief.

Of Aias, and his fortunes as they are. [AIAS _is discovered_

Aias's madness is attributed to Athena, who turned his purpose aside and made him slaughter harmless animals instead of his enemies. Afterward he remains self-alienated, tormented by shame, and apparently unable to recover his former identity.

_The scene changes to a lonely wooded spot._

Aias's madness is described as a condition that caused his ruin and death. Tecmessa says that the gods, especially Pallas, brought about the mischief that made Odysseus glad, while Menelaus claims that Aias's madness led him to attack the host.
