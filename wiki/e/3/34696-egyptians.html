Egyptians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Egyptians are a rebellious people against the Persians, repeatedly organizing Egypt’s defense. They garrison Pelusium, fortify the Nile mouths, strengthen Memphis, and prepare for invasion; after resisting and exploiting Persian failures, they eventually force the Persians from Egypt. Under Nectanebus, they fight Persia and aid Sidon with Greek mercenaries, but defeats, disputes with auxiliaries, urban seditions, and an attempted surrender at Bubastis weaken them. Led by Nectanabis after Tachos flees, they besiege and pursue Tachos and Agesilaus, yet lose despite superior numbers. Seeking an end to troubles, they expel foreigners; displaced people depart or settle in Judea. Later, they condemn Ptolemy’s cruelty and excesses.

Egyptians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Egyptians are a people described through accounts of their origins, customs, religion, laws, and conflicts. They report that Egypt produced the first humans and that heated mud around Thebes generated living creatures, citing mice from mud and slime. They present Egyptian gods, heroes, and institutions as ancient sources later appropriated or adapted by Greeks, identifying Isis with Io, Osiris with Dionysius, and Perseus as Egyptian-born; some of these foreign-origin claims are noted as insufficiently supported. Their earliest life is said to have involved roots, herbs, agrostis, and reed houses, followed by fish, cattle, cultivated fruits, and lotus bread. Their strongest stated value is the virtuous life remembered after death: houses are called inns, tombs everlasting habitations, and sepulchres receive greater care than homes. They preserve royal successions in temples and praise the dead after favorable judgment for education, piety, justice, chastity, and other virtues. Under Sesostris they were arranged into thirty-six provinces, supplied an army, and benefited from conquests and public works, while captives performed temple labor. People report their names and livelihoods to provincial governors, marry under permissive rules, raise many children, and learn occupations. Their religious life centers on gods, kings, and sacred animals. They devote extensive resources and care to revered creatures, punish their killing by death, and mourn them; explanations include protection, destruction of harmful creatures, divination, generative power, and association with Typhon. Customs vary by region, including food prohibitions. They are also said to share many laws and practices with Ethiopians and to have learned royal deification, funerary practices, statues, priestly customs, and letters from them. In the final events, they revolt from Persian control after news of Xerxes's death, expel Persian treasurers, make Inarus king, join Athenians against Persia, and later submit when a Persian army reaches the White Wall and traps the Athenian fleet.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK L

The Egyptians are said to use the generation of mice around Thebes as testimony that the earth can produce living creatures. Their claim is presented as part of the discussion of the origin of things.

We shall first speak of the barbarians; not that we judge them

The Egyptians report that Egypt produced the first humans and that living creatures can arise from the heated mud left by the Nile. They also identify natural powers with gods and preserve customs honoring Isis, Osiris, and other benefactors.

From Osiris and Isis, to the reign of Alexander the Great, who

The Egyptians report that their gods and heroes were ancient Egyptians whose identities and customs were later claimed or adapted by the Greeks. They say that Egypt sent colonies throughout the world and that Egyptian customs influenced circumcision, social organization, religious rites, and political traditions. Their reports about foreign origins are presented as claims that the text considers insufficiently supported in many cases.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK I.

The Egyptians are said to have first lived on roots, herbs, and colewort leaves, especially agrostis, and later to have eaten fish, cattle, cultivated fruits, and bread made from lotus. They made early dwellings from reeds and worshipped the gods with agrostis in memory of its usefulness.

The Egyptians are described as caring little for the brief duration of earthly life and valuing the reputation of virtuous conduct after death. They called houses for the living inns and tombs everlasting habitations, and therefore devoted more care to sepulchres than to houses.

The Egyptians were organized under Sesostris into thirty-six provinces, each governed by an appointed official. They supplied the army, benefited from the conquest and public works, and were generally spared labor on Sesostris's temple projects, which were performed by captives.

Egyptians are required to report their names and means of livelihood to provincial governors. They generally marry according to permissive rules, raise many children, and train in occupations that provide a livelihood.

The Egyptians devote land, food, money, baths, unguents, beds, and breeding care to animals they worship. They punish the killing of sacred animals, mourn them intensely, and give various explanations for their religious practices.

CHAP, VII.

Egyptians worship different creatures in different regions and avoid certain foods, including lentils, beans, cheese, and onions, according to local custom. They are described as especially grateful for favors and as honoring their kings almost as gods because they believe divine providence raised them to the throne.

DIODORUS SICULUS- / BOOK III.

The Egyptians are said by the Ethiopians to be a colony taken from Ethiopia by Osiris. They are also said to have inherited Ethiopian laws, royal deification, burial customs, statues, priestly practices, and forms of writing.

CHAP. XIX.

The Egyptians drove Persian treasurers out of Egypt and made Inarus their king. They joined the Athenians against Persia but later submitted when the Persian army reached the White Wall and trapped the Athenian fleet.

Egyptians in Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa

Augustus Le Plongeon · 1881

The Egyptians are presented as a group distinguished by beliefs and practices centered on death, religion, and ancestry. They believed the soul was immortal, faced judgment by Osiris, and would eventually return to its former body; accordingly, they embalmed bodies and placed organs and the deceased’s professional implements in tombs. Their tombs were decorated with paintings and sculptures. They practiced circumcision, divination, astronomy, several writing systems, and systems of fives and tens, and used the sistrum in religious ceremonies. They considered five supplementary days unlucky and showed reverence by crossing an arm over the chest to the opposite shoulder. They are said to have shared many customs and attainments with the Mayas, pointed westward as their ancestors’ birthplace, and worshiped personages from MAYAB as gods and goddesses.
