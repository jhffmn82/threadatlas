Jews in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Jews are a nation organized into twelve tribes, following laws and customs attributed to Moses and governed by a chief priest who interpreted God's commands; they had no kings. They resisted Antiochus's siege until provisions failed, then negotiated, gave hostages, received pardon, and suffered demolished walls and tribute.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK XXXIV.

The Jews resisted Antiochus's siege until their provisions were exhausted, then sent ambassadors to negotiate. Antiochus received hostages and pardoned them, though their walls were demolished and their tribute collected.

BOOK XXXVIL

The Jews are described as a people organized into twelve tribes under the laws attributed to Moses. The chief priest governed them as the messenger and interpreter of God's commands, and the people were expected to follow his declarations.

Jews in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

The Jews are presented as a distinct historical people, dispersed internationally yet bound by unusually strong national consciousness, memories, traditions, and religious obligation. Their ideas are said to have helped determine the religion of half the world, and they are credited with an eminent struggle against Rome, practical ability, and scientific and artistic aptitude. Across exile, persecution, and oppression, they retain identity, pride, tenacity, domestic tenderness, care for women and little ones, and a distinctive physical and mental type. The records also describe isolation and certain vices as consequences of prolonged oppression, while noting accusations of moral alienation and cosmopolitan indifference. Widely present among other peoples from east to west, they are portrayed as acclimatisable, energetic, wealthy, and influential in commerce, political life, and governments; such prominence is linked to jealousy, hostility, and persecution. The concluding discussion criticizes Europe for applying national principles inconsistently to them, rejects prejudice founded on dislike or racial generalization, stresses their religious and civilizational contributions, and urges that their energies be used beneficently while preserving national consciousness and perhaps renewing a place among the nations.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

XVII.

The Jews are discussed as a people whose national memories and identity have been judged by inconsistent standards. The text argues that Europe’s treatment of them neglects the same national principles that are praised in other peoples.

The Jews are described as a people whose ideas helped determine the religion of half the world and who made an eminent struggle against the power of Rome. Their intense sense of separateness, national value, and inherited religious obligation helped them survive persecution while retaining pride, kindliness, practical ability, and scientific and artistic aptitude.

The Jews are portrayed as widely dispersed, economically influential, and frequently accused of gaining disproportionate power in commerce and politics. The text rejects prejudice based on personal dislike or racial generalization, emphasizes their contributions to religious language and civilization, and argues that they should direct their energies into beneficent channels. It calls on them to preserve their inherited national consciousness and possibly renew their place among the nations.
