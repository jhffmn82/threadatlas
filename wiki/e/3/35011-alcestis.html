Abstract written by a model

In Alcestis, Alcestis is Admetus’s devoted wife and queen who sacrifices her life for him, asks him to care for their children and never remarry, and is later restored by Heracles. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Alcestis is Admetus’s noble wife who dies in his place, bids farewell to her household, children, and husband, and is later brought back by Heracles. In The Library, Alcestis is Pelias’s daughter and Admetus’s wife who voluntarily dies in his place and is restored by Persephone or Hercules. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Alcestis is Admetus’s devoted wife who voluntarily dies for him and is rescued from Death by Hercules.

Alcestis in Alcestis

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Alcestis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Alcestis is the beautiful, devoted, simple, homely, practical wife and queen of Admetus, daughter of Pelias, sister of Acastus, and mother of her and Admetus’s two children. An orphan raised in Admetus’s house, she is deeply loved by him and valued by the household, including servants she protected from his anger. Though not artistic, she has unconscious poetry; she is idealized without being merely blameless. What matters most is her courage and fidelity: alone among those able to do so, she gives her life in Admetus’s place to save him, without fearing death. Before dying, she makes him promise to care for their children and never remarry, fearing a stepmother might mistreat them. Her death has occurred as the main action begins. Minstrels, the chorus, and others praise her as a faithful, generous, noble wife, a queen of women, and a spouse of spouses; she is received by the underworld in another’s stead. Heracles then wins her back from Death. Veiled and resembling her former self, she returns to Admetus’s house but remains speechless for three days, until cleansing and purification are complete.

Alcestis is beautiful, devoted, simple, homely, and practical rather than artistically eloquent. Before dying, she makes Admetus promise to care for their children and never remarry, chiefly because she fears a stepmother might mistreat them; after returning from death, she remains silent as part of her passage back into the world.

Alcestis in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Alcestis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Alcestis is Admetus’s wife, Pelias’s daughter, and the mother of Admetus’s children. Judged the best of women and praised as noble and excellent, she voluntarily gives her life in exchange for Admetus’s, though she could have refused and married another man. Near death, she prepares for death, visits the household altars, bids farewell to her bridal bed, children, and Admetus, and asks him to care for their children and not marry another wife who might dishonor her. She is also remembered as a mother to the household domestics, saving a servant from many harms. After Death cuts her hair and dedicates her to Hades, she dies and is described as a blest divinity. Heracles brings back a woman recognized by her likeness to Alcestis, but she cannot speak until the appointed time and a contrary rite have passed.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

ALCESTIS.

Alcestis gives her life so that Admetus may continue living, although she could have refused and married another man. Before dying, she prepares herself, visits the household altars, bids farewell to her bridal bed, and asks Admetus to care for their children and not take another wife.

CHOR. And I indeed with thee, as a friend with a friend, will bear this

Alcestis dies for Admetus and entrusts their children to him, asking him not to marry another wife who might dishonor her. Before dying, she says farewell to her children and Admetus; the Chorus praises her as an exceptional and devoted wife.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Alcestis is praised as a noble and excellent woman who died for Admetus. The servant says that she was like a mother to the household and had saved the domestics from many troubles.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

Alcestis is described as Admetus's faithful and excellent wife, who died for him and left her love behind. Her likeness is recognized in the woman whom Heracles brings back, though she remains unable to speak until the appointed time has passed.

notes

Alcestis is described as near death and as undergoing the preparations associated with a funeral. After Death cuts her hair and dedicates her to the dead, a contrary rite is understood to be necessary before she can again speak with and accompany Admetus.

Alcestis in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Alcestis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Alcestis, daughter of Pelias and wife of Admetus, is defined by voluntarily dying in Admetus’s place when his parents would not. Admetus obtained her after yoking a lion and boar to a car. After her death, she is restored, either by Persephone or by Hercules after fighting Hades.

Πελίον θυγατέρα Αλκηστιν. ἐκείνου 1 δὲ δώσειν

Alcestis, daughter of Pelias, was obtained by Admetus after he yoked a lion and a boar to a car. When Admetus was destined to die, neither his father nor his mother would die for him, but Alcestis voluntarily died in his stead. She was later sent back according to one account, while another says that Hercules brought her back after fighting Hades.
