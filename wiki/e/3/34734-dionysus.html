  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Bacchus is the terrestrial god whom Alexander honors at a victorious feast and sacred day, and whom the Nysaeans revere as conqueror of India and founder of Nysa.
  • In The Bacchae of Euripides, Dionysus is the son of Zeus and Semele who comes to Thebes to establish his divinity, defend his mother, and punish Pentheus and those who deny him.
  • In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Dionysus is the god who spreads his mysteries, comes to Thebes in disguise, escapes imprisonment, and directs the Maenads to punish Pentheus and his family.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Bacchus is the god of wine and revelry whose wine gives pleasure and intoxication, who is seized by pirates, and whom Silenus, Satyrs, Ulysses, and the Chorus invoke or follow.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Bacchus is the god of wine and ecstatic rites who vindicates Semele, compels Thebes to recognize him, and brings about Pentheus’s death and Cadmus’s house’s punishment.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Bacchus is the child of Semele invoked to heal afflicted Thebes and honored there by Bacchae and Thyiads in revelry.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar: Bromios.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Dionysus is the immortal son of Zeus and Semele who is fostered by Nymphs, gives joyful wine, punishes pirates, and makes Ariadne immortal.
  • In The Library, Dionysus is the son of Zeus and Semele who is reared at Nysa, discovers wine, spreads his rites, punishes Lycurgus and pirates, takes Ariadne, and raises Semele to heaven.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Bacchus is the twice-born god of wine who is born from Semele and Jupiter, establishes his worship, civilizes peoples, conquers India, and punishes those who reject him.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Bacchus aids Ariadne, immortalizes her crown, punishes the Thracian women and Glaucus, and grants and removes Midas’s golden touch.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Osiris is identified as the ancient Bacchus who is born in Egypt, becomes Serapis after death, and whose worship passes into Greece through Bacchic mysteries.
  • In The Library, Dionysus is the god who discovers the vine, receives purification and initiation from Rhea, spreads his rites through many lands, drives opponents mad, defeats pirates, and brings Semele to heaven.
  • In Description of Greece, Dionysus is a god honored by temples, images, festivals, and processions, whose traditions include being reared by Ino, rescuing Semele from Hades, taking Ariadne, and fighting Perseus.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Dionysus is a widely worshipped god whose statues, festivals, oracles, punishments, liberation of captives, aid against Triton, and legendary invasion of India are recorded.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II: Dionysus Ægobolus.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Osiris is the Egyptian god of the underworld and judge of the dead who civilizes Egypt, teaches agriculture and laws, and becomes its tutelary deity after Typhon kills him.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Bacchus is the merciful god of wine who discovers viticulture, civilizes peoples, travels to Asia and India, punishes pirates, helps Midas, and marries Ariadne.

Osiris is an Egyptian god, also identified with the sun and called Dionysius, Bacchus, Serapis, Pluto, Ammon, Jupiter, and Pan; some Greek mythologists surname him Sirius and describe his name as signifying a thing with many eyes. He is Isis’s husband and brother, father of Orus, and with Isis governs the world. Presented as a benefactor and civilizer, he rules Egypt, discovers agriculture and wine near Nysa, teaches tillage, vine cultivation, useful foods and drink, prohibits cannibalism, builds Thebes, founds cities, and appoints rulers. After vowing not to cut his hair before returning to Egypt, he leads an expedition through Ethiopia, Arabia, India, Asia, Europe, and the inhabited world. His inscriptions call him Saturn’s eldest son and a conqueror. Typhon kills him and mangles his body; Isis handles his members in a wooden ox. Bacchus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Bacchus is a god and civilizing benefactor closely associated with wine, also identified with Dionysius and Osiris. The records preserve several, sometimes conflicting, genealogies and origins: he is variously a son of Ammon and Amalthaea, of Jupiter and Ceres, of Jupiter and Io, of Jupiter and Proserpina, or of Jupiter and Semele; he is linked with Nysa, Thebes, Crete, Arabia, and Naxus. Some accounts distinguish one or three Bacchuses, while the Libyans describe three incarnations. In the account of the Bacchus raised at Nysa, he discovers or teaches wine while a boy, cultivation, and agriculture, fights Saturn and the Titans, benefits mankind, and is worshipped as a god. Other accounts credit him with discovering the vine, wine-making, planting and pruning vines, and the use of wine; his name is also used for wine, and he bears the epithet Iacchus for this discovery. He is credited with teaching ploughing with oxen and agricultural arts. In India, where he is said to have conquered the country, he introduces agriculture, fruit trees, wine, cities, laws, and divine worship, reigns fifty-two years, and is later honored as a god; his sons and descendants rule after him. More broadly, he is a conqueror who overruns the world with armies. His expeditions include armed women, and he is attended by Bacchantes, the Muses, satyrs, and Silenus, his master, foster father, and associate in war. He institutes Orgia, Trieteria, Bacchanalia, festivals, plays, theatres, and music-schools, promoting rites, ceremonies, peace, and concord. He punishes opponents and impious people, including Pentheus, Myrrhanus, Lycurgus, and Butes, whom he drives mad for dishonoring Coronides. Naxian tradition says he was brought forth, bred, and educated there, aided the Naxians, enriched their island, and led Ariadne away after appearing to Theseus in a vision. Orpheus is said to have learned his sacred mysteries in Egypt and transferred his birth and rites into Greek tradition.

Bacchus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Bacchus is a god and deity, also called Liber, whose central account begins with Semele: he is her son, survives her destruction while she is pregnant, and undergoes a premature birth from her womb into Jupiter’s thigh, with Jupiter undertaking a mother’s duties during gestation. Ino secretly nurses and fosters him, and the Nyseian Nymphs conceal and nourish him in caves at Nysa, away from Juno’s fury. The cave’s two entrances give him the name Dithyrites, and Nysa contributes to the Greek name Dionysus. He later goes down to hell, brings Semele out, and carries her to heaven. The records also preserve several differing genealogies and identities: he is said to be Semele and Jupiter’s son in the Theban account, while Cicero’s account distinguishes five Bacchuses, respectively sons of Proserpina and Jupiter, the Nile, Caprius, Moon and Jupiter, and Nisus and Thione. One of these founds Nysa; another tradition reasonably identifies Bacchus with Osiris. Bacchus arrives in Greece in triumph as a new god. Associated with vine cultivation, useful arts, thyrsi, tambourines, ivy, wine, and ecstatic worship, he teaches and civilizes, spreads his rites, and is worshipped through Bacchic rites, the Dionysia, and a triennial nocturnal festival. Festive cries, Bacchanals, Satyrs, and drunken Silenus follow him; he rides lynxes yoked to his chariot. He is eternally youthful, conquers the East as far as India and the Ganges, is worshipped in India, and is honored with temples in Achaia. His power is also punitive. When sailors deceive him, he arrests their ship, produces vines and wild creatures, changes the crew into dolphin-like creatures, and spares Acœtes. He destroys or punishes Pentheus and Lycurgus, and punishes the daughters of Minyas for contempt of his worship and desecration of his festival, transforming them into bats and their webs into vines. His names include Bromius, Lyæus, Thyoneus, Lenæus, Nyctelius, Eleleus, Iacchus, and Evan, each linked to worship, wine, Semele, frenzy, night, or cries. His power is celebrated in Thebes, where Ino invokes him while fleeing with Melicerta. Other recorded acts include seeking rejuvenation for his nurses and hiding a stolen bullock as a fictitious stag.
