Sir Charles Baskerville in The Hound of the Baskervilles

Arthur Conan Doyle · 1901/1902

Sir Charles Baskerville was a late baronet, wealthy public man, and generous, childless owner of Baskerville Hall and the Baskerville estate. After making his fortune in South African speculation, he restored the Hall and lived there in retirement for the two years before his death. Known for a kind heart, he helped Mrs. Laura Lyons financially, appointed Mr. Stapleton as his almoner on several occasions, and left substantial gifts to Barrymore and his wife, Dr. James Mortimer, charities, and others. His estate, worth close to a million pounds, passed in residue to his next of kin and heir, Sir Henry Baskerville. Sir Charles had long-standing organic heart disease, a weak heart, breathlessness, and nervous depression. Deeply impressed by the curse of the Baskervilles and fearful of the family hound legend, he nevertheless enjoyed walking over the moor. On the day of his death, he received and partly burned a letter from Mrs. Laura Lyons asking him to meet her at the garden or moor-gate at ten o'clock. The appointment had been manipulated. He waited near the gate, then ran in terror down the yew alley toward the moor, where he was found dead near the end of the alley, about fifty yards from the summer-house. Medical evidence gave cardiac exhaustion as the cause, while gigantic hound footprints were reported near his body. Stapleton, whom Sir Charles had befriended and told of the family hound, exploited his weak heart and superstition. He arranged for the hound to pursue and terrify Sir Charles, killing him through heart disease and terror. Sir Charles's death shocked the devoted Barrymores, left the Hall painful to them, and made his will and fortune important to the investigation.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2.

Sir Charles Baskerville was a wealthy, generous, childless man who restored Baskerville Hall after making his fortune in South African speculation. He feared the Baskerville legend, suffered from heart disease and nervous distress, and was found dead at the end of the yew alley after appearing to flee toward the moor; Dr. James Mortimer later reported gigantic hound footprints near his body.

Chapter 3.

Sir Charles Baskerville was found dead about fifty yards from the summer-house at the end of the yew alley. He had apparently waited five or ten minutes near the moor-gate, then run down the alley in terror until he fell dead on his face.

Chapter 5.

Sir Charles Baskerville left five hundred pounds each to Barrymore and his wife, a thousand pounds to Dr. Mortimer, other smaller sums and charitable bequests, and the residue to Sir Henry Baskerville. His estate was worth close to a million, and his will becomes an important subject in assessing possible motives.

Chapter 6.

Sir Charles Baskerville formerly lived a very retired life at Baskerville Hall. Barrymore and his wife were strongly attached to him, and his death shocked them and made the house painful for them. The new building at the estate was begun with money from Sir Charles’s South African gold.

Chapter 7.

Jack Stapleton says that he was fond of Sir Charles Baskerville and knew from Dr. Mortimer that Sir Charles had a weak heart. Stapleton believes Sir Charles may have seen a dog in the yew alley and died of fright.

Chapter 10.

Sir Charles’s death is connected to a meeting he arranged at the gate with a woman whose initials were L. L. Barrymore learned this from a partially burned letter found in Sir Charles’s study after the inquest.

Chapter 11.

Sir Charles Baskerville had helped Mrs. Laura Lyons financially and was asked by her to meet her at the garden gate. He burned her letter, although part of it remained legible. He died at the place and hour at which Mrs. Lyons had arranged to meet him.

Chapter 13.

Sir Charles Baskerville was asked by Mrs. Laura Lyons to meet her at the gate at ten o'clock, the place and time of his death. Holmes treats her letter and the manipulated appointment as important evidence in the investigation of his murder.

Chapter 15.

Sir Charles befriended Stapleton and told him about the family hound. Stapleton exploited Sir Charles’s weak heart and superstition, arranging for the hound to chase him so that he died from heart disease and terror.
