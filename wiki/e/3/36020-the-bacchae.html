Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Chorus is an assembly of Asian women who worship Bacchus through hymns, dances, drums, and cries, and witnesses his power over Pentheus. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Bacchæ are holy women and followers of Bacchus who celebrate his rites and perform marvels and violent deeds in the mountains. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Mænads are female followers of Bacchus who gather on Cithaeron and, at his command, dismember Pentheus. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Bacchants are followers of Bacchus who celebrate him through dancing, shouting, sacred music, mountain rites, purification, and sacrifice. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Bacchae are foreign female followers of Bacchus who escape imprisonment, celebrate his victory and Pentheus’s death, and mourn Agave’s deed and Cadmus’s suffering. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, the Chorus of Inspired Damsels are Eastern Bacchic maidens who worship Dionysus in ecstatic mountain rites and invoke divine law and his wrath. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, the Theban women are Dionysus’s Bacchic followers, performing secret rites in the hills and displaying supernatural force. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, the Chorus are devotees of Dionysus who lament his imprisonment, call on him against Pentheus, rejoice at his revealed power, and urge Pentheus to honor him.

the Bacchæ in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Bacchæ first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Bacchæ are holy women and followers of Bacchus, initiated into his mysteries and organized in three companies led by Agave, Autonoe, and Ino. They celebrate Bacchic rites, dances, and revelry in the mountains. They sleep, awaken to perform marvels with water, milk, wine, honey, fire, and serpents, then use divine power to tear apart cattle, ravage settlements, defeat armed men, and wash away blood at their springs.

Mænads in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Mænads first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Mænads are female followers of Bacchus, driven into ecstatic Bacchic activity and marked by deerskins, the thyrsus, dancing, drums, cymbals, ivy, animal skins, and cries. They gather on Cithaeron, including women of Cadmus’s house, under Bacchus as their general. At his command they attack Pentheus, tear up a pine, and dismember him; in Oppian’s account, they are transformed into panthers.

the Bacchae in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Bacchae first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Bacchae are female followers of Bacchus, also called Mænads, foreign Bacchic women who favor Bacchus over Thebes. Imprisoned by Pentheus, they escape through unmanned openings, dance and invoke Bromius. Their chorus celebrates Bacchus’s victory and Pentheus’s death, then mourns Agave’s deed and Cadmus’s suffering, concluding divine events are unexpected.

The Bacchae whom Pentheus imprisoned escape when their fetters come loose and the prison doors open without mortal assistance. They dance in the meadows, invoke Bromius as their god, and are described as companions and worshippers of Bacchus.

Chorus of Inspired Damsels in Hippolytus; The Bacchae

Euripides · 427 BC · the work’s page · read it where Chorus of Inspired Damsels first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Chorus of Inspired Damsels is a chorus of Eastern Bacchic maidens who serve, worship, and follow Dionysus in ecstatic mountain rites. They come from Asia, awaken sacred music, and gather Thebes in song around Pentheus’s hall. Their songs praise Dionysus’s birth, powers, and worshippers; condemn Pentheus’s scorn; and seek justice while fearing for the city. Imprisoned by Pentheus, they escape without mortal aid, return freely to the woods, dance for Bromios, and invoke divine law, Justice, and Dionysus’s wrath.

the Theban women in Hippolytus; The Bacchae

Euripides · 427 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Theban women first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Theban women are Dionysus’s Bacchic followers, driven from their homes to the hills and mountain, where they carry thyrsi, dance, pray, and perform secret rites. Though Pentheus alleges sexual wrongdoing, Teiresias says pure-hearted women need not be stained. Sleeping in bands on Kithaeron, they wake to worship, draw water, wine, milk, and honey from the earth, then display supernatural force: slaughtering cattle, defeating villagers, taking children, breaking metal, ravaging settlements, and remaining protected from harm.

Chorus in The Bacchae of Euripides

Euripides · 406 to 405 BC · the work’s page · read it where Chorus first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Chorus is a group of Eastern women, devoted Bacchants who follow and worship Dionysus through song, dance, instruments, and sacred dress. It begins by honoring his birth, rites, and mountain worship, urging Thebes to join and condemning Pentheus’s scorn. Imprisoned before Dionysus’s manifestation, it laments, prays for his return, and adores him amid the shaking Castle and fire. It celebrates Pentheus’s death and its release, but recoils at Agave’s display, shares Cadmus’s grief, and ends reflecting on unexpected divine purposes.
