Theban Elders in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

The Theban Elders are a civic chorus of Cadmeans speaking for the Theban people. In a city devastated by plague, deaths, and failed childbirths, they pray to Apollo, Pallas, Artemis, and other gods for relief. They seek the truth about the oracle and Laius’s murder, deny guilt, and urge Oedipus to consult Tiresias and heed the heavenly oracle. Though initially uncertain about Tiresias’s prophecy, they support Oedipus for having saved Thebes from the female monster, help end his quarrel with Creon, and uphold divine laws and prophecies. As the shepherd approaches, they speculate about Oedipus’s origins; after his ruin and blinding, they mourn and declare that no mortal is happy before life ends without sorrow.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

KING OEDIPUS

The Theban Elders enter as the Chorus and pray to Apollo, Pallas, Artemis, and other gods for relief from the plague. They describe the city's deaths, failed childbirths, and general suffering.

[OEDIPUS _has entered during the Choral song_

The Chorus denies that it killed Laius and says that Apollo should name the guilty person. It recommends Tiresias as the one person who can reveal what Apollo sees and urges attention to the heavenly oracle.

CHORUS.

The Chorus is initially uncertain whether Tiresias's prophecy is true but continues to trust Oedipus because he once saved Thebes from the female monster. It urges Oedipus to listen to Creon, helps end their quarrel, and later prays that divine laws and prophecies retain their authority.

_Enter_ JOCASTA.

The Chorus identifies the approaching Theban Shepherd as the man previously sought and remembers him as Laius's faithful shepherd. Before the shepherd's testimony, it sings of Cithaeron as the place that may have nursed Oedipus and speculates about his divine or nymph-born origins.

CHORUS.

The Chorus mourns Oedipus's ruin and says that no one can be called happy while alive. It reacts with horror to his blinding and concludes that happiness can be judged only after life has ended without sorrow.
