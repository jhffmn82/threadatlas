Phocians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Phocians were a Greek people in the Phocian war. Condemned by the Amphictyons for cultivating oracle-claimed land at Cirrha, they made Philomelus their absolute general; he asserted their ancestral claim to the Oracle at Delphos and enlisted a thousand Peltastates. They supported Philomelus and Onomarchus in fighting over Apollo's temple and sacred treasures, seizing and robbing the oracle and using sacred wealth from Delphi to fund war. Allied with Athenians and aided by mercenaries and outside support, they won some engagements but repeatedly suffered defeats against the Boeotians and Philip of Macedon. After Onomarchus was killed, Phayllus rebuilt their forces, but later losses continued under Phalaecus. When Phalaecus departed, they surrendered to Philip and received severe Amphictyonic penalties, losing temple access and Council membership.

CHAP. VI.

The Phocians were condemned by the Amphictyons to pay many talents because they had cultivated land at Cirrha claimed by the oracle. They appointed Philomelus as their absolute general and supplied many of his soldiers, while other Phocians were recruited into his army.

CHAP. VII.

The Phocians supported Philomelus and Onomarchus in the war over Apollo's temple and the sacred treasures. Their forces won several engagements but suffered major defeats against the Boeotians and Philip of Macedon. After Onomarchus was killed, Phayllus became their general and rebuilt their forces.

CHAP. Vlll.

The Phocians were near their final extremity after suffering a defeat and heavy losses, but Phayllus rebuilt their forces with mercenaries and outside support. They were defeated in several battles against the Boeotians, and after Phayllus's death they suffered further losses under Phalaecus.

CHAP. X.

The Phocians fought the Boeotians with the help of mercenaries and used sacred wealth from Delphi to finance the war. After Phalaecus withdrew with his forces, the Phocians surrendered to Philip and were subjected to severe Amphictyonic penalties, including loss of access to the temple and membership in the Amphictyonic Council.

Phocians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Phocians are a Greek people of Phocis. They first warred against the Dorians, took their cities, then lost them to Lacedaemonians under Nicomedes. They later fought the Boeotians, sought Lacedaemonian aid, joined Lysander at Haliartus, and lost near Aricas under Lacesthenes.

CHAP. vm.

The Phocians made war upon the Boeotians and requested Lacedaemonian assistance. They joined Lysander in besieging Haliartus.

CHAP. IX,

The Phocians fought the Boeotians near Aricas under the command of Lacesthenes, a Laconian. They lost the battle and returned to their own country.

Phocians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Phocians are a Greek people, inhabitants of Phocis, whose record centers on war, Delphi, punishment for sacrilege, and restoration through later fighting. They participated in the war against Ilium and were constant enemies of the Thessalians. Before the Persian invasion, they defeated the Thessalians through stratagems including buried earthenware pots against cavalry and a night attack; after losing Gelon and 300 picked men, they prepared to destroy their own women, children, and possessions rather than yield, but won under their commanders and Tellias of Elis. From the spoils they offered or dedicated works to Apollo, Athene, Artemis, and a group of Hercules and Apollo fighting for a tripod at Delphi. When the Persians entered Europe, they were reportedly compelled to join Xerxes, but deserted the Medes and fought for the Greeks at Platæa. Afterward, an Amphictyonic fine was imposed on them. They then seized the temple at Delphi, employed mercenaries, and fought the Thebans for ten years. Defeat near Neon ended this course: their commanders died or were deposed, they lost participation in the temple and the general Greek Council, and their Amphictyonic votes went to the Macedonians. They returned from villages to Phocian towns, except those always weak, and their towns were later rebuilt with Athenian and Theban help. They subsequently fought at Chæronea and against Antipater and the Macedonians at Lamia and Crannon. Their decisive renewal came when Brennus and the Galati attacked Delphi. They bravely defended Thermopylæ but were overpowered; then men from every town defended Delphi, used their knowledge of Parnassus to attack the Celtic rear, guarded cattle, and endured heavy losses. Fighting the Galati and Celtic army more bravely than any other Greeks, they avenged the god at Delphi and, in the author’s view, atoned for the impiety of the other towns. They were restored to the Amphictyonic Council and their former position.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK X.--PHOCIS. / CHAPTER I.

The Phocians fought the Thessalians with stratagems, including buried pots and a night attack by men covered in plaster. After suffering the loss of Gelon and 300 picked men, they prepared to sacrifice their women, children, and possessions if defeated, but they won a famous victory under their commanders and Tellias of Elis.

CHAPTER II.

The Phocians were reportedly compelled to join Xerxes when the Persians entered Europe, but they deserted the Medes and fought for the Greeks at Platæa. Later, after a fine was imposed on them by the Amphictyonic Council, they seized the temple at Delphi, raised a large mercenary army, and fought the Thebans for ten years. They were eventually defeated near Neon, and their later commanders suffered death or deposition.

CHAPTER III.

The Phocians were deprived of participation in the temple at Delphi and in the general Greek Council, and their votes in the Amphictyonic Council were given to the Macedonians. Their towns were later rebuilt with help from the Athenians and Thebans, and the Phocians fought at Chæronea, against Antipater and the Macedonians at Lamia and Crannon, and against the Galati and the Celtic army at Delphi.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Phocians seized the temple and were excluded from the Amphictyonic Council after the Sacred War ended. When Brennus led the Galati against Delphi, the Phocians fought more bravely than any other Greeks and were restored to the Council and to their former position in other respects.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Phocians dedicated statues of Apollo, Athene, and Artemis at Delphi from spoil taken from the Thessalians. They also dedicated Hercules and other brazen figures connected with their fighting against the Thessalians in the Sacred War, and their leaders later abstracted the golden part of the tripod dedicated after the battle of Platæa.

CHAPTER XXII.

The Phocians guarded the pass over Mount Œta. A heavy mist prevented them from seeing Brennus's Galati until the attackers were close, and although the Phocians fought bravely, they were overpowered and withdrew to the main Greek force.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Phocians came from all their towns to defend Delphi and used their knowledge of the terrain to attack the Celtic rear from the slopes of Parnassus. They also guarded their flocks and herds, reported the barbarians’ nighttime panic, and lost nearly 6,000 in battle, with more than 10,000 additional deaths in the winter night and panic.
