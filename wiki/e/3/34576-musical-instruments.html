Narrative, section by section

15 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

The chapter concerns the universal appreciation of music and the development, construction, classification, and cultural significance of musical instruments. It describes instruments used by nations and tribes throughout the world, emphasizing distinctive materials, ornamentation, forms, and methods of producing sound. It explains how studying foreign instruments and popular songs can illuminate musical progress, national character, history, ethnology, and possible improvements to Western instruments. The South Kensington collection is presented as a valuable resource containing instruments from different periods and regions.

CHAPTER II.

The unit surveys prehistoric musical relics and the musical instruments of the ancient Egyptians. In Dordogne, France, perforated animal bones may have served as whistles, while a stag-horn pipe from near Poitiers had three finger-holes. The ancient Egyptians possessed harps, stringed instruments, pipes, flutes, drums, trumpets, tambourines, gongs, bells, cymbals, castanets, and the sistrum. Egyptian instruments are known through tomb paintings, sculptures, hieroglyphs, and surviving specimens, especially those associated with Thebes and the British museum.

CHAPTER III.

The unit describes Assyrian instruments from bas-reliefs found near Mosul and explains their construction, use, and performance in religious and secular settings. It identifies harps, dulcimers, double-pipes, lyres, trumpets, drums, cymbals, bells, and other instruments, as well as processions of Assyrian musicians and singers. It then surveys Hebrew instruments known mainly from biblical names, comparing them with Egyptian and Assyrian examples and noting uncertainty about many identifications. The unit discusses Hebrew harps and lyres, pipes, bagpipes, trumpets, drums, cymbals, bells, and the traditional attribution of instrumental invention to Jubal.

CHAPTER IV.

The unit describes the musical instruments of the Greeks, their uncertain construction, and their likely derivation from Asia more than from Egypt. It discusses Greek lyres, harps, kitharas, other stringed instruments, wind instruments, percussion instruments, and their uses. It then describes the Etruscans and Romans, including their instruments, the hydraulic organ, Roman adaptations from several cultures, and instruments used in war, worship, and performance. It concludes by noting the later spread of eastern instruments into Europe through the Greeks, Romans, Phoenicians, Arabs, and crusaders.

CHAPTER V.

The unit describes the ancient musical traditions and instruments of the Chinese, including sonorous stones, bells, percussion instruments, wind instruments, and stringed instruments. It recounts traditions attributing musical invention and the scale to heavenly spirits, Fohi, Niuva, and the miraculous bird foung-hoang. Confucius is said to have been so moved by Chinese music that he could not eat for three months. The unit also explains the construction, tuning, and ceremonial or practical uses of instruments such as the king, tchung, ou, drums, flutes, hiuen, cheng, kin, and chê.

THE HINDUS.

The unit describes Hindu musical mythology, instruments, and theories about the heavenly or watery origins of music. Nareda is said to have invented the vina, while Saraswati is associated with music, speech, and the musical scale, and Krishna with the flute. It examines ancient Hindu instruments including the vina, harp, ravanastron, and poongi, noting that many remained in use and that others were introduced from Arabic and Persian sources. It also discusses the Hindu claim that the violin bow originated in Hindustan and compares Hindu traditions with musical traditions associated with water in other cultures.

THE PERSIANS AND ARABS.

The unit describes Persian and Arabian musical instruments, their development, and the transmission of instruments and musical practices from Persia through the Arabs and Spain into Europe. It discusses the Persian harp, lute, dulcimer, violin-type instruments, flutes, and oboes, as well as the Arabian system dividing the octave into seventeen one-third-tones. It also explains how the Arabs adopted Persian musical instruments after conquering Persia and how the Saracens and Moors introduced several of them into Europe. The unit concludes with the traditional story of Al-Farabi's court performance, in which changes of musical mode produced laughter, sadness, anger, and sleep.

CHAPTER VI.

The unit examines musical instruments made and used by American Indians, especially the Aztecs and Peruvians, before European influence. It describes pottery and bone pipes, flutes, whistles, and the Peruvian huayra-puhura, noting that some instruments reveal aspects of the musical systems of their makers, including use of the pentatonic scale. It also discusses trumpets and related instruments, including the juruparis of Indians near the Rio Haupés and the botuto used near the Orinoco. Religious, funerary, martial, and ceremonial purposes are attributed to several of these instruments.

The unit describes the Mexican teponaztli, a wooden drum with two vibrating tongues that produced two regulated pitches. It discusses its construction, carvings, gum-covered drumsticks, varied sizes, loudness, and surviving examples among relatively isolated Mexican Indians. It also mentions related percussion instruments in other parts of America, including skin-covered drums, the Peruvian huanca, chhilchiles, and the Mexican ajacaxtli rattle. The rattle was made from a gourd or pottery, with small pebbles enclosed inside and a wooden handle sometimes attached.

The Tezcucans, also called Acolhuans, belonged to the same race as the Aztecs but were described as surpassing them in knowledge and social refinement. Their monarch Nezahualcoyotl opposed human sacrifice and built a temple dedicated to “The unknown god, the cause of causes,” whose nine-story tower held musical instruments for summoning worshippers to prayer. Ixtlilxochitl reported that the temple contained a sonorous metal struck with a mallet, though it is unclear whether it was a gong or a bell. The unit also discusses Peruvian bells, Mexican horse bells called yotl, and sonorous stones used as musical instruments in South America.

The unit describes the musical instruments, songs, ceremonies, and musical institutions of the American Indians, especially the Mexicans, Tezcucans, and Peruvians. It discusses the uncertain evidence for ancient stringed instruments, including the Peruvian tinya, and compares American instruments and scales with those of Asia and other regions. It also records the use of music in warfare, agriculture, religious observances, mourning, public celebrations, and Christian missions. The unit concludes that present-day Indian musical practices preserve some ancient instruments and traditions but have also been affected by oppression and foreign influence.

CHAPTER VII.

The unit examines musical instruments used by European nations during the Middle Ages, relying on representations in manuscripts, sculptures, and paintings. It discusses the lyre, cithara, psalterium, harp, rotta, Welsh crwth, and various fiddles, while noting uncertainty about the dates and accuracy of some depictions. The text describes the development and changing names of these instruments, their relationships to Asiatic forms, and their use by Anglo-Saxons, German and Celtic bards, Scandinavian skalds, and other performers. King David is repeatedly shown playing instruments such as the harp, rotta, and lyre in medieval manuscripts.

[Illustration] [Illustration] CHAPTER VIII.

The unit explains the construction and historical use of several medieval instruments, including the organistrum, monochord, wind instruments, organs, and percussion instruments. It describes the monochord as chiefly a theoretical instrument and notes the diatonic musical scale used about a thousand years ago, with both b-flat and b-natural. The organ developed from early pneumatic and hydraulic forms with very few pipes and required many performers to operate. It also distinguishes the portable regal organ from another instrument called the regal, consisting of sonorous wooden slabs.

[Illustration] CHAPTER IX.

The unit examines medieval orchestras and the instruments represented in sculptures, noting that artists may have used imagination but probably depicted instruments familiar to their contemporaries. It describes a twelve-performer group formerly at St. Georges de Boscherville, including viols, harps, an organistrum, a syrinx, stringed instruments, and bells, as well as a tumbler. It discusses several types of viol and their tuning, including the development of side strings later used in instruments such as the theorbo and archlute. It also examines the orchestras on the Portico della gloria at Santiago da Compostella and in the minstrels’ gallery of Exeter cathedral.

CHAPTER X.

The chapter surveys post-mediæval instruments, including the virginal, lute, theorbo, viols, recorder, bagpipe, bells, and carillons, describing their construction, use, tone, and historical development. It explains that older instruments often had distinctive and attractive tone qualities and ornamentation, even though modern instruments have been more highly perfected. The chapter discusses bell-ringing and carillon traditions in England and various European countries, including their mechanisms and musical possibilities. It concludes that the history of music helps explain the characteristics of musical instruments and the effects composers create with them.
