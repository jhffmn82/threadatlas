the prize-encounter for Pelides' arms in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

The contest for Achilles' arms is the event that opens Aias's great pain. Aias says the Atreidae used deceit to award the armor to Odysseus rather than to him, despite his proven bravery, causing his despair. Neoptolemus calls the award a robbery, chiefly blaming the Atreidae while also condemning Odysseus for accepting it.

Of Aias, and his fortunes as they are. [AIAS _is discovered_

Aias claims that, if Peleus' son had judged the armor while alive, no one but Aias would have received it. He says that the Atreidae used mean craft to give the heirloom to a clever but inferior man, thereby dishonoring Aias's proven bravery.

_The scene changes to a lonely wooded spot._

The contest for the golden prize is identified as the event that opened Aias's great source of pain. Tecmessa says that Aias's groans and passion revealed the suffering that followed when the contest was decided.

_Enter_ PHILOCTETES.

Neoptolemus calls the loss of Achilles' arms a robbery carried out when the Atridae gave them to Odysseus. He regards the Atridae as chiefly responsible, though he also condemns Odysseus for taking them.

contest for Achilles' arms in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Achilles’ arms were offered as a prize to the bravest, and Ajax and Odysseus competed for them. The Trojans, or according to some the allies, judged the contest and awarded victory to Odysseus.

the contest for Achilles’ arms in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

After Achilles dies, his arms become the subject of a dispute between Ajax and Ulysses. Agamemnon transfers judgment to the assembled Argive leaders. Before the Greek chiefs, Ajax argues from lineage, courage, and service and attacks Ulysses’ speech and stratagem, while Ulysses argues his claim. The chiefs award Achilles’ arms to Ulysses, who wins through his speech.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLES V. AND VI. [XII.536-628]

After Achilles dies, his armor prompts a dispute among the Greek leaders. Agamemnon transfers judgment of the contest to the assembled Argive leaders, with the son of Telamon and Ulysses presented as the main hopeful claimants.

BOOK THE THIRTEENTH.

The contest for Achilles’ armour is argued before the Greek chiefs by Ajax and Ulysses. Ajax bases his claim on his lineage, courage, and service, while attacking Ulysses’ reliance on speech and stratagem; the armour is awarded to Ulysses.

The son of Telamon had {now} ended, and a murmur among the multitude

The contest is between Ajax and Ulysses, with descent, personal merit, and service proposed as grounds for the decision. Ulysses wins the contest through his speech, and the chiefs give him Achilles’ arms.

the contest for Achilles' arms in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

A contest over Achilles’ immortal arms ends when Trojan captives, proposed by Nestor as judges, unanimously award them to Odysseus over Aias.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

BOOK V

Nestor proposes that Trojan captives judge the dispute because they would hate both Greek warriors equally. The captives unanimously give the victory and Achilles' immortal arms to Odysseus rather than Aias.
