Chorus in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Chorus is a group whose membership and role shift across the records, but whose central work is collective witnessing, lament, counsel, praise, and moral response. It includes captive Trojan women serving the Greek army and attending Hecuba; aged men who stand with Amphitryon; Trojan guards on a night watch; Phrygian women mourning Troy; the sons of Bacchus enslaved by Polyphemus; and observers of the dispute involving Andromache and Molossus. It repeatedly gives voice to people endangered, defeated, enslaved, or grieving. It begins with the captive women of Troy: they bring Hecuba news that Polyxena will be sacrificed, share her grief, lament Troy’s destruction, support her against Helen, and recount the wooden horse, slaughter, burning, enslavement, lost homes, husbands, children, shrines, and sacrifices. They also help prepare Astyanax’s funeral ornaments and fear assignment to Greek masters. Elsewhere, aged men praise Hercules, oppose Lycus, celebrate restored hope, and then fear fresh evil; they also hope Athens will protect strangers against Argos and praise Macaria’s willingness to die. During the night watch, the Trojan guards praise Rhesus, pray for Greek defeat, track the guards, fear for Dolon, pursue the night raiders, hear the charioteer, witness the dispute with Hector, respond to the Muse’s lament, and prepare to obey Hector. The sons of Bacchus, deprived of wine and enslaved by Polyphemus, welcome Ulysses’ plan to blind him but evade direct help by feigning injuries. Toward the end, Chorus acts most plainly as a moral commentator: it pities Andromache and Molossus, opposes their execution as unjust, urges reconciliation, announces Peleus, and calls for an end to quarrelling. Its songs condemn divided households and rival wives, while praising inherited nobility, lawful victory, virtue, and power without crime. Across all these appearances, it supports justice, retribution, compassion, and endurance amid changing fortune.

Narrative, section by section

15 sections; each heading opens the text

HECUBA.

The Trojan captive dames serve as Hecuba’s attendants and chorus. They bring Hecuba news of the Greek decision to sacrifice Polyxena and sympathize with her grief.

CHORUS.

The Chorus laments Troy’s ruin and recalls Hecuba’s losses. It predicts Polymestor’s downfall, supports Hecuba’s cause, and observes that the severe suffering he has endured may excuse an assault on life.

MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

The Chorus consists of aged men whom Amphitryon addresses as his comrades. He urges them to cast off grief and live as sweetly as they can because time abandons human hopes.

HERCULES, MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

The Chorus first laments old age and praises Hercules, then celebrates the downfall of Lycus and the return of hope. At the end, it becomes alarmed by a spectre hovering over the palace and fears that new evils are approaching.

DEMOPHOON, IOLAUS, CHORUS.

The Chorus hopes that Jove will help Athens protect the hapless strangers and praises Macaria’s willingness to die for her brothers and the land. It sings that human prosperity and suffering change according to destiny, and invokes Earth, the Moon, Hyperion, Minerva, and the gods for victory over the Argive threat.

RHESUS, HECTOR, CHORUS.

The Chorus praises Rhesus’s courage and prays that he will defeat the Greeks and redden the plain with their blood. During the night watch, it tracks the changing guards, wonders why Dolon has not returned, and fears that he may have been ambushed or killed.

ULYSSES, DIOMEDE.

The Chorus first pursues the night raiders and debates whether Ulysses carried out the exploit. It then hears the charioteer’s account, witnesses the dispute with Hector, and responds to the Muse’s lament before preparing to obey Hector’s orders.

THE TROJAN CAPTIVES.

The chorus of captive Trojan dames fears the judgment assigning each woman to a Greek master and mourns the loss of Troy, their homes, their marriages, and their children. It joins Hecuba in lamentation and questions the fate awaiting the captives.

CHOR. Attendants of the aged Hecuba,

The Chorus supports Hecuba, mourns Troy, and sings odes about the wooden horse, the destruction of the city, and the suffering of its inhabitants. It describes the Greeks’ deception, the slaughter within Troy, the enslavement of the survivors, and the grief of women who lose children and husbands.

MENELAUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Chorus urges Helen to defend her children and country against Helen’s persuasive speech and supports Hecuba’s demand for punishment. In its ode, it mourns Troy’s ruined shrines, lost sacrifices, dead defenders, and the suffering caused by Helen’s marriage.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Chorus helps prepare Astyanax’s funeral ornaments and joins Hecuba in lamentation. It witnesses and describes the burning and destruction of Troy and shares Hecuba’s grief at being driven from the homeland.

ULYSSES, CHORUS.

The Chorus fears Polyphemus but welcomes Ulysses’ plan and agrees to help blind him. When the moment comes, its members pretend to be lame, claim that dust or ashes fill their eyes, and leave Ulysses to rely on his own friends.

CHORUS.

The Chorus laments the destruction caused by the Trojan War and recalls Helen’s disputed reputation, Paris’s abduction, and the Greek losses. It also sings of divine grief, Demeter’s anger, and the rites and music associated with the gods.

MENELAUS, MOLOSSUS, ANDROMACHE, CHORUS.

The Chorus pities Andromache and Molossus and urges Menelaus to reconcile his daughter with the captive. In its ode, it condemns rival wives, divided households, and multiple rulers as sources of strife, and asks pity to prevent the deaths.

PELEUS, MENELAUS, ANDROMACHE, MOLOSSUS, CHORUS.

The Chorus announces Peleus's arrival, urges Peleus and Menelaus to end their unprofitable quarrel, and observes that old men speak with difficult-to-control vehemence. In its ode, it praises inherited nobility, lawful victory, enduring virtue, and power exercised without crime.

Chorus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Chorus is a group of captive Trojan women from Troy, enslaved after its fall and expecting transport to Greece as purchased slaves. It begins by bringing Hecuba news of the Greek decision, mourning slavery, Troy’s destruction, Polyxena, Priam’s house, and its own uncertain life under foreign masters. It repeatedly witnesses and voices grief over Polyxena, Polydore, the dead men of Troy, and the captivity and exile of Trojan women, while attributing catastrophe to fortune, harsh divine fate, and the judgment on Ida. It also judges wrongdoing and suffering: it foresees punishment for Polymestor’s violation of hospitality and justice, considers helping attack the tents, and condemns his abuse of women. It tells Jason that Medea has killed the children and stresses the Gods’ unexpected outcomes. It laments Hippolytus’s exile, suffering, and death, regards him as exceptionally virtuous, and recognizes Venus’s power over Gods and mortals. In Admetus’s grief, it urges endurance, declares Necessity irresistible by prayer, sacrifice, medicine, or song, and calls for Alcestis’s tomb to be honored. Before Pentheus, it supports Bacchus as inferior to no god, praises reverence and divine law, and expects impious people to be punished. Finally, it celebrates the friends of Alcmena, advises obedience to the city’s decision concerning Eurystheus, and later accepts Alcmena’s contrary order.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

HECUBA. CHORUS.

The Chorus, composed of captive Trojan women, brings Hecuba the news of the Greek decision. It laments slavery, the destruction of Troy, Polyxena's fate, and its own uncertain future among foreign masters.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Chorus laments the calamities that have overtaken Priam's house and attributes them to the harsh fate of the Gods. It recalls the judgment on Ida, the war, the destruction of Troy, and the grief of Trojan and Spartan women.

FEMALE ATTENDANT, CHORUS, HECUBA.

The Chorus witnesses Hecuba's grief, mourns the successive deaths of Polyxena and Polydore, and comments on the changes wrought by fortune and law. It laments the destruction of Troy, the deaths of its men, and the captivity and exile of the Trojan women.

POLYMESTOR, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Chorus warns that Polymestor will suffer punishment for violating hospitality and justice. It helps Hecuba and the Trojan women by considering an attack on the tents, later rebukes Polymestor's abuse of all women, and recognizes that his punishment follows his wrongdoing.

JASON, CHORUS.

The Chorus tells Jason that the children are dead by Medea’s hand and urges him to recognize the misery that has overtaken him. At the end, it says that the Gods bring about unexpected outcomes and that the affair has ended in this way.

HIPPOLYTUS, THESEUS, CHORUS.

The Chorus says that human happiness is uncertain and laments Hippolytus's exile. It later reflects on the calamity, praises Hippolytus's character indirectly through its grief, and describes the power of Venus over Gods and mortals.

DIANA, THESEUS, CHORUS.

The Chorus sees Hippolytus approaching with his young flesh and head mangled and laments the double anguish inflicted on the house. After Hippolytus dies, it says that the citizens will shed many tears over the unexpected grief.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

The Chorus urges Admetus to enter the house and endure his grief, reminding him that many have lost wives and children. It declares that Necessity cannot be overcome by songs, medicines, sacrifices, or prayers, and advises that Alcestis's tomb be honored like a divine shrine.

MESSENGER. Pentheus, ruler o'er this Theban land, I come, having left

The Chorus fears to speak openly to Pentheus but declares that Bacchus is inferior to none of the gods. It praises night-long dancing, reverence for divine law, and the eventual punishment of impious people, and it calls for the Bacchae to pursue Pentheus.

SERV. O mistress, I bring news most concise for you to hear, and to myself

The Chorus celebrates the end of fear, the victory of the friends of Alcmena, and the apparent justice of divine action. It advises Alcmena to release Eurystheus because the city has decided that he must not be killed, but later accepts her order against him.

Trojan women in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

The Trojan women are a Chorus of captives enslaved by the Greeks after Troy's fall. Living in tents under new masters, they witness Hecuba's grief amid separation, forced labor, and uncertain destinations. They help Hecuba lure Polymestor into a tent, murder his children, blind him, flee his pursuit, and return to their masters' tents.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

HECUBA. CHORUS.

The Trojan women have been allotted as slaves and led captive by the Greeks after Troy's fall. They are associated with the Chorus and face separation, forced labor, and uncertain destinations.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Trojan women identify where Hecuba is lying and witness her grief. They are captives living in tents under the authority of their new lords.

POLYMESTOR, HECUBA, CHORUS.

The Trojan women help Hecuba lure Polymestor into the tent, kill his children, and blind him. They then flee from his pursuit and later prepare to return to their masters' tents.
