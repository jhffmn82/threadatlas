the Heraclidae in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Heraclidae, children of Hercules, first lived with Ceyx in Trachinia, were expelled at Eurystheus's demand, sheltered by Athenians at Tricorythus and helped defeat him, then after Hyllus's death returned there and settled among Dorians; they held Sparta's kingship until Lysander planned to end it.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

After the translation of Hercales to the gods, hb childraa dwelt

The Heraclidae first dwelt at Trachina with Ceyx, but Eurystheus required Ceyx to banish them. The Athenians received them at Tricorythus, and with Athenian assistance they defeated Eurystheus; after Hyllus was killed, they returned to Tricorythus and later settled among the Dorians.

CHAP. III.

The Heraclidae held the royal power at Sparta. Lysander planned to end their rule by arranging that any Spartan could be chosen king.

children of Hercules in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

The children of Hercules are a group descended from Hercules and Megara, grandchildren of Amphitryon through Hercules. Their story begins under Lycus’s threat: sheltering with Megara and Amphitryon as suppliants at Jupiter’s altar, they await Hercules, while Lycus orders their execution out of fear that they will avenge Creon. Dressed in funeral garments, they are prepared as victims and imagined as bound for Charon’s boat and Pluto’s abode. Hercules returns, embraces them, and takes them into the palace to protect them. During a purification, however, he turns violent; the terrified children flee and hide, but he kills all three in the belief that they are Eurystheus’ children. Hercules mourns them, while Amphitryon must bury them and their tomb is to be visited and lamented. The records also present Hercules’ children as an exiled family pursued by Eurystheus from Argos and other Greek cities. Protected by Athens at Marathon, they seek safety at Jupiter’s altar under Iolaus. Demophoon refuses to surrender them, Macaria offers herself for their welfare, and their eventual victory frees them from Eurystheus’s rule, allowing them to return to their father’s city and inherit its fields. Their lost prospects include rule in Thebes and Oecalia and inheritance in Argos.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

HERCULES DISTRACTED.

The children shelter with Megara and Amphitryon at the altar of Jupiter and repeatedly ask when their father will return. Lycus orders their execution because he fears they will avenge Creon’s death, and Megara prepares to dress them in funeral robes.

CHORUS.

Hercules’ sons are seen being conducted in funereal vestments with Hercules’ wife and his aged father. The Chorus says that the boat of Charon waits to bear them to their eternal home.

MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

Hercules’ sons are being prepared as victims for death and are forced to descend to Pluto’s abode with Megara and Amphitryon. Their father had hoped that they would inherit Argos, Thebes, and Oecalia, but those hopes have proved false.

HERCULES, MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

The children are dressed in funeral garments and cling tightly to Hercules when he returns. Hercules carries them into the palace to protect them from Lycus.

Heraclidæ in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Heraclidæ were a group whose return to the Peloponnese, during Tisamenus’s reign, was led by Temenus, Cresphontes, and the sons of Aristodemus. Claiming Argos through these leaders, they returned to Messenia two generations after Nestor’s death. Their return with the Dorians disturbed nearly all the Peloponnese, expelled rulers from Lacedæmon, Argos, and Messenia, and brought Dorian settlers and population changes.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XII.

The return of the Heraclidæ disturbed the Peloponnese, except Arcadia, and caused many cities to receive Dorian settlers and undergo changes of inhabitants. Phliasia is said to have become Dorian after their return.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Heraclidæ returned to the Peloponnese during the reign of Tisamenus, led by Temenus and Cresphontes and by the sons of Aristodemus. They expelled Tisamenus from Lacedæmon and Argos and the descendants of Nestor from Messenia.

BOOK IV.--MESSENIA.

The return of the Heraclidæ, together with the expedition of the Dorians, drove the descendants of Neleus from Messenia two generations after the war against Ilium. The descendants of Æpytus later became known as Æpytidæ instead of Heraclidæ.
