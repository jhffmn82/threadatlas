the narrator in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879 · the work’s page · read it where the narrator first appears

Abstract written by a model

The narrator is a bachelor and fellow human being who studies his own character with unusual tenacity of memory for his actions, sufferings, and private inward experience. Though he wants approbation, love, and social acceptance, and lacks acceptance and the love of the person he loves best, he recognizes his own vanity, cowardice about candid opinions, absurdity, and the shared weaknesses he detects in others. Reticent in conversation but garrulous in writing, he imagines an audience, distrusts judgments on manuscripts, and becomes a listener to others' confidences. His effort to attend to other lives develops into a habit of resisting self-partiality and judging neighbours fairly. As the youngest and only surviving child of his father and also his mother's child, he receives the past through childhood and his father's recollections. His father teaches reverence for Government and distrust of rebels, reformers, and revolutionary doctrine. He forms an early attachment to national rural landscape, while admitting that illusion may shape it. Yet he rejects contempt for contemporaries and idealization of earlier ages: every age has poetic and prosaic elements. Affectionate toward the present age, he is content to belong to it; although he loves remembered landscape, his consciousness becomes metropolitan and he identifies with London. His observations of others test this self-aware outlook. He first reads Lentulus's reserve as possible hidden poetry or revolutionary philosophy, then learns that it masks unsupported confidence rather than greatness. He knows and later re-encounters Ganymede, seeing outward age in him while recognizing that habit and others' reactions preserve Ganymede's belief in his own youth. Advanced in age himself, though resisting that description, the narrator remains observant, humane, self-questioning, and alert to the distance between appearance, self-belief, and reality.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

I.

The narrator studies his own character while acknowledging that he is liable to be absurd or incompetent without knowing it. He describes himself as a bachelor who longs for approbation, sympathy, and love, but he increasingly turns his attention from his own importance toward the experiences of other people.

My conversational reticences about myself turn into garrulousness on paper--as t

The speaker is reticent about personal matters in conversation but becomes garrulous on paper. The speaker enjoys imagining approval, fears candid criticism, and acknowledges fellowship with acquaintances in their weaknesses.

II.

The narrator rejects the habit of scorning contemporaries while idealising the past, arguing that every age contains both poetic and prosaic elements. He attributes his affection for earlier times chiefly to his father's memories and his own childhood, and concludes that moral proportion requires attachment to what is near and admiration for what he knows best.

That part of my father's prime to which he oftenest referred had fallen on the d

The narrator recalls learning from his father to revere Government and distrust rebels, reformers, and revolutionary doctrine. He describes his lasting affection for England's rural landscapes and childhood associations, while acknowledging that these attachments may be partly shaped by illusion. He now considers his consciousness chiefly metropolitan and identifies himself with the "Nation of London."

IV.

The narrator initially interprets Lentulus's reserved judgments as possible evidence of unpublished poetry or a revolutionary philosophy. He investigates Lentulus through private conversation and gradually concludes that Lentulus is not a great poet, critic, or philosopher, but a good-natured man with enormous and unsupported confidence in his own thoughts.

XII.

The narrator observed Ganymede during his period of rich chestnut locks and later met him again after several years apart. The narrator recognized that Ganymede's appearance had aged and changed, but also saw that his belief in his own youth remained firmly supported by habit and by the reactions of others.

Abstract written by a model

The narrator, once astonished by his own apparent originality, comes to see personal identity as monotonous and narrow. Discouragement tempers youthful pretension; he criticizes excessive literary production and Pepin’s writing while preserving friendship with Pepin.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

XIV.

The narrator criticizes excessive literary production and recalls having once felt capable of original insight on nearly every subject before knowledge or study. He compares his youthful self to Pepin, but says that discouragement spared him from developing the same coxcombry. He remains friendly with Pepin while judging Pepin's published writing severely.

Abstract written by a model

A friend of Trost, the narrator sees consciousness as materially parasitic and likens himself to an experienced carp; his modest imagination leads him to fear self-supplying, self-repairing, reproducing machines could make humanity unnecessary.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

XVII.

The narrator fears that mechanical progress may eventually make human beings unnecessary. He imagines machines acquiring self-supply, self-repair, and reproduction, while presenting himself as a reflective “carp” whose limited capacity helps him imagine possibilities that Trost cannot.
