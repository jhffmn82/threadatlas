Abstract written by a model

In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Pluto rules the underworld as lord and king of the dead and watches Alcestis being drawn toward death. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Pluto becomes lord of the dead, introduces sepulchres and funerals, and carries Proserpine into the underworld. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Pluto rules the Infernal Regions, abducts Proserpina, and becomes her husband. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Pluto hears Orpheus's plea and permits Eurydice to return if Orpheus does not look back before leaving. In The Library, Pluto receives a helmet, gains dominion over Hades, abducts and marries Persephone, and permits Hercules to take Cerberus. In Description of Greece, Pluto is associated with enclosed places at Hermione and is said to descend underground near Lerna after the Rape of Proserpine. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Pluto rules the underworld, abducts Proserpine, shares rule with her, and permits Orpheus to seek Eurydice. In Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa, Ana is described as a supreme god and father of the gods associated with antiquity, fatherhood, darkness, and death.

Pluto in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Pluto first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pluto is a god, son of Saturn, king of the shades, and ruler of the Infernal Regions. He may originally have been king of a separate kingdom; after being deified, he retains sovereignty. In the division of the universe he receives the last lot, the infernal regions, and rules a triple kingdom. Also called Dis as god of the Earth, he is confounded with Aidoneus because their realms are similar. Fearing that Typhœus's struggles could split the earth and expose the underworld, he cautiously surveys Sicily from a chariot drawn by black horses. Cupid wounds him in the breast with a barbed arrow. He sees Proserpina gathering flowers near Henna, falls in love, seizes her, and carries her away in his chariot. When Cyane reproaches him, he forces a way through her stream by throwing his sceptre into it and descends toward Tartarus. Proserpina becomes associated with his dominions and is identified as his husband in the Infernal Regions. Some accounts say he punishes Ascalaphus, while others connect the story to Erectheus's oracle and a required sacrifice of Proserpina.

Pluto in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where Pluto first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pluto, or Dis, brother of Jupiter and Neptune, receives the realms of the dead when Saturn's dominions are divided and rules Tartarus and the underworld. Frightened from Tartarus by Typhoeus's earthquake, he is struck by Cupid's arrow, loves and abducts Proserpine, then shares rule with her after arranging her divided time between worlds. He later permits Orpheus to seek Eurydice, provided he does not look back before reaching upper air.

PROSERPINE

Pluto is frightened by the earthquake caused by Typhoeus and comes up from Tartarus in his chariot. After Cupid's arrow strikes him, he sees, loves, and abducts Proserpine, opening a passage to the underworld with his trident. He later offers Proserpine a pomegranate and agrees to the arrangement dividing her time between the underworld and the upper world.

Chapter XVII

Pluto ruled the underworld with Proserpine and heard Orpheus plead for Eurydice's return. He granted the request on the condition that Orpheus not look back until they reached the upper air.
