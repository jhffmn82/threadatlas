Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Daedalus is an ingenious Athenian architect, sculptor, inventor, murderer of Talus, builder of the Labyrinth, and servant of Cocalus in Sicily. In The Library, Daedalus is an architect, craftsman, inventor, and maker of the Labyrinth who escapes Crete with Icarus and reaches Sicily. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Dædalus is an Athenian architect and sculptor who builds the Labyrinth, kills Perdix, escapes with Icarus, and buries his dead son. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Dædalus is an Athenian artist and inventor whose works serve Minos and whose escape from Crete leads him to Sicily. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Daedalus is a skilled artificer who builds the Labyrinth, invents tools, escapes with Icarus, builds Apollo's temple in Sicily, and pushes Perdix from a tower.

Daedalus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Daedalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Daedalus is an Athenian of the Erecthidae, son of Hymetion, renowned for extraordinary ingenuity in architecture, statuary, stonework, and inventions. He made the saw from a serpent’s jawbone and the potter’s wheel, and was honored by Egyptians as a god for his works, including a porch at Memphis and a wooden statue. Jealousy led him to murder Talus and flee Athens. Under Minos in Crete, he built the labyrinth for the Minotaur. Father of Icarus, he escaped to Sicily, served Cocalus, and built the golden honeycomb for Venus Erycina.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

• And now it is necessary for us to speak of the legislators of Egypt^

Daedalus is said to have visited Egypt and imitated its labyrinth. The Egyptians also claim that he made the porch of Vulcan's temple at Memphis and that a wooden statue made by him was kept in a temple and honored as a god.

CHAP. V.

Daedalus was an Athenian of the family of the Erechthidae and was celebrated for his skill in architecture, statuary, stonework, and the invention of tools. After secretly murdering Talus, he fled to Crete, where he served Minos, helped Pasiphae approach the bull, and built the labyrinth. He later escaped with Icarus and settled in Sicily under the protection of Cocalus, for whom he constructed several notable works.

Daedalus in The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Daedalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Daedalus is an excellent architect, craftsman, and first inventor of images. He makes a statue of Heracles at Pisa and invents a saw by imitating a serpent’s jawbone. After killing Talos, his sister’s son, nephew, and pupil, by throwing him from the Acropolis, he is tried and condemned at the Areopagus and banished from Athens. He flees to Minos, assists Pasiphae by contriving a wooden cow, and constructs the Labyrinth, whose exit he knows. At Ariadne’s request, he reveals the way out and a thread plan. Minos then shuts Daedalus and his son Icarus in the Labyrinth. Daedalus makes wings for them, escapes, and flies to Camicus in Sicily, where Cocalus conceals him. Minos pursues and detects him after Daedalus devises a way to pass thread through a spiral shell. One record also says his corpse is discovered and he is tried at the Areopagus.

Dædalus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Dædalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Dædalus is a talented Athenian architect, of Erechtheus’s family, famed for architecture and statuary. He designs and builds Minos’s Labyrinth in Crete, filling it with winding passages, and assists Pasiphaë’s criminal designs. Jealous of his gifted nephew Perdix, he kills him. Confined by Minos and weary of exile, Dædalus makes wings from feathers, thread, and wax, escapes with Icarus, tries to guide him, then buries him after his death and curses his own arts.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Dædalus, famed for his architectural skill, designs the Labyrinth for Minos. He fills it with innumerable winding passages and makes it so intricate that he can scarcely return to its entrance.

FABLE III. [VIII.183-259]

Dædalus, weary of his exile in Crete and longing for his native soil, devises wings to escape by air. He constructs the wings from feathers, thread, and wax, flies ahead of Icarus, and repeatedly looks back to guide and protect him. After Icarus dies, Dædalus buries him and curses his own arts; the explanation identifies Dædalus as a talented Athenian famed for statuary and architecture who had killed his nephew out of jealousy.

Dædalus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Dædalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Dædalus is an Athenian of the Metionidæ, son of Palamaon, renowned above all for art and misfortunes. After killing his sister’s son, he voluntarily fled to Crete and stayed at Minos’ court, making works for Minos and his daughters. Credited works include figures of Hercules, Trophonius, Britomartis, Athene, Aphrodite, Ariadne’s dancing-ground, and works later lost or effaced by time. Imprisoned in Crete, he escaped with his son in boats he made, using sails against Minos’ rowed vessels. He went to Sicily, to Cocalus and Inycus, where his art led Cocalus’s daughters to plot against Minos; his fame extended through Sicily and most of Italy.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IV.

Dædalus was an Athenian of the Metionidæ and was renowned for his art and misfortunes. After killing his sister’s son, he fled to Minos and Crete, later escaped from prison with his son, and went to Cocalus in Sicily, where his art led Cocalus’s daughters to plot against Minos.

CHAPTER XI.

Daedalus is believed to have made the old wooden statue of Hercules in the temple called Champion. He fled from Crete in boats he made for himself and Icarus, using sails to gain an advantage over the rowed boats of Minos.

CHAPTER XL.

Dædalus was credited with works including the Hercules at Thebes, the Trophonius at Lebadea, the Britomartis at Olus, the Athene at Gnossus, and a dancing-ground of Ariadne in Crete. A wooden statue of Aphrodite at Delos was also attributed to him, while his works dedicated by the Argives in the temple of Hera and those brought to Gela from Omphace had been effaced by time.

Daedalus is a highly skilled artificer who builds the labyrinth for Minos.
