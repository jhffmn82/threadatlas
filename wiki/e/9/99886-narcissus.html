Abstract written by a model

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Narcissus rejects his suitors and Echo, falls in love with his reflection, pines away, dies, and is transformed into a flower. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Narcissus cruelly rejects Echo and other nymphs, falls in love with his reflection, pines beside a fountain, dies, and becomes a flower.

Narcissus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Narcissus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Narcissus is a beautiful youth, a Thespian by birth and son of Cephisus and Liriope, celebrated throughout Aonia but marked by stubborn pride. Many youths and damsels court him, and Echo follows him through forests, yet he despises and rejects suitors, Nymphs, youths, and Echo’s embrace. After hunting, he reaches a clear spring and falls in love with his own reflection, mistaking it for a body. He neglects food and rest, pines away, bids farewell to the image, and dies. Received into the infernal abodes, he still looks at himself in the Stygian waters. He is transformed into a flower whose fading duration reflects his career; his name is said to derive from ναρκᾷν, “to fade away.”

Narcissus was the son of Liriope and the river Cephisus. His name is derived from the Greek word ναρκᾷν, meaning “to fade away,” which characterized his career and the duration of the flower.

opening

Narcissus goes to a clear spring after hunting and becomes captivated by his own reflection. He neglects food and rest, wastes away from love, and dies after bidding farewell to the image.

notes .. notes

Narcissus was a Thespian by birth. In Pausanias’s version, he loved his sister, who resembled him and accompanied him in the chase; after her death, he mistook his reflection in a fountain for her shade, pined away, and died of grief.

Narcissus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Narcissus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Narcissus, said to love his nearly identical twin sister, is associated with a fountain where traditions say he died of love after mistaking his reflection for another person; another says, after her death, he imagined her image there. A flower depicts him but predates the Thespian Narcissus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXI.

Narcissus is said to have looked into the fountain at Donacon, mistaken his reflection for another person, and died there of love. Another tradition says that he had a twin sister who resembled him, and that after her death he visited the fountain and imagined that his reflection was her image.
