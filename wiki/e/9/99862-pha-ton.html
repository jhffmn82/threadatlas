Phaëton in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Phaëton first appears

Abstract written by a model

Phaëton is a mortal charioteer, chiefly presented as the son of the Sun, Apollo, or Phœbus and of Clymene, and as Inachus’s grandson through her. He is equal in spirit and age to Epaphus, but Epaphus’s mockery prompts him to seek proof of his divine descent. He visits the Sun’s palace and obtains permission to guide his father’s chariot for one day. Despite warnings, he drives it, loses command of the horses, and, terrified by the burning world and the Scorpion, abandons the chariot. The resulting conflagration sets the earth on fire, contributes to the darkening of the Æthiopians, and consumes his yellow hair. Jupiter kills him with lightning, and he falls headlong through the air into the Eridanus. His mother and sisters mourn him; Cycnus mourns intensely; and his father grieves, resents the disaster, and blames the flame-footed steeds. He represents rash youth that follows inclination rather than wisdom and prudence. Other accounts make him Rhoda’s son, a fourth descendant of Cecrops, or a Molossian king and student of astronomy who drowned in the Po.

BOOK THE SECOND.

Phaëton visits the palace of Apollo to obtain proof that he is Apollo’s son. He asks to drive his father’s chariot, ignores Apollo’s warnings, loses control of the horses, and becomes terrified as the world burns.

notes .. notes

Phaëton represents the rash young man who consults his own inclination instead of wisdom and prudence. He attempts to drive Phœbus’s chariot, abandons it on seeing the Scorpion, and is associated with excessive heat and devastation. Different accounts describe him as the son of Phœbus and Clymene, the son of another mother, or a king of the Molossians who drowned himself in the Po.

opening
