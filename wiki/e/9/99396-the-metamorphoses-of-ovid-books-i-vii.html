The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

This document is Henry T. Riley’s literal English translation of Ovid’s Metamorphoses, Books I–VII, supplied with an introduction, extensive notes, a synoptical view, and later transcriber, publication, and Project Gutenberg licensing material. The introduction presents Ovid’s life, poetic career, exile to Tomi, death, and major works, while the notes repeatedly explain names, variant traditions, allegories, historical rationalizations, and comparisons with Scripture. The poem begins with its stated purpose: to tell of forms changed into new bodies from the creation of the world to Augustus’s time. Book I orders Chaos into the cosmos, creates man, traces humanity’s decline through the four ages, and brings Jupiter’s flood after Lycaon’s impiety. Deucalion and Pyrrha repopulate the earth from stones. Subsequent transformations include Python’s death, Daphne becoming a laurel, Io’s suffering and restoration, Argus’s eyes becoming the peacock’s ornaments, and Phaëton’s journey toward the Sun. Books II–III continue with Phaëton’s fatal chariot ride, Calisto, Coronis, Ocyrrhoe, Battus, Aglauros, Europa, and Cadmus’s founding of Thebes. They also center on the harsh consequences of divine desire, pride, prophecy, and resistance: Actæon is changed into a stag; Semele dies in Jupiter’s splendor while Bacchus is saved; Narcissus becomes a flower; and Pentheus is destroyed for opposing Bacchus. Books IV–VI frame many tales within storytellers and contests. They include Pyramus and Thisbe, Venus and Mars, Salmacis and Hermaphroditus, the daughters of Minyas, Ino and Cadmus, Perseus and Andromeda, Proserpina’s abduction and Ceres’s search, Arachne’s weaving contest with Minerva, Niobe’s loss of her children, Latona’s Lycians becoming frogs, and the revenge of Progne and Philomela against Tereus. Book VII turns chiefly to Jason and Medea. Medea enables Jason to win the Golden Fleece, rejuvenates Æson, destroys Pelias, and, abandoned by Jason, kills Creon, Jason’s new bride, and her own children before escaping. The book then follows Medea’s failed attempt to poison Theseus, Minos’s threatened war against Athens, Æacus’s plague-stricken kingdom and the creation of the Myrmidons from ants, and Cephalus’s account of jealousy and the accidental death of Procris. The document ends with editorial and licensing records.

front matter

The front matter identifies The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII as Ovid’s work in a literal English translation by Henry T. Riley, with an introduction and notes. The introduction explains the purpose of Riley’s translation and commentary and discusses earlier English translations. The Synoptical View summarizes the principal transformations in Books I-VII, including the creation of the world, the Deluge, transformations involving gods and mortals, and the stories of Jason and Medea. It concludes with Jason’s recovery of the Golden Fleece, Medea’s magic and crimes, and the later stories of Ægeus, Theseus, Minos, and Cephalus.

INTRODUCTION.

The introduction describes Ovid’s education, career, friendships, marriages, and development as a poet. Although his father urged him to pursue law and public office, Ovid abandoned those paths and devoted himself to poetry. Around 8 A.D., Augustus banished Ovid to Tomi, probably because of the immoral tendency of one of his compositions and its connection to scandals involving Julia and the emperor’s granddaughter. Ovid died at Tomi in 18 A.D., and the unit concludes by listing his principal works, including the Metamorphoses.

THE METAMORPHOSES. / BOOK THE FIRST.

The Metamorphoses presents a design to speak of forms changed into new bodies. The speaker asks the Gods to favor the attempt because they changed the forms. The narrative is to extend from the beginning of the world to the speaker’s own times.

notes

The notes discuss the phrase “Forms changed into new bodies” and whether it is an instance of Hypallage. They explain that “formæ” can sometimes mean the things themselves, including gods or wild beasts, so the phrase may not involve deliberate word transposition. They also explain “adspirate” as a metaphor from winds filling sails and remark that the invocation is perhaps too brief for a work as grave as the Metamorphoses. “My own times” refers to the days of Augustus Cæsar.

opening

God and bounteous Nature bring order to Chaos, the rude and undigested mass that initially fills the universe. God separates the earth, waters, heavens, and atmosphere, then places the elements in harmonious arrangement. The fiery heaven rises highest, the air lies beneath it, the Earth settles below them, and the waters surround the solid globe.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the ancient belief that Chaos was pre-existent matter given form and order by a powerful cause. It contrasts this belief with the account of creation in the Book of Genesis, suggesting that mythological traditions preserve a confused version of that account. Hesiod is identified as the first poet to discuss Chaos extensively, while Sanchoniatho is said to have influenced Greek ideas about it. The notes also explain mythological terms associated with the formation and ordering of the universe.

opening

God divides the separated mass into distinct parts and gives the universe its forms, boundaries, climates, waters, winds, stars, and living creatures. The Earth receives plains, valleys, woods, mountains, waters, wild beasts, and eventually Man. Prometheus fashions Man from earth tempered with water, after the image of the Gods, and Man is animated by Minerva. Man is distinguished from other animals by his upright form and ability to look toward the heavens.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Ovid’s account of creation resembles Genesis, especially in presenting man as the Creator’s final work. It identifies the Creator with God, who formed man from divine elements and gave him dominion over other living things. It further interprets Prometheus as tempering the earth and Minerva as animating his workmanship, together reflecting God’s formation and animation of man. The notes also describe the ordering of the earth, heavens, waters, zones, and winds.

opening

The formation of man is followed by the succession of the four ages of the world, beginning with the Golden Age. During the Golden Age, Innocence and Justice govern the world without laws, punishment, or fear. Men live safely and peacefully, without war, soldiers, or concern for foreign shores. The Earth produces food freely, and an eternal spring brings abundant crops, milk, nectar, and honey.

notes .. notes

The explanation says that the heathen poets probably learned from tradition that the first parents initially lived in peaceful innocence. The garden of Eden supplied them with abundant fruit and food without tillage, and animals obeyed them. After the fall, the ground became unfruitful without labor, and nature no longer spontaneously acknowledged man as master. The poets called the happier earlier period the Golden Age, while Latin writers placed corresponding events in Italy under Saturn and Janus; the Scriptures relate them in the histories of Adam and Noah.

opening

After Saturn is driven into Tartarus, Jupiter rules the world and the Silver Age follows the Golden Age. In the Silver Age, Jupiter divides the year into four seasons, and men begin building shelters and cultivating the earth. The Brazen Age is fiercer and more warlike, while the Iron Age brings crime, fraud, violence, greed, warfare, and the breakdown of family and social bonds. Astræa, the virgin goddess, is the last of the heavenly deities to leave the Earth, which is drenched in slaughter.

notes .. notes

The explanation describes the successive Golden, Silver, Brazen, and Iron Ages as stages in humanity’s decline from innocence to wickedness. During the Golden Age, perpetual spring prevailed, while the Silver Age introduced the division of the year into seasons, and the Iron Age continues into the present. It also notes inconsistencies in ancient accounts, including wars and crimes during Saturn’s Golden Age. Saturn expelled his father and seized his throne, Jupiter later treated Saturn similarly, and Jupiter fought the Giants when they tried to dispossess him of the heavens.

opening

The Giants attempt to become masters of heaven by piling mountains up to the stars. Jupiter defeats them with lightning, burying their bodies beneath the mountains they assembled. Earth is drenched with their blood and forms a fierce generation of men from it, but this new generation also despises the Gods above and delights in violence.

notes .. notes

The notes explain that the Giants are said to have piled Olympus, Pelion, and Ossa on one another to reach heaven. The explanation distinguishes the war of the giants from the war between Jupiter and the Titans, who were inhabitants of heaven. It suggests that the fall of the angels may have inspired the story of the Titans, while the building of the tower of Babel may have inspired the Giants’ attempt to reach heaven. It also proposes that the descendants of Cain may correspond to the people described in Scripture as the children “of men” and “giants.”

opening

Jupiter sees the crimes of the human race and summons the Gods to decide the destruction of the world. He recalls Lycaon's polluted banquet and says that Lycaon has plotted against him, leading the Gods to demand vengeance. Jupiter declares that mortals must be cut off because their corruption cannot be cured, and he explains that he descended to Earth in human form to investigate the reports of wrongdoing. The Gods' unanimous alarm is compared with the Romans' reaction when an impious band sought to destroy the Roman name in the blood of Cæsar, and with the subjects' devotion to Augustus.

notes .. notes

Ovid is described as following the prevailing tradition concerning a divine punishment of mankind by a deluge. In the account, Jupiter calls the Gods to a grand council to deliberate about punishing mankind for its wickedness. The explanation compares this tradition with Scripture's account of God deciding to destroy the corrupt earth and its living creatures by a flood. Tradition preserves one just man, whom it identifies as Deucalion, corresponding to Noah in Scripture.

opening

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, tests whether the visiting Jupiter is truly a God by preparing a murderous feast and serving the cooked body of a hostage from the nation of the Molossians. Jupiter destroys Lycaon's house and transforms him into a wolf, who retains traces of his former appearance and continues to desire blood. Jupiter declares that savage Erinnys reigns throughout the earth because men have become wicked. He resolves that all men should soon experience the vengeance they deserve.

notes .. notes

The notes discuss Lycaon’s story, including the claim that he served human flesh to Jupiter and was transformed into a wolf. They question whether Ovid’s account contains an anachronism and suggest that Lycaon’s story may derive from the antediluvian story of Cain. Pausanias and Suidas are said to distinguish between two Lycaons, with the second associated with human sacrifice to Jupiter. The explanation compares Lycaon’s punishment with Cain’s sentence to wander the earth.

opening

Jupiter decides that the corruption of mankind requires the destruction of the human race by a universal deluge. He abandons thunderbolts as the means of punishment and sends rain across the earth, while Neptune assists by releasing the rivers and opening the sources of the waters. The flood overwhelms the Earth, blurs the boundary between land and sea, and destroys or carries away people, animals, buildings, crops, and temples. Most living creatures perish, while those spared by the water are overcome by prolonged fasting and lack of food.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Ovid’s account concerns the deluge of Deucalion, one of the five floods mentioned by Pausanias. Although the deluge was generally said to have covered only Thessaly, Ovid adopts the tradition of a universal flood, with waters from the sea joining those from heaven. Deucalion and Pyrrha are presented as counterparts to Noah and his family, and both pairs are said to have been saved for their virtue. The account also compares Parnassus with Mount Ararat and notes that Deucalion’s deluge, like Noah’s flood, was to be the last.

opening

Neptune commands Triton to sound his shell, causing the sea and rivers to retreat within their shores and banks. Deucalion and Pyrrha, the only surviving people, come to rest on Mount Parnassus and worship the gods and Themis. Jupiter clears the clouds and reveals the Earth as the waters subside, while Neptune restores order to the sea. Seeing the empty world, Deucalion tells Pyrrha that they alone remain of the mortal race.

notes .. notes

Jupiter, Neptune, and Pluto are described as three brothers who were associated with separate kingdoms and, after being deified, with divisions of the world. Neptune received sovereignty over the sea and was occupied with raising and calming it. The unit depicts Neptune commanding the sea to become orderly, with Triton sounding the retreat on his conch shell. Prometheus and Epimetheus are identified as the fathers of Deucalion and Pyrrha, respectively, and Deucalion invokes the arts of his father Prometheus.

opening

Deucalion and Pyrrha seek Themis's help after the loss of their race and pray at her neglected temple. Themis tells them to cover their heads, loosen their garments, and throw the bones of their great mother behind them. Deucalion interprets the “bones” as stones from the body of the earth, and they cast stones behind them. The stones transform into human forms, renewing the human race.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the flood story of Deucalion and Pyrrha, who fled to Mount Parnassus when Thessaly was inundated. The river Peneus was probably stopped by an earthquake, while extraordinary rainfall caused the flooding. After the waters abated, the children of the survivors were said to be stones, possibly reflecting words meaning both “stone” and “child.” Deucalion later went to Athens, where he built a temple to Jupiter and instituted sacrifices.

opening

The Earth, heated by the sun and nourished by moisture, produces animals from the mixture of heat and wetness. Among the creatures it produces is the enormous serpent Python, which terrifies the new race of men. Apollo kills Python with many arrows and establishes the Pythian games to preserve the memory of the deed. The games include contests in boxing, running, and chariot-racing, with crowns of beechen leaves awarded to the winners.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the serpent Python as either noxious reptiles formed from flood slime and destroyed by the sun’s heat, or a robber near Parnassus defeated by Apollo or an Apollo-associated priest. It connects Python’s destruction with the institution of the Pythian games near Delphi. The games began perhaps as a religious or musical contest honoring the Pythian God and later included athletic and equestrian events. The explanation notes that this event must have occurred much later than the time of Deucalion, when Themis, rather than Apollo, delivered the oracles at Delphi.

opening

Apollo falls in love with Daphne, the daughter of Peneus, after Cupid wounds him with a golden arrow. Daphne, struck by Cupid’s leaden arrow, rejects love and flees from Apollo, eventually praying to her father and the powers of nature for rescue. Her body is transformed into a laurel tree, which Apollo adopts as his sacred tree and promises to honor in triumphs and before Augustus’s doors.

notes .. notes

This unit explains notes and interpretations concerning Apollo’s pursuit of Daphne and her transformation into a laurel. Apollo is identified with the Delian God, a youthful deity associated with the lyre, healing herbs, prophecy, the sun, and victory. Daphne flees Apollo to the river Peneus, while later explanations connect her name or nearby laurels with the transformation story. Alternative accounts identify Daphne with Manto or describe her as the victim of an incident involving Leucippus and Apollo.

opening

Jupiter pursues Io, the daughter of Inachus, as she returns from her father's stream. He invites her into the groves of Tempe, claiming he will protect her, but she flees from him. Jupiter covers the earth with darkness, stops her flight, and ravishes her. Inachus, unaware of what has happened, mourns Io and fears that she is dead.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that the story of Io probably originated in Egypt and was incorporated into Greek mythology through the spread of Isis worship to Greece. It identifies Io with Isis and says that Greek authors described Io as the daughter of Inachus, whom Jupiter carried away and by whom he had Epaphus. Juno, jealous of Jupiter's intrigue, placed Io under the watch of Argus, but Jupiter killed Argus and had Io taken aboard a vessel shaped like a cow. The notes also describe Thessalian rivers and places mentioned in the surrounding passage, including Tempe, Pindus, Spercheus, Enipeus, Apidanus, Amphrysus, Æas, Inachus, and Lerna.

opening

Jupiter changes Io, the daughter of Inachus, into a cow to conceal her from Juno, but Juno discovers the deception and takes the cow as a gift. Juno entrusts Io to Argus, whose hundred eyes keep watch over her while she suffers in captivity. Jupiter sends Mercury to kill Argus, and Mercury approaches in disguise as a shepherd, using music and conversation to lull him toward sleep.

notes .. notes

The notes explain several references in the story, including a frequently detected husband, a sleek heifer, military-style watch duty, and attributes associated with Mercury. They identify Phoroneus, Maia, the soporiferous wand or caduceus, and the petasus cap. The explanation states that the story of the Metamorphosis of Io has already been discussed and suggests that myths may derive from historical events altered by tradition and poetic invention.

opening

Syrinx, a Naiad of Arcadia, flees when Pan pursues her because he has fallen in love with her. At the river Ladon, the waters stop her flight, and she asks the Naiads to change her, so they transform her into reeds. Pan grasps the reeds instead of Syrinx, then joins unequal reeds with wax after hearing the wind make a sweet, murmuring sound. The resulting seven-piped instrument bears Syrinx's name.

notes .. notes

The unit identifies the story as possibly an Egyptian fable imported into Greek poetry. It says Pan was probably an Egyptian divinity associated with the worship of nature, though the story may have concerned one of the twelve Pans who was native to Greece. Pan was probably the inventor of the Syrinx, or Pandæan pipe, perhaps making it from reeds growing along the River Ladon. Syrinx may therefore have been called the daughter of the River Ladon.

opening

Mercury lulls Argus completely to sleep by ending his song and using his magic wand. He then kills Argus by striking him with his crooked sword at the neck and casts his blood-stained body from the rock. Juno takes Argus’s hundred eyes and places them as starry gems on the tail feathers of her peacock.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Argus was said to be the son of Arestor and possibly the fourth king of Argos after Inachus. His great wisdom and penetration were represented by the claim that he had a hundred eyes, and he watched over Io. Juno was said to have placed Argus’s eyes in the peacock’s tail, perhaps because its spots resemble human eyes. The peacock was consecrated to Juno, who may symbolize the Air or Æther through which light reaches people.

opening

Juno torments Io with a Fury and invisible stings, driving her across the earth until she reaches the Nile in Egypt. Jupiter persuades Juno to end Io’s punishment, and Io regains her human form before being worshipped in Egypt as the goddess Isis. Io’s son Epaphus is said to be born from Jupiter, and he later quarrels with Phaëton, who asks his mother Clymene for proof that the Sun is his father. Clymene confirms his divine descent, so Phaëton journeys toward the Sun’s dwelling.

notes .. notes

The notes identify Io with Isis, whose priests and worshippers wore linen, and state that her son Epaphus was associated with the Egyptian god Apis by Herodotus. They also discuss Clymene, Merops, Ethiopia, and the meaning of the Sun’s rays in the narrative. Some mythologists add that Mercury lulled Argus to sleep, but Hierax awoke him, after which Mercury killed Argus with a stone and transformed Hierax into a spar-hawk.

BOOK THE SECOND.

Phaëton goes to the Sun’s palace and asks his father, Apollo, for proof of his divine parentage. After Apollo swears by the Styx to grant any request, Phaëton asks to drive the Sun’s chariot for one day. Apollo warns him of the dangers but finally allows him to take the chariot, which the Sun horses soon carry beyond his control. The ride brings destructive fire to the earth, rivers, seas, and heavens, and Earth begs the ruler of the gods to stop it.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the fable of Phaëton as an allegory of the rash young man who follows his own inclination instead of wisdom and prudence. Phaëton, said by different writers to have had different parentage, attempts to drive Phœbus’s chariot and causes excessive heat and devastation. The story may derive from historical or mythic accounts of destructive flames, unusual heat, or the sun’s course. Various writers interpret Phaëton as a king, an astronomer, or a figure connected with Egyptian traditions.

opening .. opening

Jupiter hurls a lightning bolt at Phaëton to stop the fires consuming the universe. Phaëton dies, falls through the air, and is received by the river Eridanus, while the wreckage of his chariot is scattered. The Hesperian Naiads bury him, and Clymene and Phaëton's sisters mourn him. His sisters are transformed into trees, from which tears harden into amber.

notes

The Hesperian Naiads are identified as the Naiads of Italy. They are named Phaëthusa, Lampetie, and Phœbe. The note also suggests that the phrase “passed without the sun” may faintly refer to traditions involving the sun standing still upon Gibeon or the shadow returning ten degrees backward.

opening

Cycnus, king of the Ligurians and related to Phaëton, mourns Phaëton’s death beside the Eridanus and is transformed into a swan. As a swan, he avoids the heavens and fire, choosing pools, lakes, and streams instead. The father of Phaëton, grieving and angry, refuses to continue driving the Sun’s chariot and challenges Jupiter to try the task. The Gods plead with him, while Jupiter defends his action and threatens further punishment; Phœbus then calls and chastises the still-frightened flame-footed steeds.

notes .. notes

The explanation concerns traditions about Phaëton, his tomb, his grieving sisters, and Cycnus. Plutarch places Phaëton’s tomb on the banks of the river Po, where his mother and sisters were said to have ended their lives in grief. Their transformation into poplars that distilled amber, and Cycnus’s transformation into a swan, were poetic traditions. Lucian reported that the inhabitants near the Po had never seen such poplars or swans and did not know the tradition of Phaëton and his sisters.

opening

Jupiter surveys the world after the fire and sees Calisto, a virgin of Nonacris and devoted follower of Diana, in Arcadia. He assumes Diana’s form to approach her, then embraces and assaults her despite her resistance. Calisto returns to Diana’s company ashamed, and the Nymphs notice signs of what has happened. During a later bathing, her pregnancy is exposed, so Diana drives her from the sacred springs and orders her to leave the train.

notes .. notes

Juno transforms Calisto into a bear after learning that she has borne Jupiter’s son, Arcas. Arcas later encounters his mother without recognizing her and is about to kill her, but Jupiter places both of them among the stars. Juno protests that Calisto has been made a goddess and asks that the new constellations be excluded from the sea. The raven is changed from white to black because of its garrulous report, while Coronis’s infidelity is discovered by a bird of Phoebus.

opening

A virgin who is Apollo's favorite tells Minerva how her fidelity led to her punishment and how she became a crow. Minerva entrusted Ericthonius, who was born without a mother, to Pandrosos, Herse, and Aglauros in a basket, forbidding them to investigate its secret; Aglauros opened it and saw a child with a dragon beside him. The virgin reported this to Minerva but was banished from her protection and placed after the bird of the night. She also explains that the God of the Ocean pursued and attempted to assault her before a virgin transformed her into a bird, and she resents being replaced in honor by Nyctimene.

notes .. notes

The unit concerns Ericthonius, his disputed succession to the throne of Athens, and traditions about his origins and deformity. Ericthonius disputed the crown with Amphictyon after Cranaus’s death; Amphictyon initially prevailed, but Ericthonius later succeeded him, while Apollodorus says Ericthonius dethroned Amphictyon. Ericthonius was credited with inventing chariots to conceal his deformed legs and instituting the Panathenæa at Athens. Hyginus says that after his death Ericthonius became the constellation Auriga, or the Charioteer.

opening

Nyctimene is transformed into an owl after defiling the bed of her father, and she avoids human gaze and daylight. The raven tells Apollo that Coronis has been unfaithful with a youth of Hæmonia. Apollo kills Coronis with an arrow, then regrets his vengeance when she dies. He rescues their unborn child from the funeral flames and carries the child to the cave of the two-formed Chiron, while banishing the raven from the white birds.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that history provides no clear basis for the story of Coronis being transformed into a crow for making too faithful a report. It also discusses the raven being changed from white to black for talking too much. These Fables may refer to two unknown persons who perhaps lived as far back as the time of the daughters of Cecrops. Lucian and other writers further associated Coronis, as both a Nymph and a crow, with the birth of her son Æsculapius from a crow's egg in the form of a serpent.

opening

Ocyrrhoe, daughter of the Centaur Chiron, prophesies the future of the infant Æsculapius and the fate of her father. She foretells that Æsculapius will heal mortals, restore the dead to life, be struck down by his grandsire’s bolts, and become a God again after death. She also predicts that Chiron will eventually become subject to death after being wounded by a baneful serpent’s blood. Before she can say more, Ocyrrhoe is transformed into a mare and given the new name Enippe.

notes

The notes explain that a baneful serpent injured Chiron when one of Hercules’ arrows pierced his foot. The arrow had been dipped in the poison of the Lernæan Hydra. The notes also identify the three Goddesses as Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos, the Destinies.

opening

Mercury steals Apollo's cattle and hides them in the woods near Pylos while Apollo is away tending to love and playing his pipe. Battus, an old man who has witnessed the theft, agrees to keep silent after Mercury gives him a cow. Mercury returns in another form and offers a greater reward, causing Battus to reveal where the cattle are. Mercury then transforms Battus into a hard stone called the Touchstone.

notes .. notes

Chiron was a Centaur and the son of Philyra and Saturn. He was renowned for knowledge of medicine, music, and astronomy, and he taught Æsculapius and other heroes, including Hercules and Jason. The Centaurs may have been early horsemen who were later regarded as half-human, half-horse monsters. Ocyrrhoë’s transformation into a mare may have originated from her skill in riding and managing horses under her father’s instruction.

opening

Mercury falls in love with Herse, the most beautiful of the virgins returning from a sacred ceremony to Minerva's citadel. He approaches her house and asks Aglauros to help him gain access to Herse, identifying Jupiter as his father. Aglauros demands a large amount of gold for her assistance and forces Mercury to leave. Minerva, remembering Aglauros's profanation of her mysteries, goes to the abode of Envy.

notes .. notes

The unit explains references to Munychia, Balearic slingers, Mercury’s mantle and wings, and Vulcan’s connection with Lemnos and Ericthonius. It states that Cicero reported several ancient persons named Mercury. One of these Mercuries was probably in love with Herse, a daughter of Cecrops, king of Athens, and Aglauros became jealous of Herse. The tradition was likely based on ordinary events.

opening

Minerva commands Envy to make Aglauros jealous of her sister Herse. Envy infects Aglauros with poison and forces her to dwell on Herse’s fortunate marriage to Mercury. Aglauros then blocks Mercury’s passage to Herse, but Mercury opens the door and Aglauros is transformed into a bloodless stone statue.

notes .. notes

The notes explain that Minerva was called Tritonia either because she sprang from Jupiter's head or because of Trito, a lake in Libya. Tritonian refers to Athens, which was sacred to Pallas or Minerva. Pausanias gives a version in which the daughters of Cecrops, driven mad, threw themselves from a tower. The explanation suggests that their story may have reflected resistance to the introduction of Pallas's worship, while Pandrosos was honored for remaining faithful to Minerva.

opening

Jupiter assumes the form of a beautiful, gentle white bull and mingles with the king's herd. Europa, daughter of Agenor, approaches the bull, decorates him with garlands, and sits on his back. Jupiter carries Europa from the shore into the ocean, where she looks back in fear while clinging to his horn and back. He takes her toward the isle of Crete.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the mythological abduction of Europa, daughter of Agenor, King of Tyre, by Jupiter Asterius. It presents several theories that the story arose from a ship shaped like a bull, a commander named Taurus, or a Phoenician word meaning either ship or bull. Jupiter Asterius is described as the son of Teutamus and as connected with the settlement and first kingship of Crete. After Europa's death, she was honored as a divinity, and a festival called Hellotia was established in her memory.

BOOK THE THIRD.

Jupiter carries away Europa in the shape of a bull and later reveals himself in the Dictæan land. Agenor commands his son Cadmus to search for Europa, threatening him with exile if he fails to find her. Cadmus consults the oracle of Phœbus, follows a cow to the place where she lies down, and is instructed to build a city there and call the country Bœotia. As Cadmus prepares a sacrifice, his servants approach a spring guarded by a dragon sacred to Mars.

notes .. notes

The notes explain that Cadmus continued his search after Telephassa’s death and went to Delphi to consult the oracle. They describe Europa’s ancestry according to Apollodorus: Libya and Neptune had Belus and Agenor, and Agenor and Telephassa had Cadmus, Phœnix, Cilix, and Europa. Other writers instead made Europa the daughter of Phœnix and granddaughter of Agenor. The explanation also discusses theories that Europe was named after Europa or after the fair complexion of its inhabitants.

opening

Cadmus's companions go to the fountain of Mars for water and are devoured by the dragon guarding it. Cadmus finds their bodies, fights the dragon, and kills it by pinning its throat to an oak with his spear. At Minerva's advice, Cadmus sows the dragon's teeth, from which armed earth-born warriors arise and kill one another until five remain. These five assist Cadmus in building the city of Thebes, as ordered by the oracle of Phœbus.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the notes on the dragon encountered by Cadmus and the myth of the earth-born warriors. Agenor sent his sons to search for his lost daughter, while Cadmus eventually settled in Bœotia, killed the dragon, and sowed its teeth. The explanation presents alternative interpretations in which the dragon was a hostile king or serpent and the dragon’s teeth and earth-born men represented soldiers who joined Cadmus. These men helped Cadmus defeat his enemies and build the citadel of Thebes, while Apollodorus says that Cadmus served Mars to atone for killing the dragon.

Actæon, grandson of Cadmus, accidentally enters Gargaphie and sees Diana bathing with her Nymphs. Diana transforms him into a stag after he sees her without her garments. Actæon's hunting dogs pursue and tear apart their own master, while his companions unknowingly urge them on. The text presents Actæon's misfortune as an accident rather than a crime.

notes .. notes

The unit provides notes and explanations about the fable of Actæon, who saw Diana bathing and was punished. It identifies Actæon as a Boeotian youth and lists the names and meanings of many of his hunting dogs. The explanation criticizes Ovid’s portrayal of Diana’s cruel revenge and discusses several ancient interpretations of Actæon’s transformation and death. Some sources say that Actæon’s dogs devoured him, while others explain the story symbolically or assign different offenses to him.

opening

Juno, enraged that Semele is pregnant by Jupiter, resolves to destroy her and transfers her hatred of Jupiter's Tyrian mistress to Semele. Disguised as the old nurse Beroë, Juno persuades Semele to demand that Jupiter appear in the same divine splendor in which he approaches Juno. Semele asks Jupiter to grant this request, and he swears by the Styx that he will deny her nothing. Regretting the oath, Jupiter prepares to appear with clouds, storms, lightning, thunder, and the thunderbolt.

notes .. notes

The Fable most probably arose from an intrigue between Semele and one of the princes called Jupiter, which ended tragically. Pausanias says that Cadmus had Semele and her son thrown into the sea, after which Semele reached Oreate and was buried there. According to Apollodorus, Bacchus later brought Semele from hell and carried her to heaven, where she was ranked among the Goddesses as Thyone. Nonnus describes her as conversing with Pallas and Diana and dining with Jupiter, Mercury, Mars, and Venus.

opening

Jupiter visits Semele as promised, but the effulgence of his lightning burns her to ashes. Bacchus, whom Semele is carrying, is removed from her womb and placed in Jupiter's thigh until he completes his gestation, after which Ino and the Nyseian Nymphs care for him. Jupiter and Juno dispute whether sexual pleasure is greater for females or males, and Tiresias confirms Jupiter's opinion after having experienced both sexes. Juno punishes Tiresias by making him permanently blind, while Jupiter grants him knowledge of future events.

notes

The unit explains that the Nyseian Nymphs brought up Bacchus in Nysa, where he was concealed from the fury of Juno. It names the nymphs as Cysseis, Nysa, Erato, Eryphia, Bromia, and Polyhymnia. The cave where Bacchus was concealed was said to have two entrances, and the name Nysa contributed in part to his Greek name Dionysus.

opening

Narcissus, son of Cephisus and Liriope, is renowned for his beauty and rejects everyone who courts him. A prophecy says he will live to old age only if he never recognizes himself. Juno punishes Echo by limiting her speech because Echo distracted her with stories while Jupiter's mistresses escaped. Echo falls in love with Narcissus, but he rejects her, and she wastes away until only her voice remains.

notes .. notes

The notes explain the names Aonia, Liriope, and Narcissus, and comment on ancient customs involving sulphur-tipped torches. The explanation treats the story of Echo as an attempt to account for an extraordinary natural phenomenon through natural philosophy rather than history. It says that the poets portrayed Echo as the daughter of the Air and the Tongue and Pan as in love with her. Historically, the story may have originated with a Nymph lost in the woods, whose rescuers heard only echoes of their own voices.

opening

Narcissus, having rejected many Nymphs and youths, is cursed to love without possessing what he loves. At a clear, untouched spring, he falls in love with his own reflection and wastes away while gazing at it. Echo repeats his words and laments his decline. After Narcissus dies, his body disappears and a yellow flower with white leaves is found in its place.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Narcissus was a Thespian whose story serves as a moral lesson about the fatal effects of self-love. His pursuit of his own image is compared to the unreal pleasures that people eagerly pursue. Pausanias gives a different account in which Narcissus mistakes his reflection for the shade of his dead sister and dies of grief. The unit also discusses Tiresias, the renowned soothsayer who predicted Narcissus’s fate and became blind late in life.

Tiresias predicts that Pentheus will be torn apart if he refuses to honor Bacchus, whose arrival in Greece is accompanied by public celebrations. Pentheus rejects the prophecy, forbids Bacchic worship, and orders Bacchus captured, but Bacchus’s follower Acœtes recounts how the god transformed impious sailors into dolphins. Pentheus then goes to Mount Cithæron to stop the rites, where Agave and the other Bacchantes attack and dismember him. Afterward, the matrons of Thebes accept the new worship and honor Bacchus’s altars.

notes .. notes

The unit explains references connected with Bacchus, including his symbols, festivals, worship, and the story of Pentheus. Pentheus, king of Thebes and grandson of Cadmus, opposed the abuses associated with Bacchic worship and was torn apart by Bacchantes, including his mother Agave. The account also describes Bacchus’s punishment of impious mariners near Naxos and discusses traditions identifying Bacchus with the Egyptian Osiris. It further explains the Trieterica and the role of Cithæron in Bacchic rites.

BOOK THE FOURTH.

Alcithoë and the other daughters of Minyas reject the rites of Bacchus and remain indoors working while the Bacchic festival proceeds. One daughter tells how Pyramus and Thisbe, lovers in Babylon whose parents forbid their marriage, arrange to meet at the tomb of Ninus. Thisbe flees from a lioness and leaves behind her veil, leading Pyramus to believe she has died and to kill himself with his sword. Thisbe returns, finds Pyramus dead, and kills herself with the same sword; their bodies are placed in one urn, and the mulberry fruit becomes dark.

notes .. notes

The unit explains notes concerning Alcithoë, the daughters of Minyas, and the worship and rites of Bacchus, including the meanings of several names and the opposition to his cult. It discusses Dercetis, her association with fish imagery, Semiramis, and Babylon. It also explains the story of Pyramus and Thisbe, whose meeting and deaths are associated with Babylon, and interprets the tale as a moral story. The unit compares this story with Shakspere’s Midsummer Night’s Dream.

opening

Leuconoë relates how the Sun discovered the adultery of Venus with Mars and revealed it to Vulcan. Vulcan trapped Venus and Mars in invisible chains and exposed them to the gods, who laughed at them. Venus retaliated by causing the Sun to fall in love with Leucothoë, distracting him from his duties and altering his movements across the sky. The Sun disguised himself as Leucothoë’s mother, revealed his identity, and won her submission to his embrace.

notes .. notes

The notes explain references to the story of Mars and Venus, including Vulcan as the son of Juno, Venus’s connection with Cythera, and the Sun’s role in discovering the intrigue. Plutarch says that people born under the conjunction of Mars and Venus are naturally amorous, but that their indiscretions are quickly discovered if the Sun is not distant. Palæphatus gives a historical explanation in which Helius, the son of Vulcan and king of Egypt, exposes and punishes a courtier involved with a lady of the court. The explanation says that similarity of names helped produce the fable, which Homer first related and Ovid copied with a small variation.

opening

Clytie, jealous of the Sun's love for Leucothoë, reveals Leucothoë's affair to her father. Leucothoë's father buries her alive, but the grieving Sun uses nectar to transform her body into a frankincense tree. The Sun then rejects Clytie, who pines away while watching him and is transformed into a sunflower.

notes .. notes

The notes explain that Leucothoë was said to have been buried alive by her father Orchamus after falling in love with Apollo, while her rival Clytie was metamorphosed into a sunflower. No historical basis has been established for these events, which may instead derive from principles of natural philosophy. Leucothoë may have represented the frankincense tree introduced into Persia by Orchamus, and her connection with Apollo may reflect the plant's medicinal use. Clytie's jealousy may have been linked to a claim that the sunflower kills the frankincense tree when growing nearby.

opening

Alcithoë is called upon to tell another story while working at her web. She recounts that Daphnis, a shepherd of Ida, was turned into stone by the resentment of his paramour, a Nymph. She also mentions Scython changing from a man into a woman, Celmus becoming adamant, and the Curetes springing from a plenteous shower of rain. Crocus and Smilax were changed together into little flowers.

notes .. notes

The unit discusses Daphnis, who was said to have been turned into stone, possibly as an allegory for insensibility, and identifies Thalia as the Nymph he affronted. It explains that Scython's sex change may reflect the renaming of Thrace from Scython to a feminine name. Celmus was described as a wise and moderate young man who became adamant, or as Jupiter's foster-father imprisoned for revealing the Gods' immortality. Crocus and Smilax were said either to have been a faithful married couple or to have become flowers after Crocus rejected Smilax, while the Curetes were associated with Crete, rain, and the protection of Jupiter.

Salmacis, a Naiad who avoids hunting, falls in love with Hermaphroditus when she sees him near her spring. Hermaphroditus rejects her advances, but she hides, waits until he enters the water, and seizes him when he is bathing. The Gods unite their bodies into one two-sexed form after Salmacis prays that they never be separated. Hermaphroditus then asks that men who enter the spring emerge half male, and his parents cause the waters to enervate and soften those who touch them.

notes .. notes

The unit describes Caria, near Halicarnassus, where a fountain was said to change men into women. Barbarians driven from the area by an Argive colony obtained water from the fountain and encountered Greek colonists there. Their intercourse with the colonists civilized them but also corrupted them through the introduction of the luxurious manners of Greece. The fountain’s effects may have resulted from a peculiar chemical quality in its water, which supposedly made drinkers soft and effeminate.

The unit explains the myth of Ino, Athamas, and Melicerta, including their family history, conflicts, deaths, and later worship as sea deities. opening

Cadmus leaves Thebes after the calamities and prodigies affecting his family and wanders with his wife Hermione to Illyria. Remembering the dragon he killed and whose teeth he sowed, Cadmus wishes to become a serpent and is transformed. Hermione laments his change and asks the Gods to transform her as well, after which she becomes a serpent too. The two serpents enter a nearby grove and no longer harm or avoid mankind.

notes

Cadmus was driven from the throne of Thebes after a conspiracy and retired with his wife Hermione into Illyria. He commanded the Illyrian army, became king, and assisted the Enchelians in their war against the Illyrians. The story that Cadmus and Hermione became serpents was later invented, possibly from associations among the Phoenicians, the word for serpent, and the Illyrians. Various writers also proposed alternative explanations for Cadmus's name and origin.

Perseus, son of Jupiter and Danaë, carries Medusa’s head across Africa and Libya, where its bloody drops produce serpents. He travels to Atlas’s kingdom and asks for hospitality, but Atlas remembers an oracle that a son of Jupiter will take his golden fruit and drives Perseus away. The unit concerns explanations of the myths of Danaë, Perseus, Medusa, and the Gorgons. It describes Acrisius’s attempt to prevent Danaë from having a son who would kill him, the birth and exposure of Perseus, and Perseus’s killing of Medusa. opening

Perseus reaches Æthiopia and rescues Andromeda from a sea monster after finding her chained to a rock as punishment for her mother's boast. He kills the monster, marries Andromeda, and places Medusa's head on sea plants, which become coral. At the wedding feast, Perseus recounts how he beheaded Medusa and explains that Minerva transformed Medusa's hair into serpents after Neptune violated her in Minerva's temple.

notes .. notes

The notes explain details of Perseus’s rescue of Andromeda, his encounter with the sea monster, and his slaying of Medusa. They identify the sea monster as a punishment sent by Neptune at the Nereids’ request because Cassiope compared her beauty with theirs, and describe the oracle of Jupiter Ammon as demanding Andromeda’s exposure. The explanation proposes that the stories of Pegasus, coral, Andromeda’s exposure, and Phineus may derive from ships, safer navigation, a forced marriage to a pirate, and political conflict. BOOK THE FIFTH.

While Perseus relates his adventures, Phineus attacks him at the wedding feast because Andromeda had previously been promised to Phineus. Cepheus defends Perseus and explains that Perseus saved Andromeda from the sea monster, while Perseus fights Phineus and his numerous followers with help from Pallas. When Perseus displays the Gorgon head, many attackers and some of his own soldiers become stone, and he finally petrifies Phineus. Perseus then enters his native city with Andromeda and attacks the usurper Prœtus.

notes .. notes

The notes explain that the story of Andromeda may have taken place in Æthiopia, but is more probably associated with the coast of Africa near Joppa. Josephus, Strabo, Pomponius Mela, and Pliny connect the event with Joppa and describe local traditions about Andromeda’s chains, the royal family, and the monster. The explanation interprets Perseus’s power to turn enemies to stone as a metaphor for his heroism and the terror caused by his victory over the Gorgons. It also suggests that the alleged metamorphoses into stone may have arisen from the rocky nature of Seriphus or from reports about Medusa’s head.

Minerva leaves Seriphus and visits the Muses at Mount Helicon, where she learns about Pegasus, the fountain he created, and Pyreneus’s failed attempt to assault the Muses. The Muses also tell Minerva how the nine Pierides challenged them to a singing contest and were transformed into magpies after losing. Calliope then prepares to sing for the contest.

notes .. notes

The notes describe Polydectes, king of Seriphus, who desired Danaë and demanded that Perseus bring him Medusa’s head; Perseus later turned Polydectes to stone after finding Danaë and Dictys seeking refuge. They also explain references to the Muses, including their connection with Helicon, Urania, Mnemosyne, and Aonia. The explanation presents Plutarch’s interpretation of the Muses’ adventure with Pyreneus as an allegory about hostility to learning. It also interprets the Pierides’ challenge to the Muses as a story derived from Pierus’s poor and irreverent poetry, with his daughters being changed into magpies.

opening

Ceres is praised as the first to plough the earth, provide corn and wholesome food, and establish laws. The island of Trinacria lies upon and restrains the giant Typhœus, whose struggles cause earthquakes and threaten to open the underworld. Pluto leaves his dark abode to inspect the foundations of Sicily, and Erycina, seeing him, urges Cupid to use an arrow against him. Cupid selects one especially sharp and unerring arrow and wounds Pluto in the breast.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that the ancients often attributed difficult natural phenomena to supernatural causes because they lacked natural-philosophical explanations. Ætna’s flames and violent earth shocks were said to result from the Gods throwing Mount Ætna onto the defeated Giant Typhœus, or, in another account, Enceladus. The giant’s attempts to escape the mountain’s weight were believed to cause volcanic eruptions and earthquakes. The notes also identify Sicily’s three promontories and explain several names associated with the island and its mythology.

The unit explains the myth of the river Alpheus and the fountain Arethusa, including their supposed connection between Elis and Sicily. The Chimæra is described in poetry as a lion-headed, goat-bodied, dragon-tailed monster, though it may have been a volcanic mountain in Lycia. Boreas seeks to marry Orithyïa, but Erectheus refuses his consent, so Boreas carries her away by force to Thrace. Boreas declares that violence suits his nature and uses his wind power to abduct Orithyïa. Orithyïa becomes Boreas's wife and gives birth to the winged twins Calaïs and Zethes. When they grow older, Calaïs and Zethes join the Minyæ in seeking the Golden Fleece aboard the first ship.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Plato treated the rape of Orithyïa as an allegory for Orithyïa being blown into the sea and drowned. Apollodorus and Pausanias instead regarded it as historical, saying that Boreas, king of Thrace, seized Orithyïa near the river Ilissus and carried her to his dominions. There she became the mother of the twins Calaïs and Zethes, who later delivered Phineus from the Harpies during the Argonautic expedition.

BOOK THE SEVENTH.

Jason and the Argonauts arrive in Colchis seeking the Golden Fleece from Æetes. Medea falls violently in love with Jason and uses her enchantments and enchanted herbs to protect him from the brazen-footed bulls, the Earth-born warriors, and the dragon guarding the fleece. Jason completes the required labors, obtains the Golden Fleece, and returns victoriously to Iolcos with Medea as his wife.

notes .. notes

The unit explains the origins and legendary development of the Argonauts’ expedition to Colchis. Jason leads the Argonauts from Iolcos to recover the treasures of Athamas and avenge Phryxus, aided by Medea after she falls in love with him. It recounts the Argonauts’ earlier adventures, including their stay at Lemnos and their rescue of Phineus from the Harpies. The explanation also suggests that the Golden Fleece, fiery bulls, dragon, and dragon’s teeth may have arisen from misunderstood foreign-language accounts of treasures, defenses, and troops.

opening

Jason asks Medea to restore his aged father Æson, and Medea uses her magic, herbs, rituals, and a potion to rejuvenate him. Medea then deceives the daughters of Pelias by demonstrating that she can turn an old ram into a lamb and promising to restore their father’s youth. The daughters stab the sleeping Pelias so that Medea can replace his old blood, but Medea cuts his throat and puts his mangled body into the boiling cauldron. Medea thereby carries out her revenge against Pelias and escapes the consequences of his death.

notes .. notes

The notes explain the magical rites and ingredients associated with Medea’s restoration of Æson to youth, including bare feet, Hecate, copper implements, repeated ritual numbers, and various animals and plants. They identify Medea as the daughter of Æetes and describe her as using a potent mixture and ritual actions to renew Æson. The explanation presents competing interpretations, including the transfusion of youthful blood or the administration of a restorative potion made from herbs. It further suggests that Medea may have killed Pelias by mixing venomous herbs into his drink when his daughters sought the same favor for him.

opening

Medea travels to Corinth after killing Pelias and discovers that Jason has married the daughter of King Creon. She sets fire to the palace, killing the new bride and her father. Medea then murders the two children she had with Jason before his eyes and flees in a chariot drawn by winged dragons.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Jason, after reconciling with the children of Pelias, gave the crown to Acastus and married Glauce, also called Creüsa, daughter of Creon, after becoming tired of Medea. Medea caused Creon and Glauce to be consumed in the burning palace and killed her own children, although Euripides and Pausanias report that the Corinthians were responsible for the children’s deaths. The notes describe Medea’s flight in a chariot drawn by dragons and identify numerous allusions to transformations of people into animals, plants, birds, and natural features. The explanation also interprets some transformations as poetic accounts of escape, concealment, grief, lost persons, or resemblance of names.

opening

Hercules drags the chained Cerberus from the Infernal Regions, and the dog’s foam is said to have produced aconite. Medea prepares aconite in a poisoned cup for Theseus, but Ægeus recognizes his son by the markings on his sword and strikes the cup away. Ægeus celebrates Theseus’s survival with sacrifices and a public festival, while Minos prepares a war against Athens to avenge the death of his son Androgeus and gathers allied forces from several islands. Medea escapes by using enchantments, and a woman who betrayed a citadel for gold is changed into a jackdaw.

notes .. notes

The notes describe Cerberus, aconite, Medea’s attempt to remove Theseus, and Theseus’s recognition by Ægeus and subsequent exploits. Theseus killed several robbers and monsters, including the bull at Marathon, and later traveled among islands near Crete. The explanation connects Greek mythology about the Elysian Fields and Infernal Regions with Egyptian ideas about the afterlife. It also suggests that stories about Cerberus, aconite, witches, and magical spells may have originated in real animals, plants, and ritual practices.

opening

Minos seeks an alliance with Æacus on the island of Œnopia but is refused and departs threateningly. Cephalus then arrives from Athens and successfully asks Æacus for military assistance against those seeking to subdue Achaia. Æacus explains that a pestilence caused the deaths of nearly all the people he once ruled. He describes the disease’s devastation of animals, farmers, citizens, families, religious rites, and funeral practices.

notes .. notes

Minos, after succeeding his father Lycastus, conquered islands near Crete and eventually controlled those seas through the strength of his fleet. At the Panathenæa in Athens, his son Androgeus won all the prizes and gained the friendship of the sons of Pallas, provoking the jealousy of Ægeus. Ægeus caused Androgeus to be assassinated near Œnoë, though another account says that the Bull of Marathon killed him. Minos responded by preparing a strong fleet, seeking alliances, and pursuing revenge; the narrative also includes the plague at Ægina.

opening

Æacus asks Jupiter to restore his people after an overwhelming misery has left his city empty. Near a sacred oak, Æacus sees ants carrying grain and asks Jupiter for citizens as numerous as they are. Jupiter transforms the ants into men, who approach Æacus, accept him as king, and become the Myrmidons. Æacus gives them the vacant city and lands, and they prepare to accompany Telamon to war.

notes .. notes

The explanation concerns Æacus, his subjects, the plague that afflicted their country, and the origin of the Myrmidons. It suggests that Æacus’s subjects hid in woods and caverns and returned after the contagion ended, while many old men may have died and younger people survived. Another account identifies the Myrmidons as a barbarous, industrious people of Thessaly whom Æacus brought to repopulate his island after a pestilence. Their association with ants likely inspired the story that Jupiter transformed ants into men.

opening

Cephalus tells Phocus how Aurora carried him off, how he tested Procris’s fidelity in disguise, and how Procris later returned to him with a magical dog and javelin. Procris’s virtue initially withstands his advances, but she flees in shame when he reveals himself and later becomes reconciled with him. Cephalus recounts how Lælaps pursued a wild beast sent by Themis to ravage Thebes, until both animals were transformed into marble statues.

notes

The unit explains that there were two princes named Cephalus: one was the son of Mercury and Herse, and the other was the son of Deïoneus and Diomeda. The son of Deïoneus married Procris, while the text says that Ovid and other writers described him as being carried off by Aurora and later returning to Procris. Apollodorus initially follows this genealogy but later confuses the actions of the two princes.

opening

Cephalus recounts to Phocus how jealousy caused Procris to suspect him of loving another. Procris secretly followed Cephalus into the forest, where he mistook the rustling leaves for a wild beast and killed her with the javelin she had given him. As Procris died, she asked Cephalus not to let the Nymph Aura share their marriage ties, but he explained that Aura meant the breeze. Cephalus’s account ends with Procris dying more peacefully after recognizing the mistake.

notes .. notes

The unit explains that Cephalus's early-morning love of the chase gave rise to the story of his love for Aurora. According to Apollodorus, Procris had an amour with Pteleon and probably spread a report of her own infidelity to divert attention from it, after which she fled to Minos in Crete. Minos fell in love with Procris, who later returned to Thoricus and was reconciled with Cephalus, receiving from him the celebrated dog and javelin mentioned by Ovid. Procris's death was attributed partly to resentment, and Cephalus was condemned to perpetual banishment by the Areiopagus.
