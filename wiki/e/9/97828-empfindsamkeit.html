Abstract written by a model

Empfindsamkeit is a German literary and emotional movement centered on natural humanity, freedom from convention, primal feeling, sympathy, tears, humane moral relations, and the expression of restrained emotion. It gained acceptance in Germany within two years after the 1768 review and was strongly organized by Laurence Sterne’s works, especially Yorick and the Sentimental Journey. These supplied models of tender beneficence, noble humanity, sentimental encounters, and concern for animals, servants, poor travelers, beggars, and people in distress. It also provided groundwork for Werther, though Sterne’s whim and sentiment were distinguished from later treatments. The movement spread through translations, additions, and imitations. Bode emphasized sympathy, tears, animals, service, and self-reproach, often without Sterne’s whimsy and caprice. Other writing intensified sentimental sympathy through religious encounters, compassion for erring women, graveyards, beggars, soldiers, and child-murderesses. Yet such treatments could become excessive, abortive, conventional, or detached from Sterne’s delicacy. Over time Empfindsamkeit provoked parody and opposition. Some writers mocked its formulas; later works reduced it to a “grinning shade,” retaining only light-heartedness or moderate sentiment. Critics charged it with artificial emotional display, eccentricity, imitation, and even moral and psychological harm. It was called a moral pestilence and was portrayed as causing mental aberration. Satire and more serious criticism presented it as a disorder fostered by temperament, education, custom, reading, imagination, and imitation. Thus it moves from rapid acceptance and influential humane feeling to dilution, ridicule, opposition, and eventual loss of force as an actuating literary principle.
