The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

These records summarize Books Eight through Fifteen of The Metamorphoses, a connected sequence of myths, explanations, and historical legends in which divine action, passion, violence, loss, and changing forms repeatedly shape human and nonhuman lives. The narrative begins around Crete and Athens: Minos conquers through Scylla’s betrayal of Nisus, while Theseus escapes the Labyrinth after killing the Minotaur, abandons Ariadne, and leaves her to Bacchus. Dædalus and Icarus attempt flight; Icarus falls, and Dædalus’s jealous treatment of Perdix is recalled. Calydon’s boar hunt brings Meleager glory and death through Althæa’s fatal choice. The work then moves through tales of hospitality, impiety, desire, and punishment: Philemon and Baucis are rewarded, Erisicthon is consumed by hunger, Hercules defeats Acheloüs, dies through Deïanira’s poisoned gift, and becomes divine. Byblis, Iphis, Orpheus, Cyparissus, Pygmalion, Myrrha, Adonis, Midas, and others undergo losses or transformations arising from love, grief, folly, or divine favor. Orpheus’s failed rescue of Eurydice frames later songs and stories, before he is killed by Thracian women and reunited with her below. The account expands into Trojan and Greek warfare. Ceyx dies at sea and becomes a kingfisher with Halcyone; the Greeks reach Troy after Iphigenia is spared, Achilles kills Cygnus, and tales of Cæneus and the Centaurs interrupt the campaign. Achilles dies through Paris’s arrow; Ajax and Ulysses dispute his armour, Ulysses wins, and Ajax kills himself. Troy then falls amid the deaths and sufferings of Priam’s family, Polyxena, Hecuba, and Polydorus. After Troy, Æneas travels through places and encounters stories involving Galatea, Acis, Glaucus, Circe, the Sibyl, Ulysses’s survivors, Picus, and Canens. His eventual deification links Trojan survival to later Italian and Roman traditions. Vertumnus finally wins Pomona, while Romulus becomes Quirinus and Hersilia becomes Ora. The final book centers on Numa and Pythagoras, whose teaching makes continual change the work’s governing principle: souls, bodies, elements, lands, animals, and nations alter but do not wholly perish. Numa’s peaceful rule, Rome’s plague and rescue by Æsculapius, and Julius Cæsar’s ascent as a star lead to praise of Augustus. The Poet closes by claiming that the poem itself will preserve his name.

BOOK THE EIGHTH.

Minos besieges Alcathoë, the city of Nisus, whose safety depends on a purple lock of hair on Nisus's head. Nisus's daughter Scylla falls in love with Minos, cuts off the lock, and gives it to him so that he can conquer the city. Minos condemns her treachery, refuses her love, and sails away. Scylla follows his fleet, but when Nisus, transformed into a sea eagle, attacks her, she is changed into the bird called Ciris.

opening

Minos, after conquering the Athenians, requires a recurring tribute of young men and women, who are said to be exposed to the Minotaur in the Labyrinth. Dædalus builds the intricate Labyrinth, and Theseus enters it, kills the Minotaur, and escapes with Ariadne’s help using her thread. Theseus then abandons Ariadne on Dia, another name for Naxos, where Bacchus embraces and aids her. Bacchus places Ariadne’s crown in the heavens as a constellation, positioned between Hercules and Ophiuchus.

FABLE III. [VIII.183-259]

Dædalus, confined in Crete and longing for his native soil, constructs wings from feathers, thread, and wax so that he and his son Icarus can escape by air. Dædalus warns Icarus to fly between the sea and the Sun, but Icarus flies too high, causing the Sun to melt his wings and sending him to his death in the sea. Dædalus buries Icarus and later witnesses a partridge, which recalls the transformation of Perdix, his talented nephew whom he had thrown from a tower out of jealousy. Minerva saves Perdix by transforming him into a partridge, a bird that thereafter avoids high places.

FABLE IV. [VIII.260-546]

Diana punishes Œneus and Calydon because he omits her from his offerings by sending a monstrous boar to ravage the land. Meleager leads a company of heroes, including Theseus and Atalanta, in the Calydonian boar hunt and kills the beast after Atalanta wounds it. When the sons of Thestius seize the boar’s head and hide from Atalanta, Meleager kills Plexippus and Toxeus. Althæa then burns the fatal billet linked to Meleager’s life, causing his death, after which Diana transforms most of his mourning sisters into birds.

The unit explains the story of the Calydonian boar chase, focusing on Œneus, Diana, Meleager, and Althæa. Œneus was said to have offended Diana by forgetting her in his sacrifices, after which a wild boar ravaged his territory and killed country people. Meleager gathered neighboring nobles to destroy the boar, and the deaths of Plexippus and Toxeus led Althæa to condemn her son and to the invention of the fatal billet story. The explanation suggests that the legend may preserve historical events while the magical and metamorphic elements are poetic fiction.

FABLE V. [VIII.547-610]

Theseus is delayed by the swollen Acheloüs river and accepts Acheloüs's invitation to rest in his grotto. Acheloüs tells Theseus how five Naiads were swept into the sea and became the Echinades, and how Neptune transformed Perimele into an island after Acheloüs pleaded for her. Lelex then recounts how Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as men, were welcomed by Philemon and Baucis when their neighbors refused them lodging. The gods destroyed the surrounding village by flooding, changed the couple's cottage into a temple, granted their wish to serve there, and transformed them into adjoining trees; Acheloüs concludes by describing Proteus's ability to assume many shapes.

EXPLANATION.

The unit tells how Erisicthon’s impiety toward Ceres leads to an insatiable hunger that consumes his wealth and ultimately causes him to devour himself. Ceres sends Famine to punish him, while his daughter Metra uses transformations granted by Neptune to escape masters and provide food for her father. Erisicthon repeatedly sells Metra after exhausting his property, and her transformations temporarily enable her to return to him. The story is explained as a moral tale about filial devotion, extravagance, and the consequences of violating sacred places.

BOOK THE NINTH.

Theseus asks Acheloüs why he is sighing and why his forehead is mutilated, and Acheloüs recounts his contest with Hercules for Deïanira. Acheloüs describes transforming into a serpent and then a bull, but Hercules overcomes him and breaks off one of his horns. The Naiads fill the broken horn with autumn fruits and flowers, making it the Horn of Plenty associated with Plenty. The explanation identifies the combat allegorically with Hercules regulating the river Acheloüs, whose altered course brought fertile land and peace between neighboring regions.

FABLE II. [IX.101-272]

Hercules returns with Deïanira and entrusts her to Nessus while crossing the swollen river Evenus, but Nessus attempts to abduct her and Hercules kills him with a poisoned arrow. Nessus gives Deïanira a tunic stained with his blood, which she later sends to Hercules after hearing of his love for Iole; the tunic causes him unbearable agony. Hercules throws Lichas into the sea, prepares a funeral pile on Œta in obedience to an oracle, and dies when Philoctetes sets it alight. Jupiter then receives Hercules into the heavens and makes him a god.

FABLE III. [IX.273-323]

Juno, seeking revenge on Alcmena for her affair with Jupiter, has Ilithyïa obstruct Alcmena’s labor and prevent the birth of Hercules. Ilithyïa sits on an altar before the door of Alcmena’s abode, joins her fingers, and uses charms to impede the delivery. Galanthis deceives Ilithyïa by announcing that Alcmena has already given birth, allowing Hercules to be born. Ilithyïa then transforms Galanthis into a weasel, which is said to bring forth its young through its mouth.

EXPLANATION.

The unit recounts the ancestry, marriage, and adventures of Amphitryon and Alcmena, including the claim that Jupiter fathered Hercules while Amphitryon fathered Iphiclus. It explains how Hercules became subject to Eurystheus and summarizes the many exploits later organized as the Twelve Labours, while offering rationalizing interpretations of monsters and supernatural episodes. It also discusses the possible historical origins, solar symbolism, and moral meaning of the Hercules mythus, drawing on several ancient and modern authorities.

FABLE IV. [IX.324-425]

Iole tells Alcmena how her sister Dryope, after plucking flowers from a lotus that was the transformed Nymph Lotis, became a tree while carrying her infant son. Dryope’s family witnesses the transformation, and she gives instructions for caring for her child and protecting her branches. During Iole’s account, Iolaüs is restored to youth by Hebe, prompting Themis to prophesy the Theban civil war and the future vengeance of Calirrhoë’s sons. The other gods then complain that similar gifts of renewed youth are not granted to others.

FABLE V. [IX.426-665]

Jupiter declares that the Fates govern both gods and mortals, and the other divinities accept his words. Byblis, daughter of Miletus and sister of Caunus, develops an incestuous love for her brother and confesses it in a letter, but Caunus rejects her. Caunus flees his homeland and founds a city, while Byblis pursues him through Caria and neighboring lands. Exhausted and consumed by grief, Byblis dissolves into tears and is transformed into a fountain.

Fable VI tells how Ligdus orders his pregnant wife Telethusa to kill the child if it is a girl, but Isis appears to Telethusa in a dream and commands her to raise the child regardless of sex. Telethusa gives birth to a daughter, names her Iphis, and raises her as a boy; Iphis later falls in love with Ianthe, who believes Iphis is male. When their marriage approaches, Isis transforms Iphis into a man. Iphis and Ianthe then marry with the approval of Venus, Juno, and Hymenæus.

BOOK THE TENTH.

Eurydice, the wife of Orpheus, dies after a serpent bites her ankle, and Orpheus descends to the Infernal Regions to recover her. Pluto, Persephone, and the Fates permit her return on the condition that Orpheus not look back before leaving, but he turns and loses her again. After mourning, Orpheus withdraws to Thrace, where his music draws animals, trees, and rocks to him. The unit also briefly explains the stories and worship associated with Cybele and Attis.

FABLE III. [X.106-142]

Cyparissus accidentally kills a stag sacred to the Nymphs, grieves so intensely that Apollo transforms him into a cypress, and the tree becomes associated with mourning. Jupiter, captivated by Ganymede, takes the form of an eagle and carries him to Heaven to serve as the gods’ cup-bearer. Apollo accidentally kills Hyacinthus with a rebounding discus, and from the youth’s blood grows the hyacinth, which bears marks of lamentation and is honored at the Hyacinthian festival.

FABLE VI. [X.220-242]

Venus punishes the Cerastæ of Cyprus for sacrificing strangers by transforming them into bulls, while the dissolute Propœtides are changed into rocks. Pygmalion, disgusted by the Propœtides, rejects marriage and carves an ivory statue of an ideal woman, with whom he falls in love. During the festival of Venus, he prays for a wife like the statue, and Venus brings it to life. Pygmalion marries her, and she bears Paphos, after whom the island is said to have been named.

FABLE VIII. [X.298-518]

Myrrha, daughter of Cinyras and Cenchris, develops an incestuous passion for her father and struggles unsuccessfully to suppress it. Her nurse prevents her attempted suicide, learns the cause of her despair, and arranges for Myrrha to enter Cinyras's bed under a false identity. When Cinyras discovers the crime, Myrrha flees while pregnant, prays to be removed from both life and death, and is transformed into a tree. The child is born from the tree, whose tears become myrrh.

The unit explains footnotes concerning places, substances, customs, and mythological references in the story of Cinyras and Myrrha. It describes Myrrha’s transformation into a tree and the production and use of myrrh. Le Clerc interprets the story as a distorted tradition in which Cinyras condemns Myrrha and Adonis after Myrrha reports Cinyras’s indecent behavior. The explanation connects Myrrha’s transformation to the Arabic meaning of her name, “Mor,” meaning myrrh.

FABLE IX. [X.519-707]

Adonis grows from a beautiful infant into a youth and attracts Venus, who warns him against hunting dangerous wild beasts. Venus tells Adonis how Atalanta, warned by an oracle that marriage would destroy her, challenges her suitors to outrun her, with death awaiting the defeated. Hippomenes wins the race with Venus's help by using three golden apples, and he and Atalanta later defile Cybele's shrine and are transformed into lions. Venus warns Adonis to avoid animals that confront hunters rather than flee from them.

The unit explains the story of Atalanta and Hippomenes, including the race in which Hippomenes won Atalanta’s favor with golden apples. It states that Venus caused Myrrha’s passion according to Apollodorus and that Venus may have supplied or plucked the apples in Cyprus. The explanation compares Ovid’s account with Apollodorus’s version, in which Atalanta is exposed as an infant, becomes a hunter, and marries Melanion after he defeats her in a race. It also says that Melanion and Atalanta were transformed into a lion and lioness after profaning Jupiter’s temple.

FABLE X. [X.708-739]

Adonis ignores Venus’s warning and is killed by a wild boar while hunting. Venus finds him dying, mourns him, and changes his blood into a crimson flower, identified as the anemone or wind-flower. The flower is short-lived because the winds beat it down. The Adonia commemorated Adonis’s death and, according to the explanation, included mourning and rejoicing at his return.

BOOK THE ELEVENTH.

Orpheus sings on Mount Rhodope until the women of Thrace, enraged during their orgies, attack and kill him. His head and lyre travel down the river Hebrus to Lesbos, where Apollo turns a serpent attacking the head into stone. Lyæus punishes the Bacchanals by transforming them into trees. After his death, Orpheus descends to the underworld, finds Eurydice, and walks with her through the fields of the blessed.

FABLE II. [XI.85-145]

Bacchus leaves Thrace after punishing the Thracian women for the murder of Orpheus, while his intoxicated tutor Silenus is brought to Midas by Phrygian peasants. Midas hospitably entertains Silenus and returns him to Bacchus, who grants Midas's request that whatever he touches become gold. The power makes food and drink unusable, so Midas repents and asks Bacchus for relief. Bacchus orders him to bathe at the source of the Pactolus, where the golden power leaves Midas and enters the river, giving it golden sands.

FABLE III. [XI.146-193]

Pan challenges Apollo to a musical contest before the mountain god Tmolus, with Nymphs and Midas present. Tmolus judges Apollo's lyre music superior to Pan's rustic reeds, and everyone agrees except Midas. Apollo punishes Midas by giving him ass's ears, which Midas hides beneath a purple turban. Midas's barber discovers the ears and secretly tells the earth, from which a grove of reeds later reveals the secret.

FABLE IV. [XI.194-220]

Apollo and Neptune assume mortal forms and build the walls of Troy for Laomedon in exchange for an agreed payment. Laomedon refuses to pay and commits perjury, so Neptune inundates Troy and demands that Laomedon's daughter Hesione be exposed to a sea monster. Hercules rescues Hesione, but Laomedon also refuses Hercules the promised horses, leading Hercules to sack Troy. Telamon, who shares in the warfare, receives Hesione as his wife.

FABLES V. AND VI. [XI.221-409]

Proteus foretells that Thetis will bear a son greater in power and valor than his father, so Jupiter yields her to Peleus. Thetis assumes several forms to resist Peleus, but he follows Proteus's advice, holds her fast, and marries her; she bears Achilles. After Peleus kills Phocus and is exiled, Ceyx receives him in Trachyn and recounts how Dædalion became a hawk after Chione's death. Psamathe sends a wolf to avenge Phocus by attacking Peleus's herds, but Thetis intercedes and the wolf is turned to stone before Peleus is exiled again.

FABLE VII. [XI.410-748]

Ceyx prepares to undertake a voyage to Claros to consult the Clarian God about his brother’s fate, despite Halcyone’s fear of the sea. He leaves by ship, and after traveling some distance encounters a violent storm that overwhelms the vessel. The ship breaks apart and sinks, and many of the crew are drowned. Ceyx clings to wreckage, repeatedly calls for Halcyone, and is finally buried beneath a breaking wave.

Halcyone, still unaware that her husband Ceyx has died in a shipwreck, prays to Juno for his safe return. Juno sends Iris to Sleep, who appoints Morpheus to appear to Halcyone in the form of dead Ceyx and reveal the truth. Halcyone discovers Ceyx’s body at the shore, mourns him, and is transformed with him into a bird. As kingfishers, they remain devoted to each other, and the sea is kept calm for seven winter days while Halcyone broods on her floating nest.

FABLE VIII. [XI.749-795]

Æsacus, son of Priam and brother of Hector, falls in love with Hesperia, daughter of Cebrenus, while she is by her father’s stream. Hesperia is bitten by a serpent while fleeing him and dies, prompting Æsacus to throw himself into the sea. Tethys transforms him into a feathered didapper, but he repeatedly dives into the sea because he still desires death. The explanation notes that Apollodorus gives a different account, making Æsacus the son of Priam and Arisbe and attributing his transformation to grief over Sterope’s death.

BOOK THE TWELFTH.

The Greeks assemble at Aulis to sail against Troy after Paris brings Helen to the Trojans, but contrary winds delay them. Calchas interprets an omen of a serpent devouring eight birds and predicts a long war, then declares that Agamemnon must appease Diana by sacrificing Iphigenia; Diana substitutes a hind and allows the fleet to depart. After the Greeks land at Troy, Protesilaüs is killed by Hector, while Achilles fights and kills the invulnerable Cygnus. Neptune transforms Cygnus into a white swan, leaving only his armor behind.

FABLES III. AND IV. [XII.146-535]

During a truce, the Grecian chiefs gather at a feast after Achilles defeats the invulnerable Cygnus. Nestor tells how Cænis, after Neptune’s assault, was transformed into Cæneus, a man who could not be wounded. At the wedding feast of Pirithoüs and Hippodame, the drunken Eurytus attacks the bride, provoking a battle between the Centaurs and Lapithæ. Cæneus survives countless blows but is finally crushed beneath piled-up tree trunks, after which Neptune changes his body into a bird.

During the Lapith–Centaur battle, Peleus kills several Centaurs, including Demoleon, Dorylas, Phæocomes, Chthonius, and Teleboas. Nestor recounts these combats and names other fighters, including Cyllarus and Hylonome, whose mutual love ends in their deaths. He then describes Cæneus resisting the attacks of the Centaurs until they bury him beneath a heap of trees. Mopsus says that Cæneus emerged from the pile as a tawny-winged bird, and the Lapiths continue fighting until many Centaurs are killed and the rest flee or are scattered by night.

EXPLANATION.

The unit explains that the Thessalians, especially those near Mount Pelion, were reputed to have pioneered riding and to have excelled in horsemanship. It interprets the Centaurs as possibly representing Thessalian or other northern horse-riding tribes, while also discussing several proposed explanations for their name and their alleged descent from Ixion and a cloud. The Centaurs’ conflict with Pirithoüs began over succession claims and was renewed when Eurytus, or Eurytion, attempted to seize Hippodamia at her wedding. After their battle with the Lapithæ, the Centaurs were driven from Thessaly toward Arcadia, Malea, and, according to some accounts, Italy.

FABLES V. AND VI. [XII.536-628]

Periclymenus, brother of Nestor, transforms into an eagle while fighting Hercules, but Hercules wounds him with an arrow and kills him. Neptune mourns Periclymenus and asks Apollo to avenge him by secretly killing Achilles. Apollo directs Paris's arrow into Achilles, whose body is burned after his death, while his glory survives. Agamemnon then transfers the contest for Achilles' armor to the assembled Argive leaders, with Ajax, the son of Telamon, and Ulysses as the principal hopeful claimants.

BOOK THE THIRTEENTH.

After Achilles’ death, Ajax and Ulysses contend before the Greek chiefs for Achilles’ armour. Ajax argues that his strength, lineage, and defense of the Grecian fleet make him more deserving than Ulysses, whom he accuses of cowardice, deceit, and abandoning wounded comrades. He recounts defeating Hector and protecting Diomedes, and challenges Ulysses to retrieve the arms from the enemy. The contest ultimately results in Ulysses receiving the armour, after which Ajax kills himself and his blood is changed into a flower; Ulysses later brings Philoctetes to the siege of Troy, and Troy falls.

Ulysses argues before the Greek chiefs that he deserves Achilles’ arms because his counsel and daring enabled many Greek successes in the Trojan War. He recounts discovering Achilles in disguise, recovering Philoctetes, killing Dolon and Rhesus, carrying Achilles’ body, entering Troy to seize the statue of Minerva, and performing other services. Ajax is portrayed as strong but lacking intelligence, while Ulysses claims that wisdom has made Troy capable of being captured. The chiefs award the arms to Ulysses, and Ajax, overcome by anger and grief, kills himself with his sword; from his blood a purple flower grows.

After Ulysses recovered the arrows of Hercules with their owner’s help, the Greeks finally completed the long Trojan War and Troy fell. Priam was killed at Jupiter’s altar, Hecuba was carried away after mourning Hector’s ashes, Cassandra was dragged away, and Astyanax was hurled from the towers. The text then recounts how Polydorus, entrusted to Polymnestor in Thrace, was murdered for the sake of his riches. The explanation also discusses the rivalry of Ajax Telamon and Ulysses over the armour of Achilles and the related Palladium dispute.

FABLES III. AND IV. [XIII.439-622]

After the Greeks moor in Thrace, Achilles' ghost demands that Polyxena be sacrificed at his tomb, and Neoptolemus kills her despite her courage and pleas for modest treatment. Hecuba, grieving over Polyxena, discovers the murdered body of Polydorus, takes revenge on Polymnestor by blinding him, and is transformed into a bitch. Aurora mourns her son Memnon, whom Achilles killed, and Jupiter transforms the ashes from Memnon's funeral pyre into the fighting birds called Memnonides.

FABLES V. AND VI. [XIII.623-718]

After the fall of Troy, Æneas travels with Anchises and Ascanius to Delos, where Anius receives them and recounts how his daughters were transformed into doves by Bacchus. Apollo’s oracle directs the Trojans to seek the ancient mother and kindred shores, and Anius gives them gifts before their departure. The account also describes the daughters of Orion, who sacrifice themselves to save Thebes from a plague, after which two youths arise from their ashes and are called Coronae. Æneas’s voyage then passes Ambracia and Dodona, whose traditions include divine disputes, transformations, and an ancient oracle.

FABLE VII. [XIII.719-897]

Galatea tells the Nereid Scylla how Polyphemus, a Cyclops, loved her while she loved Acis, the son of Faunus and the Nymph Symæthis. Polyphemus declares his love, boasts of his strength and possessions, and threatens Acis after seeing the two together. He hurls a fragment of a mountain at Acis and crushes him, after which Galatea transforms Acis into a river. The resulting stream retains the name Acis, and the explanation connects that name with the rapidity of its course.

FABLE VIII. [XIII.898-968]

Glaucus tells Scylla how he became a sea God after eating grass that revived the fish he had caught. The grass caused his body and mind to change, and the sea Gods, Oceanus, and Tethys completed his transformation through purification and immersion in many waters. Glaucus declares his love for Scylla, but she flees from him, leaving him enraged. He then goes to the court of Circe, the daughter of Titan.

BOOK THE FOURTEENTH.

Glaucus asks Circe to cure his love for Scylla or make Scylla share his passion, but Circe instead poisons Scylla’s bathing place and transforms her into a monster with barking dogs. Glaucus rejects Circe, while Scylla later attacks Ulysses’s companions and is transformed into a rock. Æneas is received by Dido, who falls in love with him and later kills herself after he abandons her. Jupiter transforms the perjurious Cercopes into hairy, ape-like creatures and sends them to the islands called Pithecusæ.

FABLE III. [XIV.101-153]

Aeneas visits the Cumæan Sibyl at Cumæ and asks her to guide him through Avernus to the shade of his father, Anchises. The Sibyl explains that Apollo offered her eternal life and youth, but she asked only for as many years as grains of sand and therefore grows old and decrepit. The explanation discusses the different Sibyls, the Sibylline verses, the Roman Sibylline books, and their later association with Christianity. It also states that Ovid represents the various Sibyls as one person whose long life was granted by Apollo.

FABLE IV. [XIV.154-247]

Æneas arrives at Caieta, where he encounters Macareus, who recognizes Achæmenides aboard the Trojan ship. Achæmenides recounts how he survived Polyphemus after Ulysses and the other Greeks escaped, and how Æneas rescued him. Macareus then describes Ulysses’ voyage with the winds confined in a leather bag, the sailors’ release of them, and the destruction caused by the Læstrygons. He also tells how Circe transformed his companions into swine until Ulysses used moly and compelled Circe to restore them.

FABLE VI. [XIV.320-440]

Picus, a king of Ausonia devoted to his wife Canens, rejects the love of Circe, the daughter of the Sun. Circe transforms him into a purple-winged woodpecker and changes his attendants into various wild beasts. Canens searches for Picus for six days and nights, then grieves beside the Tiber until she pines away into air. The place where she disappears is afterward called Canens.

FABLES VII. AND VIII. [XIV.441-526]

Turnus seeks aid from Diomedes against Aeneas, but Diomedes refuses because he fears Venus and recounts how Venus transformed many of his companions into birds. An Apulian shepherd insults dancing Nymphs and is transformed into a wild olive tree. Turnus burns Aeneas’s fleet, but Cybele changes the ships into sea Nymphs that aid ships except those carrying Greeks. After Turnus and Ardea fall, Venus obtains Jupiter’s consent to deify Aeneas, who is washed by Numicius and made a god called Indiges.

FABLE XI. [XIV.609-697]

Pomona, a Hamadryad of Latium, devotes herself to tending fruit trees and avoids love and the approach of males. Vertumnus loves her, but repeated attempts in various forms do not win her favor. Disguised as an old woman, he praises her gardens and urges her to choose Vertumnus in marriage, claiming that he loves only her and shares her tastes. He then begins to recount examples intended to persuade her to relent.

FABLES XII. AND XIII. [XIV.698-851]

Vertumnus tells Pomona how Anaxarete's cruelty drove Iphis to hang himself and how she was transformed into stone when she viewed his funeral. The unit then recounts the founding of Rome, the Sabine attack, Tarpeia's death, and the intervention of Juno, Venus, and the Naiads of Ausonia. After the Roman-Sabine war, Romulus and Tatius share sovereignty and give laws to both peoples. Mars carries Romulus to heaven as Quirinus, and Iris leads Hersilia to him, after which she becomes the goddess Ora.

BOOK THE FIFTEENTH.

Myscelos, an Argive favored by the Gods, is twice warned in a dream by Hercules to leave Argos and settle near the river Æsar in Italy. Although Argive law forbids him to leave without permission and he is brought to trial, Hercules causes the black pebbles of condemnation to turn white, and Myscelos is acquitted. He sails to Italy, finds the place where Croton is buried, and founds the city of Crotona there. The account is told to Numa, who is seeking knowledge of the origins of the city associated with Hercules.

FABLES II. AND III. [XV.60-478]

Pythagoras, a Samian exile who opposed tyranny, teaches people about the universe, nature, the gods, and hidden causes, while Numa Pompilius is drawn to hear his discourses. Pythagoras urges mortals to reject animal food and animal sacrifice, arguing that the earth provides abundant nourishment without slaughter and that souls pass between human and animal bodies. He teaches that souls, matter, time, and all natural forms continually change without wholly perishing. He also explains the transformations of the four elements and gives examples of changing lands, waters, rivers, islands, hills, and volcanoes.

Pythagoras teaches that bodies and living things continually change, citing examples such as insects arising from decaying animals, frogs developing from mud, and the Phoenix renewing itself. He describes the rise and fall of nations and cities, especially Troy's destruction and Rome's promised future greatness through Aeneas's descendants. He also teaches that human souls can pass into other bodies, including animals, and therefore urges people to avoid eating animal flesh. The explanation connects these teachings with Pythagorean philosophy, especially metempsychosis and the idea that nothing perishes but everything changes.

FABLES IV. V. AND VI. [XV.479-621]

Numa returned to his country, became king of the Roman people, and taught them sacrificial rites and peaceful arts before dying after a long life. His wife Egeria mourned him so intensely in the groves of Aricia that Diana transformed her into a flowing fountain. Hippolytus recounts how a sea-monster frightened his horses, causing his death, after which Apollo's son restored him and Diana renamed him Virbius. Tages arose from a clod of earth, Romulus's lance became a cornel-tree, and Cippus discovered horns that foretold kingship but chose exile rather than threaten Rome's liberty.

EXPLANATION.

The unit discusses Ovid’s anachronistic claim that Numa Pompilius studied under Pythagoras, along with Numa’s alleged consultations with the Nymph Egeria and the Roman use of divination. It recounts competing versions of the story of Theseus, Phædra, and Hippolytus, including Hippolytus’s death, divine honors, and supposed restoration to life. It also describes Romulus’s miraculous spear-tree, the Etruscan origins of divination, and the prodigious story of Genucius Cippus. Throughout, the explanations distinguish traditional legends from more plausible historical or natural interpretations.

FABLE VII. [XV.622-744]

Rome is devastated by a pestilence, and its Senate consults Apollo at Delphi for relief. Apollo directs the Romans to bring his son Æsculapius from Epidaurus, where the god appears in a dream and agrees to go with them in the form of a serpent. The serpent travels by ship to Rome and chooses the Island of the Tiber as its dwelling. After Æsculapius arrives, the pestilence ends, and a temple is built in his honor.

FABLE VIII. [XV.745-879]

Julius Cæsar is assassinated in the Senate-house, but Venus carries his soul to heaven, where it becomes a star. Augustus is praised as Cæsar’s greater successor, destined to conquer widely, establish peace, regulate laws, and rule until old age. The Gods and nature provide omens of the impending murder, though they cannot prevent Fate. The Poet concludes The Metamorphoses by praising Augustus and claiming that his poetry will preserve his name forever.
