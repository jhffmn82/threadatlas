Phocus

person · 5 works · 21 quoted facts · 10 connections
Also called: Nereian youth, O son of Æacus, Phocus of the family of Ornytion, Phocus the son of Ornytion, Son of a Goddess, brother, departed Phocus, him, the stripling Phocus

Abstract written by a model

In The Library, Phocus, son of Aeacus and Psamathe, excels in athletic contests before Peleus and Telamon plot against him and Telamon kills him with a quoit. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Phocus, Aeacus’s son and a Nereian youth, receives Cephalus and the sons of Pallas, notices Cephalus’s javelin, and listens to Cephalus’s account of it. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Phocus is killed by Peleus, causing Peleus’s banishment from his native home and prompting Psamathe to send a wolf against Peleus’s herds. In Description of Greece, Phocus, son of Thetis and father of Panopeus and Crisus, is killed by Telamon and Peleus with a quoit during a pentathlon, after which they flee Ægina and his tomb stands near the Hall of Æacus. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Phocus is described as Ornytion’s son and Sisyphus’s grandson who finds, cures, marries, and is buried with Antiope, and as Aeacus’s son who crosses to Phocis, gains sovereignty, receives Iaseus’s signet-ring, returns to Ægina, and dies through Peleus’s contrivance.

Phocus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Phocus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Phocus is described both as Ornytion’s son and Sisyphus’s grandson, who finds, cures, marries, and is buried with Antiope, and as Aeacus’s son. In the latter account he crosses from Ægina to Phocis, gains sovereignty, receives Iaseus’s gold-set stone signet-ring, returns to Ægina, and dies through Peleus’s contrivance.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVII.

Phocus, the son of Ornytion and grandson of Sisyphus, found Antiope while she was wandering in madness. He cured and married her, and he was buried with her.

CHAPTER XXX.

Phocus, the son of Æacus, crossed from Ægina to the mainland region later called Phocis, obtained sovereignty there, and intended to live there. Iaseus was his friend and gave him various presents, including a gold-set stone signet-ring; when Phocus later sailed back to Ægina, Peleus contrived his death. Polygnotus depicted Phocus as a stripling allowing Iaseus to take the ring from his left hand.
