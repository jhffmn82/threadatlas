Abstract written by a model

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, the horses are the fiery, winged team drawing Apollo’s chariot, who run wildly after Phaëton’s death before being subdued and reproached by Phœbus. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, the horses of the Sun are the fiery steeds drawing the Sun’s chariot, who leave the proper road and run uncontrolled after Phaeton drops the reins.

the horses (Phaëton's chariot) in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where the horses (Phaëton's chariot) first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sun horses are a fiery, winged team of four, Pyroeis, Eoüs, Æthon, and Phlegon, drawing Apollo’s chariot and fed on ambrosia. Hard to restrain, they sense unusual lightness, abandon their regular track, and run in fury. After Phaëton’s death, terrified and maddened, they are subdued and reproached by Phœbus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK THE SECOND.

The Sun horses, Pyroeis, Eoüs, Æthon, and Phlegon, are fiery, winged steeds that are difficult to restrain. After sensing the chariot’s unusual lightness, they leave the regular track and run wherever their fury carries them.

opening

The flame-footed steeds are maddened and still trembling with terror after Phaëton’s death. Phœbus subdues them with whip and lash, angrily reproaches them, and blames them for Phaëton’s death.

the horses of the Sun in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where the horses of the Sun first appears

Abstract written by a model

Fiery steeds fed ambrosia draw the Sun's chariot; sensing Phaeton's lighter load, they leave the proper road and run uncontrolled after he drops the reins.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

LATONA AND THE RUSTICS

The horses of the Sun were fiery steeds fed with ambrosia and accustomed to drawing the chariot. They recognized that Phaeton's load was lighter than usual, broke from the proper road, and ran uncontrolled when he dropped the reins.
