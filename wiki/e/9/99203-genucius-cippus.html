Genucius Cippus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Genucius Cippus is a Roman figure associated with a prodigy: horn-like growths, or horns, appeared on his forehead after he returned victorious from conquest. An Etrurian soothsayer or augurs interpreted the omen to mean that, if he entered or reentered Rome, he would become king. Rejecting kingship and fearing Rome’s loss of liberty, he summoned the people and Senate and chose voluntary exile or banishment. Rome rewarded his patriotism with land, said to be as much as he could encircle with yoked oxen in a day, and with a brazen statue. In Ovid’s version, he returns after assisting Consul Valerius.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLES IV. V. AND VI. [XV.479-621]

Cippus saw horns reflected on his forehead after returning victorious from conquest and confirmed that they were real. An Etrurian soothsayer predicted that he would become king if he entered the city, but Cippus rejected the prediction, summoned the people and Senate, and chose exile rather than rule Rome.

EXPLANATION.

Genucius Cippus was said to have developed horn-like growths on his forehead and to have learned from the augurs that he would become king if he reentered Rome. He chose exile rather than risk enslaving his country, and the Romans reportedly honored his patriotism with a brazen statue and lands. The explanation suggests that the story may have originated in a dream or in a natural bodily excrescence later magnified by superstition.
