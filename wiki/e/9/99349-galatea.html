Galatea

mythological figure · 2 works · 5 quoted facts · 1 connection
Also called: Pygmalion's ivory statue, fictitious body, image, ivory maid, maiden, statue, the counterfeit creation, the object of his hopes, the statue, the virgin

Abstract written by a model

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Pygmalion's ivory statue is carved as an ideal virgin, adorned and loved as a companion, and transformed by Venus into a living woman. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Galatea begins as an ivory statue, becomes a living woman through Venus's favor, awakens to learn love and life, and marries Pygmalion.

FABLE VI. [X.220-242]

Pygmalion carves the statue from snow-white ivory and gives it the beauty of an ideal virgin. He dresses it, adorns it with jewels, treats it as his companion, and falls in love with it; Venus later makes it a living woman.

Galatea in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Abstract written by a model

Galatea begins as an ivory statue, becomes a living woman through Venus's favor, awakens in her niche to learn love and life, and marries Pygmalion in Venus-blessed union.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter V

Galatea is the living statue in William Morris's version of the Pygmalion story. She describes opening her eyes in the niche where she had stood and hearing a woman tell her to come down, learn to love, and be alive.
