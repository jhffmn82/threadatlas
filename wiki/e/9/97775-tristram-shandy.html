Tristram Shandy in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

Tristram Shandy is a work written by Laurence Sterne, whose first two volumes were published in York. It is characterized as a whimsicality touched with sentiment, distinct from the Sentimental Journey, and by essentially British humor that was difficult to transplant. It became more popular than Sterne’s sermons in Germany and elsewhere and was a major source of Shandean narration: eccentric, digressive, interrupted, self-conscious, and concerned with how trivial events and parental peculiarities shape lives. The records chiefly trace its German reception and influence. Before 1768 it reached Germany through a weak, inaccurate 1763 translation, notices, and limited literary circles. Reviewers found it original or strange but often misunderstood its humor, structure, authorship, popularity, and even the authenticity of translated parts. Herder, Hamann, Riedel, Wieland, and Swiss readers knew it, yet its British eccentricity initially prevented broad success. After the Sentimental Journey appeared, German readers returned to its previously neglected Maria passage, whose sentiment had been concealed amid Shandean quibbles. Tristram Shandy regained attention; Johann Joachim Christoph Bode’s translation, completed in 1774, was widely praised and later revised or reworked. Critics used it as a standard for judging other genuine or purported Sterne works, while Friedrich Nicolai treated it as proof that Sterne transformed borrowed material through observation, thought, feeling, and original treatment. Its influence then spreads through German literary life. It supplied characters and motifs, was read aloud by Johann Wolfgang Goethe, and shaped domestic novels through its narrative manner. Schummel adapted its parental material, interruptions, digressions, delayed dedication, and eccentric structure, though later condemned his own Tristram-like autobiography as incoherent. Die Geschichte meines Vaters, Tobias Knaut, and Der Empfindsame drew on its hobby-horse idea, original characters, ab ovo openings, readerly conversation, typographical eccentricity, mock-pedantry, family scenes, christening discussion, and commentary on book-making. By the end, it stands as the principal model for whimsical, original narrative techniques rather than the sentimental manner associated with the Sentimental Journey.

] CHAPTER III THE PUBLICATION OF THE SENTIMENTAL JOURNEY On February 27, 1768, the Sentimental Journey was published in London,[1] less than three weeks before the author’s death, and the book was at once transplanted to German soil, beginning there immediately its career of commanding influence and wide-spread popularity.

Tristram Shandy contained a passage concerning Maria that was virtually part of the Sentimental Journey, but its sentiment was concealed among Shandean quibbles. The publication of the Sentimental Journey led German readers to turn back to this previously forgotten, neglected, or unknown passage.

] CHAPTER V STERNE’S INFLUENCE IN GERMANY Thus in manifold ways Sterne was introduced into German life and letters.

Tristram Shandy contributed characters and motifs to German sentimental and eccentric culture, including Uncle Toby and Corporal Trim in the imagined cemetery at Marienwerder. Its Shandean style of narration was adopted in German novels dealing with middle-class domestic life, and Wieland later discussed a failed novel written in its style.
