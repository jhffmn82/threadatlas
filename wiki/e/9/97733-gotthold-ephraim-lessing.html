Gotthold Ephraim Lessing in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

Gotthold Ephraim Lessing is presented through his sustained admiration for Laurence Sterne and his engagement with Sterne’s writing and reception in Germany. Although the date of his first reading of Tristram Shandy is uncertain, he admired Sterne with remarkable devotion, appreciated him unequivocally, and was later counted among clear-minded critics who recognized Sterne’s importance. After The Sentimental Journey appeared in 1768, Lessing reportedly wished to surrender part of his own life to extend Sterne’s literary activity. He read Bode’s translation in full, approved both the work selected and its translation, and helped shape the rendering of “sentimental” as “empfindsam,” though he is not proven to have proposed the translation. His Sternean engagement also appears in references to Yorick and “Steckenpferd” in letters to Mendelssohn, and in a sermon composed in Yorick’s manner as a trick involving Alberti and Goeze. Lessing owned Burton’s Anatomy of Melancholy and marked passages later cited as evidence of Sterne’s borrowings, yet remained silent about those borrowings, apparently judging them unworthy of mention. No later abandonment of his appreciation is recorded.

Gotthold Ephraim Lessing expressed intense admiration for Laurence Sterne and reportedly wished he could give part of his own life to prolong Sterne’s literary activity. He read through Bode’s translation, helped shape the rendering of “sentimental” as “empfindsam,” and approved Bode’s choice of work, though the text provides no proof that he suggested the translation.

Sterne’s unacknowledged borrowings, his high-handed and extensive appropriation

Gotthold Ephraim Lessing possessed a copy of Burton’s Anatomy of Melancholy containing marks beside several passages that Ferriar later cited as evidence of Sterne’s theft. Although Lessing evidently recognized the borrowings, he remained silent about them.
