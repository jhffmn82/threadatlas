Sterne’s influence in Germany in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

Sterne’s influence in Germany is a cultural and literary influence, treated as a particular case of broader English influence on German literature and examined around the publication of The Sentimental Journey. Although it followed Sterne’s English fame, it became substantial in Germany, producing translations, reviews, imitators, and a German Sterne cult; the records say Yorick’s works retained especially serious consideration and recognized merit there. Early influence is disputed: possible connections with Lessing are mostly qualified, though Corporal Trim may have informed Just and Alberti’s lost Yorick sermon deliberately used Sterne’s manner. The influence then spread widely through German letters and life. It promoted sentimental rituals, gentleness and reconciliation, sympathy toward animals, and practical changes in human relationships. It reshaped travel writing toward personal, subjective, volatile, humorous narratives. Writers borrowed episodes, motifs, character types, digressions, catalogues, self-conscious narration, hobby-horsical characterization, Yorick’s behavior, and the sentimental-journey form. Schummel, Bock, Kotzebue, Göschen, and Hedemann adapted these features, while Goethe’s admiration and possible connections resist firm measurement and direct imitation is rarely demonstrable. Over time, earnest sentimental imitation gave way to more self-aware, whimsical, or satirical uses, often without Sterne’s subtlety. Popularity and imitation also provoked opposition to Sterne and his sentimentalism, especially criticism of imitators who misunderstood Yorick. Ultimately the influence developed independently and gradually ceased to operate as an actuating principle in German letters.

The unit considers possible Sternean influence on German literature through Lessing’s characters and writings. Sterne’s influence in Germany extended beyond literature into social conduct and sentimental rituals, including the Lorenzodosen and proposed orders devoted to gentleness and reconciliation. It also encouraged more personal and humorous travel narratives, sympathy toward animals, and imitation of Sterne’s whimsical style.

Sterne’s influence on German literature appears in direct borrowing of episodes, motifs, character types, narrative techniques, and the sentimental journey format. Schummel, Bock, Kotzebue, Göschen, and Hedemann variously adapted the Sentimental Journey, Tristram Shandy, Yorick’s behavior, hobby-horsical characterization, catalogues, digressions, and self-conscious narration. The influence changed over time from earnest sentimental imitation to more self-aware, whimsical, or satirical treatments, often with German writers losing Sterne’s subtlety.

] CHAPTER VII OPPOSITION TO STERNE AND HIS TYPE OF SENTIMENTALISM Sterne’s influence in Germany lived its own life, and gradually and imperceptibly died out of letters, as an actuating principle.

Sterne’s influence in Germany developed independently and gradually died out as an actuating principle in literature. Its decline was accompanied by criticism of imitators who misunderstood or inadequately adapted Yorick’s ways of thinking and writing.
