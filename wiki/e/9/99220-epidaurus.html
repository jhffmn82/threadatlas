Epidaurus

place · 4 works · 9 quoted facts · 7 connections
Also called: Epidauria, Epidaurian, Epidaurians, Epidaurus the Holy, Grecian elders, coasts of Epidaurus, temple of Epidaurus, ᾿Επιδαύρῳ

Abstract written by a model

In The Library, Epidaurus is the first place where Theseus killed an evildoer, Periphetes. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Epidaurus is the city where Æsculapius dwells before leaving with the Romans. In Description of Greece, Epidaurus is described as a city in Argolis associated with traditions about its ancestry and the exposure of Æsculapius, and as the site of his temple and other dedications. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Epidaurus is the site of the celebrated oracle of Aesculapius, where the sick slept in the temple seeking divine responses and recovery.

Epidaurus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Epidaurus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Epidaurus is a city in Argolis, associated with differing traditions about its ancestry and with the exposure of the infant Æsculapius, whose worship originated there. Pityreus yielded its land to Deiphontes and the Argives, after which Epidauria became especially sacred to Æsculapius. Epidaurus contains his open-air temple and dedications to Epione, Dionysus, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hera, and Cissæan Athene, as well as Melissa’s tomb.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIV.

Epidaurus in Argolis is described as the place where Æsculapius was exposed as an infant and where his worship originated. Its traditions differ concerning the ancestry of Epidaurus and Æsculapius, identifying Epidaurus variously as the son of Pelops, Argus the son of Zeus, or Apollo, and Æsculapius as the son of Apollo and Coronis. Deiphontes and the Argives took possession of Epidauria from Pityreus, and the country became especially sacred to Æsculapius.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Epidaurus contains temples and statues dedicated to Æsculapius, Epione, Dionysus, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hera, and Cissæan Athene. It was occupied by Argives under Deiphontes, who later crossed to Ægina and introduced Doric language and customs there.
