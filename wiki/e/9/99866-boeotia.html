Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Boeotia is the continental Greek region associated with a practical school of epic poetry and with Hesiod, a Boeotian of Ascra. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Boeotia is the territory centered on Thebes, serving as its guard, bulwark, and military base in conflicts with Sparta, Phocis, Athens, and Macedonia. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Boeotia contains Delium, is linked to Euboea by a mole, comes under Theban authority, and is the scene of battles with Athens and victories at Chaeronea and Delium. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Bœotia is the country where Cadmus settles after following Phœbus’s oracle, and whose name is linked to a heifer, βοῦς, or Bœotus. In Description of Greece, Bœotia is the region whose boundary with Attica lies first at the Platæan district and later at Mount Cithæron. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Bœotia is a region containing numerous towns and places, associated with the Dædala festival, mythical works, migrations, and conflicts involving Thebes and other forces.

Boeotia in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Boeotia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Boeotia is a place centered on Thebes, described as its guard and bulwark and as territory claimed and kept under the sole jurisdiction of Thebes. Agesilaus entered it with the Spartan army, but the Thebans defended difficult high ground and forced his retreat. The Thebans’ claim that its cities were tributary only to Thebes was resisted by Athenians and other Greeks, especially after Plataea was destroyed and Thespiae taken. After Leuctra, Thebes retained control, made alliances with the Phocians and Aetolians, and returned to Boeotia. It then served as the Boeotians’ base in fighting the Phocians: they raided Phocian-held towns and destroyed corn, though they were defeated on their return. Philip intended to march through it toward Attica, and Athenian and Boeotian forces met his army at Chaeronea. Later Leosthenes entered while Boeotians were at Phila, and Cassander gained their help in rebuilding Thebes.

Boeotia in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Boeotia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Boeotia is a place containing Delium and connected to Euboea by a mole across Euripus. It was brought under Theban authority with Lacedaemonian assistance, then ravaged as Myronides won all but Thebes. Boeotian forces later won at Chaeronea, restoring cities’ liberties, and defeated an Athenian invasion at Delium.

Bœotia in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Bœotia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Bœotia is a region contiguous to Attica, said to be named for Bœotus, son of Itonus and the Nymph Melanippe. It contains various towns and places, including Thebes, Lebadea, Leuctra, Teumessus, Glisas, Harma, Mycalessus, Aulis, Alalcomenæ, Anthedon, Mount Messapium, and the Euripus. Its communities joined the festival of Dædala, and its oaks near Alalcomenæ were noted for fine roots. It was a setting for the Argive host and the battle at Ismenius, for conflict involving Thebans and other Bœotians, and for Epaminondas’s return with the Thebans. Telchinians migrated there and may have built a temple of Athene Telchinia. It also contains works of Dædalus at Thebes and Lebadea.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

8 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I.

Bœotia is contiguous to Attica. The Bœotians are said to have received their name from Bœotus, son of Itonus and the Nymph Melanippe.

CHAPTER III.

Bœotia was the region whose communities joined the great festival of Dædala. The text says that the oaks near Alalcomenæ had the finest roots of all the oaks in Bœotia.

CHAPTER IX.

Bœotia was the region to which the Argive host came from the middle of the Peloponnese. The battle at Ismenius took place there.

CHAPTER XIII.

Bœotia was the region in which Leuctra lay, and the various towns of Bœotia were discussed during the peace associated with Antalcidas. Bœotian forces fought with the Thebans, though the Thespians and other Bœotians hostile to the Thebans withdrew before the battle.

CHAPTER XIV.

Epaminondas took the Thebans back into Bœotia after the Theban allies had plundered Laconia. He returned to Thebes after pursuing Iphicrates’s forces toward Athens.

CHAPTER XIX.

Bœotia is the region in which Teumessus, Glisas, Harma, Mycalessus, and Aulis are situated. The Telchinians formerly dwelt at Cyprus and migrated into Bœotia, where some of them were said to have erected the temple of Athene Telchinia.

CHAPTER XXII.

Boeotia has Mount Messapium on the left of the Euripus, with Anthedon at its foot on the sea.

CHAPTER XL.

Bœotia contains two works attributed to Dædalus: the Hercules at Thebes and the Trophonius at Lebadea.
