  • In Laurence Sterne in Germany: Laurence Sterne is presented as an English author, clergyman, literary innovator, and central source of German responses to English literature. His literary fame began immediately in England with the first volumes of Tristram Shandy in 1760, while German notice emerged more slowly through a possible 1762 mention and an unsuccessful 1763 translation. Early German readers knew him chiefly through Tristram Shandy and Yorick’s Sermons, but often only imperfectly; his British humor also limited wider initial reception. During the 1760s, however, German literati including Herder, Hamann, Riedel, Wieland, and others increasingly admired his mixture of whimsy, sentiment, and humor. The decisive turn came with Sentimental Journey, published February 27, 1768, less than three weeks before Sterne’s death on March 18, 1768. The work received immediate German enthusiasm and made him a recognized leader in German literary and emotional life. German readers idealized Yorick and attended closely to Sterne’s letters, sermons, novels, and even works wrongly attributed to him. Though criticized for unacknowledged borrowing, especially from Burton’s Anatomy of Melancholy, he was defended as an original artist whose additions, alterations, personal tone, Empfindsamkeit, and Laune transformed borrowed material. In Germany, Sterne became a figure of benignant humanity, sympathy, emotional expression, tolerance, and practical moral guidance. His example helped oppose pedantry and philistinism and influenced conduct, sentimental customs, travel writing, humor, narrative form, typography, direct address to readers, digression, mock science, and attention to trifles. He became the great progenitor and leading model of sentimentalism and influenced German literature from the later eighteenth into the early nineteenth century, including Wieland, Jacobi, Schummel, Die Tagereise, Wezel, Wegener, and others. His imitators reproduced sentimental episodes, travel structures, interruptions, lists, pseudo-scientific analyses, whimsical narration, typography, and recognizable motifs, often with diminished delicacy and subtlety. His reception eventually included parody, moral condemnation, and disputes over whether his humane writing matched his personal character. Though his influence gradually ceased to act as a living literary principle, he remained recognized in Germany as a prophet, uplifter, and leader.

Laurence Sterne in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

Laurence Sterne is presented as an English author, clergyman, literary innovator, and central source of German responses to English literature. His literary fame began immediately in England with the first volumes of Tristram Shandy in 1760, while German notice emerged more slowly through a possible 1762 mention and an unsuccessful 1763 translation. Early German readers knew him chiefly through Tristram Shandy and Yorick’s Sermons, but often only imperfectly; his British humor also limited wider initial reception. During the 1760s, however, German literati including Herder, Hamann, Riedel, Wieland, and others increasingly admired his mixture of whimsy, sentiment, and humor. The decisive turn came with Sentimental Journey, published February 27, 1768, less than three weeks before Sterne’s death on March 18, 1768. The work received immediate German enthusiasm and made him a recognized leader in German literary and emotional life. German readers idealized Yorick and attended closely to Sterne’s letters, sermons, novels, and even works wrongly attributed to him. Though criticized for unacknowledged borrowing, especially from Burton’s Anatomy of Melancholy, he was defended as an original artist whose additions, alterations, personal tone, Empfindsamkeit, and Laune transformed borrowed material. In Germany, Sterne became a figure of benignant humanity, sympathy, emotional expression, tolerance, and practical moral guidance. His example helped oppose pedantry and philistinism and influenced conduct, sentimental customs, travel writing, humor, narrative form, typography, direct address to readers, digression, mock science, and attention to trifles. He became the great progenitor and leading model of sentimentalism and influenced German literature from the later eighteenth into the early nineteenth century, including Wieland, Jacobi, Schummel, Die Tagereise, Wezel, Wegener, and others. His imitators reproduced sentimental episodes, travel structures, interruptions, lists, pseudo-scientific analyses, whimsical narration, typography, and recognizable motifs, often with diminished delicacy and subtlety. His reception eventually included parody, moral condemnation, and disputes over whether his humane writing matched his personal character. Though his influence gradually ceased to act as a living literary principle, he remained recognized in Germany as a prophet, uplifter, and leader.

Laurence Sterne was criticized for extensive, unacknowledged appropriation of material from other writers, especially Burton’s Anatomy of Melancholy. Friedrich Nicolai nevertheless argued that Sterne’s additions, alterations, personal tone, Empfindsamkeit, and Laune made him more than a brainless copyist and that investigating his sources did not depreciate him.

Sterne remained widely recognized in Germany as a prophet, uplifter, and leader, despite some criticism during the height of his popularity.
