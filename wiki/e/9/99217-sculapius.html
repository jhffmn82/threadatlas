  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Asclepius is a famed physician regarded as the founder of medicine, whom Jupiter killed with a thunderbolt after he restored the apparently dead.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Asclepius is a lord and healer of sicknesses, son of Apollo, who brings joy by healing and soothing pain.
  • In The Library, Aesculapius is taught healing and hunting by Chiron, becomes a skilled surgeon, and uses Gorgon’s blood to raise the dead.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Æsculapius is Coronis’s divine child, saved by Apollo, taught by Chiron, and destined to restore life and become a god.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Æsculapius is a saving and health-restoring god who travels as a serpent to Rome and ends a pestilence.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Æsculapius is honored through temples, sacred enclosures, and many statues, with traditions recounting his divine origins, childhood, and worship at numerous sites.
  • In Description of Greece, Æsculapius is a healing deity associated especially with Epidaurus, renowned for curing sickness and raising the dead, whose worship spread widely and whose sacred animals and images are described.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Aesculapius is a renowned physician and healer who could restore the dead, was killed by Jupiter, and was worshipped at Rome in serpent form.

Æsculapius in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Æsculapius is Coronis’s divine child, saved by Apollo from her womb and the flames after Apollo kills her, then carried to Chiron’s cave. Some writers say he came from a crow’s egg and was born and widely worshipped as a serpent. Foretold to become a giver of health to the whole world, he restores life to mortal bodies, though this power brings his grandsire’s bolts against him. He becomes a lifeless carcase, then a God again, renewing destiny twice. He is the tutelary divinity of Epidaurus.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Coronis’s child is still in her womb when Apollo kills her. Apollo snatches the child from the flames and from the womb of its mother and carries it to the cave of the two-formed Chiron.

notes .. notes

Æsculapius is identified as the son of Coronis. Lucian and other writers fabled that he was produced from the egg of the crow and born in the shape of a serpent, under which form he was very generally worshipped.

opening

Æsculapius is an infant of divine origin whom Ocyrrhoe foretells will become the giver of health to the whole world. He will restore life to the dead, be hindered by the bolts of his grandsire after doing so against the will of the Gods, and become a God again after becoming a lifeless carcase.

Æsculapius, son of Apollo and Coronis, is a god of saving and health-restoring who protects Epidaurus. Sought by Romans during pestilence, he promises aid in a dream, travels as a serpent to Rome, and ends the disease on the Island of the Tiber.

Æsculapius in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Æsculapius is a deity of healing, worshipped from the beginning and especially associated with Epidaurus. According to the Epidaurian tradition, he was born there to Apollo and Coronis, though Arsinoe is also named as his mother and other accounts differ. Exposed on Mount Myrgion, he was fed by a she-goat and guarded by a watch-dog; he became renowned for healing sickness and raising the dead. His worship spread from Epidaurus to Athens, Pergamum, Smyrna, Cyrene, Crete, and many other places. Temples, shrines, altars, and statues mark this spread, including sites at Sicyon, Titane, Argos, Booneta, and Epidaurus Limera. Dragons, particularly tawny tame ones, were sacred to him: at Sicyon he was said to have come from Epidaurus as a dragon, while at Epidaurus Limera altars stood where a sacred dragon disappeared. His images varied, including a beardless gold-and-ivory image, a veiled statue, and a seated white-stone statue beside Hygiea.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

Sicyon possessed a temple of Æsculapius with a gold-and-ivory beardless image by Calamis. The Sicyonians said that the god came from Epidaurus in the form of a dragon and that Aratus was his son through Aristodama.

CHAPTER XI.

Æsculapius was worshipped at Titane in a temple built by Alexanor. His statue’s material and maker were unknown, though some attributed it to Alexanor; only its face, fingers, and toes were visible beneath a white woollen tunic and cloak. A stone statue described as Gortynian Æsculapius also stood in the porch.

CHAPTER XXIII.

A shrine of Æsculapius lies near the tomb of Eriphyle on the Hollow Way. The most famous temple of Æsculapius at Argos contains a white-stone statue of Æsculapius seated beside a statue of Hygiea.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Æsculapius is said to have been born to Apollo and Coronis, the daughter of Phlegyas, at Epidaurus, though other traditions make his mother Arsinoe or describe Coronis as unfaithful to Apollo. He was exposed on Mount Myrgion, where a she-goat fed him and a watch-dog guarded him, and he became renowned for healing sickness and raising the dead. He was worshipped as a god from the beginning, and his worship spread from Epidaurus to Athens, Pergamum, Smyrna, Cyrene, Crete, and elsewhere.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Æsculapius was worshipped at Epidaurus Limera, where the founding Epidaurians established altars at the place where their sacred dragon disappeared. He has a stone statue at Epidaurus Limera and temples or statues at Cyphanta, Brasiæ, Las, Hypsi, Leuctra, and other places.

Æsculapius in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Æsculapius is a deity honored through temples, sacred enclosures, and many statues. Accounts differ: the Phœnician tradition makes him Apollo’s son, while a Sidonian identifies him with health-giving air rather than a mortal woman’s child; at Titane his statue was called Health. As a boy, he was said to have been exposed at Thelpusa, found and raised by Autolaus, and nursed by Trygon, whose tomb stood in his temple. Child-form temples and statues preserve this story. His worship extended through sites including Patræ, Ægium, Tithorea, Thelpusa, and near Mysæum, with works attributed to Alcamenes, Parian Scopas, Timocles, and Timarchides.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIII.

Æsculapius had a sacred enclosure and statues at Ægium. A Sidonian said that Æsculapius was the health-giving air rather than the child of a mortal woman, while the narrator noted that a statue of Æsculapius at Titane was called Health.

CHAPTER XXV.

Æsculapius has a temple at Thelpusa, as well as a temple of the Boy Æsculapius near the Ladon. The boy was said to have been exposed at Thelpusa, found by Autolaus, the bastard son of Arcas, and brought up by him; the latter story is given as the reason for the temple. The temple also contains the tomb of Trygon, said to have been Æsculapius’s nurse.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Asclepius had a temple and statues together with Hygiea. Another temple under the hill contained a statue of Asclepius as a boy, standing erect and about a cubit in height.

Aesculapius in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Aesculapius is a renowned physician and healer capable of restoring the dead, whose art Machaon inherited. He had numerous oracles, especially at Epidaurus, and sacred serpents; after Jupiter killed him at Pluto’s request, his worship reached Rome in serpent form.

MYTH OF OSIRIS AND ISIS

Aesculapius had numerous oracles, the most celebrated being at Epidaurus. Serpents were sacred to him, and when his worship was introduced into Rome during a time of sickness, he was said to accompany an embassy's returning ship in the form of a serpent.
