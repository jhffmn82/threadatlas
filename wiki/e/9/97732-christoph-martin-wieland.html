Christoph Martin Wieland

person · 1 work · 12 quoted facts · 4 connections
Also called: Hofraths Wielands, Müller, Wieland, Wieland’s

Abstract written by a model

Christoph Martin Wieland is presented as an important German literary figure, writer, reviewer, translator-associated figure, and reader of Laurence Sterne. He began Agathon in 1761 and published Don Silvio von Rosalva in 1764. Although his works and translations reflected Richardson and Fielding, he apparently did not know The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman before autumn 1767. On encountering Sterne, he responded with immediate, extravagant admiration, regretted the poor German translation, considered translating Shandy himself, and may instead have contemplated philosophical commentary on it. From 1768 to 1774, Sterne’s style and motifs demonstrably influenced works including Endymions Traum and Chloe; in 1772 he was criticized for imitating Yorick in Diogenes. Yet he is characterized as an intelligent interpreter rather than a slavish imitator, using Sterne’s inspiration to combat false ideals and life-lies. He later opposed foolish, unsuccessful, and vulgar Sterne imitation, regarded Sterne’s influence as baneful in reviewing Tobias Knaut in Merkur, and continued to refer to Sterne and Yorick. His esteem endured: he called Sterne the author from whom he would last part. In 1773/1774, he indignantly repudiated Zückert’s unauthorized Tristram Shandy translation that used his name.

Christoph Martin Wieland was an important German literary figure whose works and translations reflected the influence of Richardson and Fielding. In 1773, Lange used Wieland’s name on a purported new translation of Tristram Shandy without authorization, prompting Wieland to express indignation and repudiate any connection with it.

Wieland apparently did not know Tristram Shandy before autumn 1767, but he responded to Sterne with immediate and extravagant admiration. He regretted the poor German translation, considered translating Shandy himself, and was later reported to have abandoned that intention, possibly while contemplating essays serving as a philosophical commentary on the novel.

] CHAPTER V STERNE’S INFLUENCE IN GERMANY Thus in manifold ways Sterne was introduced into German life and letters.

Wieland was one of Sterne’s most ardent admirers and intelligent interpreters, and Sterne’s style and motifs appear in several of Wieland’s works from 1768 to 1774. Wieland later continued to call Sterne the author from whom he would last part, while protesting against foolish and unsuccessful imitation of Sterne.

Christoph Martin Wieland reviewed Tobias Knaut in his Merkur and, like Kurz, regarded Sterne’s influence as baneful. His Merkur also contains later references to Sterne and Yorick and condemns Wegener’s vulgar imitation.

] CHAPTER VII OPPOSITION TO STERNE AND HIS TYPE OF SENTIMENTALISM Sterne’s influence in Germany lived its own life, and gradually and imperceptibly died out of letters, as an actuating principle.

Christoph Martin Wieland was criticized in 1772 for imitating Yorick in Diogenes. The review treated Wieland’s works as likely to outlast Sterne’s, but another reviewer called this compliment dubious and insulting, especially because of Wieland’s personal esteem for Sterne.
