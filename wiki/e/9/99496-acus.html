Æacus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Æacus is a king of Œnopia and Ægina, grandson of Asopus, son of Ægina and Jupiter, and father of Telamon, Peleus, and Phocus. He rules a kingdom of a hundred cities whose numerous inhabitants are devastated by a pestilence. Allied with Athens, he refuses Minos’s request, welcomes Cephalus, and grants Athens military assistance. Grieving for his lost people, he asks Jupiter either to restore them or bury him with them. After seeing ants, he requests citizens as numerous as they are; when they become men, he recognizes them, gives them the empty city and lands, and names them Myrmidons. Another account says he brought industrious people from Thessaly to repopulate the island.

opening

Æacus, king of Œnopia and father of Telamon, Peleus, and Phocus, refuses Minos’s request because of his alliance with Athens. He welcomes Cephalus, grants Athens the military assistance it requests, and recounts how a pestilence destroyed most of his people.

notes .. notes

Æacus is identified as the son of Jupiter and Ægina and as the father of Telamon, Peleus, and Phocus. He is associated with the country and temple described in the notes, and he appears in the account connected with the plague at Ægina.

opening

Æacus begs Jupiter either to restore his people or to bury him with them. After seeing the ants, he asks for citizens as numerous as they are; when they become men, he recognizes them, offers vows to Jupiter, gives them the empty city and lands, and names them Myrmidons.

notes .. notes

Æacus nearly lost hope of seeing his subjects again while they remained hidden in woods and caverns. In another account, he brought the Myrmidons from Thessaly to repopulate his island after a pestilence.

Æacus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Æacus is remembered as Ægina’s only early king: he built civic and sacred works, aided Greek envoys during drought by appealing to Pan-Hellenian Zeus, sought settlers and defenses for the island, and later judged Telamon over Phocus’s death.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIX.

Æacus was the only king known to have ruled Ægina in the early tradition, and he asked Zeus to provide settlers for the island. He was credited with making Ægina difficult for enemies to approach, erected the temple of Pan-Hellenian Zeus, and refused to let Telamon land while judging him for Phocus’s death.
