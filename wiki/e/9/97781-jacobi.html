Jacobi

Also called: Johann Georg Jacobi

Abstract written by a model

Johann Georg Jacobi is a German author, Fritz Jacobi’s brother, and one of the best-known, typical German disciples and imitators of Laurence Sterne. After reading Sterne’s account of Franciscan Lorenzo with his brother and several ladies, he introduced the Lorenzodosen as a practical sentimental custom. He explained it in an open letter to Gleim published in the Hamburgischer Correspondent, promoted it among friends, and later observed that merchants had made it a commercial speculation; the conceit also linked him to Yorick. In Gleim’s circle he was called Uncle Toby. His Winterreise and Sommerreise, the latter published in late September 1769, imitate Sterne’s sentimental travel writing, with Winterreise also externally influenced by Chapelle. Jacobi exaggerates Yorick’s sentimentalism but largely avoids Sterne’s whimsical side, adding exuberant fancy, mythological figures, serious advocacy of immortality, sympathy for animals, and humane religious characters. His youth works and indebtedness to Sterne became subjects of study. His success and Winterreise helped influence Johann Christian Bock’s Die Tagereise, especially its verse within prose travel narrative, and he remained associated with the sentimental fashion and Lorenzodosen.

Johann Georg Jacobi is identified as one of the best-known German disciples of Sterne. His Winterreise and Sommerreise imitate Sterne’s sentimental travel writing, though Jacobi adds more exuberant fancy, mythological figures, serious instruction, sympathy for animals, and humane religious characters while largely avoiding Sterne’s whimsical side.

Jacobi influenced Johann Christian Bock’s Die Tagereise alongside Sterne. Bock’s work resembles Jacobi’s Winterreise in its outward form, particularly in the use of verse within a prose travel narrative. Bock’s later anonymous sentimental travel books also appeared in the context of Jacobi’s success.

Jacobi is associated with the sentimental fashion and with the arbor mentioned in connection with Die Geschichte meiner Reise nach Pirmont. Göchhausen receives a Lorenzodose from J . . . and refers to Jacobi’s accompanying words; the traveler’s insistence that Pumper ride inside the coach may derive from Jacobi’s failure to be equally democratic.
