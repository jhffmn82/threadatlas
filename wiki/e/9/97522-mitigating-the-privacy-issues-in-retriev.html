Mitigating the Privacy Issues in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) via Pure Synthetic Data

Abstract written by a model

This document presents SAGE, a two-stage method for using synthetic data as retrieval context in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. RAG combines retrieved material with a user query to improve large language model accuracy, relevance, and hallucination reduction, but retrieving private source data can expose sensitive information through leakage and extraction attacks. The central goal is therefore to retain the useful contextual content of original retrieval data without serving the original private data itself. SAGE first performs attribute-based data generation: it identifies essential attributes, extracts related key information from original documents, and produces synthetic retrieval documents that preserve useful content. It then applies agent-based interactive refinement, in which a privacy agent detects risks and a re-writing agent repeatedly removes risky material while seeking to preserve utility. This synthesis occurs offline; at inference, the corresponding synthetic document is returned when the original document would have been retrieved. The paper positions SAGE against paraphrasing, ZeroGen, AttrPrompt, and earlier synthetic-data approaches, arguing that prior privacy-preserving generation does not necessarily preserve the contextual information needed for RAG. Experiments use Llama3-8b-chat for utility, GPT-3.5-turbo and Llama3-8b-chat for privacy testing, bge-large-en-v1.5 embeddings, and FAISS retrieval. Settings include HealthcareMagic-101 medical dialogs, Wiki-PII combining Enron Mail information with wikitext-103, and open-domain question answering datasets including Natural Questions, Trivia QA, WebQuestions, and CT. Across ROUGE-L, BLEU, Exact Match, and LLM-based evaluation, synthetic retrieval data performs comparably to, and sometimes better than, original retrieval data and surpasses no retrieval, simple paraphrasing, and ZeroGen. On HealthcareMagic-101, SAGE’s two stages report the strongest BLEU-1 and ROUGE-L results. Privacy is tested with targeted and untargeted composite structured prompting attacks that seek verbatim reproduction, high ROUGE-L similarity, disease cases, or personally identifiable information. Stage 1 reduces leakage relative to original data and paraphrasing; Stage 2 reduces attack success to nearly zero. Ablations show that model choice, attribute count, and retrieved-document count affect utility and exposure: more attributes help until improvements stop, fewer attributes reduce exposure, and more retrieved documents can worsen Stage-1 leakage, while refinement remains minimally leaky. The paper concludes that SAGE can support safer RAG in sensitive domains, while noting the need for broader validation and stricter guarantees such as Differential Privacy.

Abstract

The text concerns retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems that use external knowledge sources, including private data. It introduces SAGE, a two-stage paradigm for generating synthetic data as a privacy-preserving alternative to retrieval data. SAGE first preserves key contextual information through attribute-based extraction and generation, then improves privacy through agent-based iterative refinement. Experiments show that synthetic retrieval data achieves comparable performance to original data while substantially reducing privacy risks.

Introduction

The unit explains that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines retrieved information with a user query for a large language model, but retrieval from private data can cause serious information leakage. It proposes using synthetic data instead of original data as retrieval context to reduce privacy risks while preserving useful information. The authors introduce SAGE, a two-stage method that first generates synthetic retrieval data from extracted attributes and key information, then iteratively refines it through privacy assessment and rewriting agents. Experiments show that synthetic retrieval data can achieve comparable performance to original data while substantially reducing privacy risks.

Related Works

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), introduced by Lewis et al. (2020), improves the accuracy and relevance of large language model outputs and can reduce hallucinations, but it is vulnerable to privacy attacks and information extraction. Huang et al. (2023), Zeng et al. (2024), and Qi et al. (2024) document privacy leakage and attacks affecting retrieval-based language models and production RAG systems. Researchers including Ye et al. (2022), Meng et al. (2022), and Gao et al. (2023a) use large language models to generate synthetic data for downstream tasks and improve its quality and diversity. Tang et al. (2023) and Xie et al. propose privacy-preserving synthetic data methods, but the described data is not guaranteed to retain contextual information from the original data and therefore may not fit RAG systems well.

Methods

The SAGE framework generates synthetic retrieval data through two stages: attribute-based data generation and agent-based interactive refinement. Stage-1 identifies essential attributes, extracts related key information from original data, and generates synthetic data that preserves useful content. Stage-2 uses a privacy agent and a re-writing agent to iteratively detect and remove privacy risks while retaining utility. The resulting synthetic data is generated offline and returned as retrieval data during inference when the corresponding original data is identified.

Experiment

The experiment evaluates SAGE's utility and privacy properties in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) settings. It uses Llama3-8b-chat for utility evaluation and both GPT-3.5-turbo and Llama3-8b-chat for privacy experiments, with bge-large-en-v1.5 embeddings and FAISS retrieval. The evaluation covers medical dialog monitoring with HealthcareMagic-101 and a Wiki-PII setting combining personal information from Enron Mail with wikitext-103, as well as open-domain question answering datasets. Baselines include simple paraphrasing, ZeroGen, and AttrPrompt; on HealthcareMagic-101, Stage-1 and Stage-2 achieve the strongest reported BLEU-1 and ROUGE-L results.

Utility of using synthetic data

The unit evaluates the utility of synthetic data as retrieval data by comparing generated answers with ground-truth answers using ROUGE-L, BLEU, Exact Match, and LLM-based evaluation. On medical dialog and open-domain question answering, synthetic retrieval data performs comparably to or better than original data and outperforms generation without retrieval, simple paraphrasing, and ZeroGen. The experiments use medical dialog data, WikiText-101 with Enron Mail, and ODQA datasets including Natural Questions, Trivia QA, WebQuestions, and CT. Additional tables report targeted and untargeted attack results on the Wiki-PII and HealthCareMagic datasets.

Privacy of using synthetic data

The unit evaluates the privacy of using synthetic data as retrieval data through targeted and untargeted attacks following Zeng et al. (2024). It describes a composite structured prompting attack on retrieval-augmented generation systems, using information and command components to induce retrieval and reproduction of data. On HealthCareMagic and Wiki-PII, the first-stage synthetic data shows reduced leakage compared with original data and simple paraphrasing, while agent-based refinement reduces attack success to nearly zero. The evaluation measures verbatim repetition, ROUGE-L similarity, and extraction of targeted information such as disease cases and personal identifiable information.

Ablation Studies

The ablation studies investigate how model choice, the number of attributes, and the number of retrieved documents per query affect synthetic data quality and privacy. On the HealthCareMagic dataset, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 perform well for the information extractor and data generator, while Llama3-Chat-8b produces poor utility and can perform worse than zero-shot prediction. Increasing the number of attributes generally improves performance until gains stop, while smaller attribute sets reduce privacy exposure. Increasing retrieved documents can amplify privacy leakage for stage-1 data, but agent-based refinement maintains minimal privacy leakage as retrieval increases.

Conclusions

The paper investigates using synthetic data as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) data to mitigate privacy concerns. It proposes a two-stage synthetic pipeline combining attribute-based data generation with iterative agent-based refinement. Experimental results show that the generated synthetic data achieves comparable performance to original data when used as RAG data while mitigating associated privacy issues. The work identifies opportunities for applying RAG systems safely in sensitive domains.

Limitations

The research investigates using synthetic data for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and proposes a pipeline for generating high-utility, privacy-preserving synthetic data. The pipeline is evaluated in representative scenarios, including healthcare, for effectiveness and safety. The authors acknowledge that practical attacks on RAG systems have different definitions and settings from Differential Privacy (DP). They identify validation across more domains and datasets, as well as integrating strict privacy guarantees such as DP into RAG, as future work.

Ethic Statement

The work explores using synthetic data to mitigate privacy risks in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), particularly in safety-critical domains. It generates synthetic data to replace sensitive data during RAG, with the aim of reducing privacy breach risks and protecting individuals’ well-being and privacy rights. The authors state that they followed ethical guidelines and that further research is needed to understand the risks and benefits of the method. They argue that privacy-preserving techniques are essential for the responsible deployment of RAG systems and for balancing the benefits of advanced language models with individual privacy protection.
