  • In Laurence Sterne in Germany: Sentimental Journey is a work of fiction and a book by Laurence Sterne, published in London on February 27, 1768, less than three weeks before Sterne’s death. It presents a sentimental experience guided by whimsicality, using travel, human feeling, humor, and sympathy as central materials. Its story includes Franciscan Lorenzo, who begs alms of Yorick, the dead-ass episode at Nampont, and the Starling narrative. Immediately after publication, the book was transplanted to Germany, where it received immediate translations and quickly gained wide popularity and commanding influence. Its success drew on Sterne’s existing reputation, interest in travel books, and its appeal to emotional and sentimental tendencies. German readers found its direct presentation of human sentiment more accessible than the eccentric British humor of Tristram Shandy; it received a more immediate welcome and became the principal source of Sterne’s German success. It changed German critical reception of Sterne, helping establish his recognized position as a literary leader and becoming more popular than his sermons. German criticism praised the work’s sentiment and humor and considered its emotional and moral effects, while comparing translations by Bode and Mittelstedt. In Bode’s translation it was reviewed as an individual, unparalleled work of genius, and it was rated above doubtful works attributed to Sterne. Its translations and imitators influenced the sentimental movement in Germany. It became the primary model for sentimental travel writing, shaping titles, structures, characters, episodes, expressions, sentimental and whimsical narration, sympathy for people and animals, and criticism of superficial travel writing. Its Lorenzo story inspired the Lorenzodosen, while its traveler-centered feeling influenced discussions of travel writing and prompted an opposing preference for objective travel accounts. Its motifs, including Lorenzo, the dead ass, Maria of Moulines, and the starling, were repeatedly adapted and were also satirized by Timme, whose satire chiefly targeted its sentimental influence while valuing its narrative whimsies.
