He appears in Tristram Shandy and, as narrator of episodes including the dead ass and Starling in The Sentimental Journey, became the figure through whom German readers chiefly interpreted Sterne’s sentimental qualities. Early German reviews identified him as a clergyman and linked his name on sermons to Sterne; later readers connected him with excellent sermons and sentimental feeling. After The Sentimental Journey reached Germany, German literary and emotional conditions remade Yorick into a delicate, benevolent, morally restrained champion of the oppressed: a model of gentleness, sympathy toward people and animals, and resistance to self-seeking, impropriety, and arbitrary power. The records stress that this was a selective, sometimes superficial conception, reinforced by the Yorick-Eliza letters, translations, and spurious works rather than by a full reading of Sterne’s controlled suggestiveness. This German Yorick became a preceptor in conduct and a powerful literary model. His treatment of Franciscan Lorenzo inspired practical sentimental adherence; his sympathy for animals, whimsical humor, mock-scientific classification of travelers, “hobby-horse” language, blank pages, and other formal vagaries entered discussion, correspondence, and later eccentric writing. He helped establish sentimental ground later associated with The Sorrows of Young Werther and was described as a liberating, beautiful spirit. German writers produced translations, continuations, journeys in Yorick’s manner, and works attributed to his supposed son. Imitators followed his footprints through adapted encounters, humane reflections, sentimental poses, and scenes modeled on Lorenzo, the Monk, and other episodes. Yorick accordingly became both the central emblem of German sentimentalism and a target for satire. Critics objected to followers’ exaggerated sympathy and emotional display while often distinguishing Yorick himself from their excesses; Timme especially used would-be Yoricks to expose sentimental imitation.

Bock explicitly took Yorick and Jacobi with him on his journey and adapted Yorick’s encounters, reflections, humane sentiments, and self-questioning.
