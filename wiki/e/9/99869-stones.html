Abstract written by a model

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, stones are interpreted as the bones of earth and as the children of Mount Parnassus survivors, cast behind Deucalion and Pyrrha after the inundation; they gradually lose their hardness as their earthy and humid parts become human bodies, their solid parts become bones, and their veins remain veins, with Deucalion’s stones becoming men and Pyrrha’s becoming women. In The Library, stones thrown by Deucalion and Pyrrha over their heads at Zeus's bidding become men from Deucalion's and women from Pyrrha's. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, stones cast behind Deucalion and Pyrrha soften into humans, with his becoming men and hers women, forming a hard race suited to labor.

The stones cast behind Deucalion and Pyrrha gradually lose their hardness and stiffness and assume human forms. Those thrown by Deucalion become men, while those thrown by Pyrrha renew the female race.

notes .. notes

The children of those preserved from the inundation were said to be the stones mentioned by the Poet. The explanation connects this fable with words that could mean either “stone” or “child,” and notes that a word meaning “people” could also mean “stone.”
