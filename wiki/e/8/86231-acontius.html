Acontius

person · 1 work · 15 quoted facts · 9 connections
Also called: Aconti, Acontio, ego, me, meique, mihi, nostrum, te, tibi, tu, tua

Abstract written by a model

Acontius is a wealthy, honorable lover and poet from Cea who loves Cydippe and seeks marriage as the fulfillment of what he regards as her pledged faith. Wounded by Love and jealous of a rival suitor or possible attendant, he uses deception to bind her: he writes a verse on an apple, causing her to pronounce an oath before Diana. In his letter he calls that single pledge sufficient, presents his ancestry and morals, asks Cydippe to reject her rival, and insists he seeks to be her husband rather than an adulterer. He petitions Diana for Cydippe’s welfare, fears for her affection and safety, and endures her anger, yet threatens force or further schemes if persuasion fails. Cydippe portrays his written oath as a distant wound that harms her, doubts his influence with Diana, but ultimately says she wishes to be united with him.

Acontius is in one work on this site, Heroides and Amores. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Acontius in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where Acontius first appears

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

XX

Acontius writes to Cydippe and asks her to remember that she pledged herself to him once, which he considers sufficient. He describes his deception, jealousy, love, and willingness to endure her anger, while insisting that he seeks her as a husband rather than as an adulterer. He threatens that, if persuasion fails, he will resort to force or other schemes to obtain her.

nam quod habes et tu gemini verba altera pacti, 155

Acontius argues that Cydippe’s oath to him is binding because it was made before Diana, and he presents himself as wealthy, honorable, and of reputable ancestry from Cea. He urges Cydippe to reject the rival suitor, marry him, and preserve both her safety and his.

XXI

Acontius wrote the deceptive verse that Cydippe read on an apple and believes that a single pledge from her was enough. He is also described as beseeching Diana on Cydippe's behalf, though Cydippe doubts his power with the goddess.

Cum tamen haec dixi, cum me tibi firma negavi,

Acontius has bound the speaker through a written oath and caused her to be struck by a distant, letter-like wound. He wishes to visit her, is urged to pray for her recovery, and is finally told that she wishes to be united with him.
