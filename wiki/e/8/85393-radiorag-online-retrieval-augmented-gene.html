RadioRAG is designed to address limitations of large language models in diagnostic radiology, especially hallucinations and lack of current information, by retrieving real-time context from Radiopaedia before generating concise answers. The study retrospectively tests six models, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, Mistral-7B-instruct-v0.2, Mixtral-8x7B-instruct-v0.1, Llama3-8B, and Llama3-70B-instruct, with and without RadioRAG. Evaluation uses RSNA-RadioQA, 80 expert-curated questions adapted from the Radiological Society of North America Case Collection across many radiology subspecialties, and ExtendedQA, 24 expert-curated previously unseen questions intended to limit data-contamination concerns. A board-certified radiologist provides a human comparator. The pipeline generates search phrases, retrieves Radiopaedia material, embeds and selects relevant text using Chroma and LangChain, then prompts the model to answer from that context. Results show that RadioRAG generally improves diagnostic accuracy and factuality, with relative accuracy gains of up to 54%. Improvements are statistically significant for GPT-3.5-turbo and Mixtral-8x7B-instruct-v0.1 on RSNA-RadioQA; open-weights models show especially large gains on ExtendedQA and can become competitive with GPT-4. RadioRAG-powered models generally outperform the human expert radiologist, and the approach reduces hallucinations overall, with particularly strong benefits in breast imaging and emergency radiology. However, irrelevant retrieved articles can produce answers that are grounded in retrieved material yet incorrect. The discussion emphasizes that performance depends on retrieval quality and identifies limitations: retrieval errors, reliance on one online source, possible website load, response-time cost, and the small ExtendedQA dataset. Appendix material details specialty-level accuracy, hallucination rates, retrieval relevance, datasets, model answers and sources, technical terminology, and timing. Conventional GPT-4 answering takes about 30 seconds per RSNA-RadioQA question, whereas RadioRAG takes about 115 seconds because of search, embedding, retrieval, and generation steps.

RadioRAG: Online Retrieval-augmented Generation for Radiology Question Answering

The unit introduces RadioRAG, an online retrieval-augmented generation system for radiology question answering. The work is authored by Soroosh Tayebi Arasteh and colleagues from institutions including University Hospital RWTH Aachen, Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Stanford University, and other centers in Germany and the United States. The document is identified as a preprint version of a paper published in Radiology: Artificial Intelligence in 2025. Soroosh Tayebi Arasteh is listed as the corresponding author.

Abstract

The unit concerns RadioRAG, an end-to-end retrieval-augmented generation framework for answering radiology questions using real-time information from authoritative online sources. It evaluates GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, and Llama3 models on 80 RSNA Case Collection questions and 24 expert-curated questions, with and without retrieved information from Radiopaedia. RadioRAG improved diagnostic accuracy across most models, with relative increases of up to 54%, and matched or exceeded non-RAG models and a human radiologist in several areas. Its benefits varied by model, with particularly strong performance in breast imaging and emergency radiology.

1. Introduction

The unit concerns the use of artificial intelligence and large language models in diagnostic radiology, including their benefits and limitations. It introduces retrieval-augmented generation as an approach that allows language models to use external, up-to-date sources and potentially reduce hallucinations. The authors present Radiology RAG (RadioRAG), a framework that retrieves current information from Radiopaedia to answer detailed radiology questions. They investigate whether real-time context retrieval reduces hallucinations and improves response accuracy.

2. Materials and Methods / 2.1. RSNA Cases .. 2.6. Code and Data Availability

This retrospective study evaluated RadioRAG, a radiology retrieval-augmented generation pipeline, using the RSNA-RadioQA and ExtendedQA datasets. RSNA-RadioQA comprised 80 cases from the Radiological Society of North America Case Collection, while ExtendedQA contained 24 expert-curated radiology questions designed to reduce data-contamination concerns. Six language models, including GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, Mistral-7B-instruct-v0.2, Mixtral-8x7B-instruct-v0.1, Llama3-8B, and Llama3-70B-instruct, were assessed with and without RadioRAG and compared with a board-certified radiologist. The system generated search phrases, retrieved and embedded Radiopaedia content, selected relevant text with Chroma and LangChain, and instructed each model to answer concisely using the retrieved context.

3. Results / 3.1. Dataset Characteristics .. 3.5. Comparison to Human Performance

This unit evaluates RadioRAG for improving the diagnostic performance and factuality of large language models on the RSNA-RadioQA and ExtendedQA datasets. RadioRAG significantly improved GPT-3.5-turbo and Mixtral-8x7B-instruct-v0.1 on RSNA-RadioQA, while improvements for the other models were not statistically significant. Open-weights models showed particularly large gains on ExtendedQA and became competitive with GPT-4 when using RadioRAG. RadioRAG generally reduced hallucinations, although irrelevant retrieved articles sometimes caused factually grounded but incorrect answers; RadioRAG-powered models generally outperformed the human expert radiologist.

4. Discussion

The unit evaluates Radiology RAG (RadioRAG), a framework that retrieves context from Radiopaedia to improve large language models in radiological question answering. It introduces RSNA-RadioQA for internal testing and ExtendedQA, containing 24 previously unseen questions, for external validation. RadioRAG generally improved accuracy, particularly for open-source models such as Mixtral-8x7B-instruct-v0.1, although irrelevant retrieved context sometimes caused incorrect answers. The framework remains limited by retrieval errors, response time, reliance on a single online source, potential website load, and the small size of ExtendedQA.

This unit lists metadata for RSNA-RadioQA questions, including identifiers, original-case DOIs, authors, titles, publication dates, and subspecialties. The cases cover breast, chest, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, cardiac, vascular, neurologic, oncologic, pediatric, and other radiological specialties. The material states that the original cases are copyrighted by the Radiological Society of North America, Inc. (RSNA).

Appendix S2

The ExtendedQA dataset consists of 24 expert-curated radiology questions with corresponding reference standard answers and subspecialty labels. The questions cover diagnostic findings and differentials in chest, cardiac, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, musculoskeletal, vascular, emergency, pediatric, and interventional radiology. Computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and X-ray findings are used to identify conditions including malignancy, vascular disease, organ lesions, bone disorders, and imaging artifacts.

Appendix S3

This unit is a glossary of technical terms related to language models and computational text processing. It defines tokens as small units of text used to process, represent, or generate text. It explains vector representations and text embeddings as numerical representations that capture similarity or related meaning. It also describes a vector database as a system designed to store, retrieve, and compare vector representations through similarity search.

Appendix S4

The unit compares the time required for conventional question answering and RadioRAG-powered question answering using experiments with the RSNA-RadioQA dataset. Conventional QA using GPT-4 through the OpenAI API took approximately 30 seconds per question. RadioRAG-powered QA added key-phrase extraction, Radiopaedia searching, database creation and embedding, and LLM response generation, taking approximately 115 seconds per question. This was about four times longer than conventional QA.
