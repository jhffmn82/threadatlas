battle of Ægos-potamoi

Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, it was an Athenian naval defeat in which Lysander exploited the fleet’s disorder and captured its ships; Conon and a few commanders escaped, while Philocles was taken prisoner and executed. In Description of Greece, it was the defeat and capture of the Athenian fleet, after which Samians dedicated a statue of Lysander and Ephesians honored Spartans; later Conon’s victories prompted Ionians to shift allegiance and honor Conon and Timotheus. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, it was fought by Spartans, allies, and Lysander against Athenians, whose defeat Athenians attributed to Lysander bribing admirals Tydeus and Adimantus; after the victory, Lacedæmonians dedicated statues at Delphi.

The battle of Ægos-potamoi was an Athenian fleet defeat and capture, after which Samians dedicated a statue of Lysander and Ephesians honored Spartans; later Conon’s victories prompted Ionians to shift allegiance and honor Conon and Timotheus.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

After the Athenian fleet was defeated at Aegospotami, the Samians dedicated a statue of Lysander at Olympia and the Ephesians honored Lysander and other Spartans. When Conon later won at Cnidus and Mount Dorium, the Ionians shifted their allegiance again and honored Conon and Timotheus.

Aegospotami battle in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Aegospotami battle first appears

Abstract written by a model

Aegospotami battle was fought by Spartans, allies, and Lysander against Athenians; after victory, Lacedæmonians dedicated Delphi statues, while Athenians attributed their defeat to Lysander bribing admirals Tydeus and Adimantus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IX.

The Lacedæmonians dedicated statues at Delphi to commemorate their victory over the Athenians at Ægos-potamoi, including figures of Lysander, his prophet Abas, his pilot Hermon, and Spartan or allied participants. The Athenians claimed that their defeat resulted from the bribery of their admirals Tydeus and Adimantus by Lysander, citing Sibylline and Musæan oracles in support of this accusation.
