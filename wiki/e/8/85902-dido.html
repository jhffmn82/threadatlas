Dido in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Dido, also identified as Elissa Sychaei, is queen and founder of Carthage after exile, ruling a kingdom she offers Aeneas. Sister to Anna and Pygmalion, she receives Aeneas when he is cast ashore and loves him despite his faithlessness, hoping for wedlock and possibly carrying his child. As he prepares to leave, she writes pleading for a little time to calm her love and sorrow and learn endurance. Fearing abandonment, wounded by cruel love, she threatens and prepares to die by her sword.

Dido in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Dido is a Tyrian princess, widow of Sichaeus, sister of Anna, and queen and founder of Carthage. After her brother Pygmalion’s hostility and Sichaeus’s murder, revealed by his ghost, she flees Tyre with companions, a fleet, and hidden treasure. In Carthage she rules a growing Tyrian city: hearing petitions, making laws, assigning tasks, directing walls and towers, building Juno’s temple, and protecting and governing her people. She welcomes the shipwrecked Trojans, restores and protects their fleet, receives Aeneas as a noble guest, and presides over a lavish feast. Cupid, taking Aeneas’s form, causes her former care to give way to love. Captivated, she asks about Troy, its heroes, and Aeneas’s travels, and her feeling becomes consuming passion. Though she had vowed fidelity to dead Sichaeus, she tells Anna that Aeneas has revived her former love. After sharing shelter with him in a cave, she calls their union a marriage and treats him as lover and supposed husband. Her passion makes her neglect Carthage’s construction, royal trust, duties, and reputation. When Dido learns that Aeneas is preparing to leave, she confronts him in grief and anger. She recalls saving, feeding, housing, and aiding him and the Trojans, and giving him her throne and bed; she accuses him of broken faith and asks him to remain, then merely to delay. He leaves despite her pleas. In despair she prepares secret funeral rites, deceives Anna about their purpose, curses Aeneas and the Trojans, and kills herself on the funeral pile with Aeneas’s sword. Aeneas later sees the shore’s blaze and understands it as Dido’s destruction. Among the dead, Dido still bears her wound; she rejects Aeneas’s tearful explanation and turns away to Sichaeus.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK I

Dido is identified in the argument as the queen of Carthage. Venus’s device causes her to begin developing a passion for Aeneas, and she asks him to recount his adventures after the siege of Troy.

Meantime, in shades of night Aeneas lies:

Dido rules the growing city of Carthage after fleeing Tyre because of her brother Pygmalion’s hostility. She had been married to Sichaeus, whose murder was revealed to her by his ghost, and she led companions away with a fleet and his hidden treasure.

To whom, with sorrow streaming from his eyes,

Dido rules the rising Tyrian city, hears petitions, dispenses laws, and directs its building. She welcomes the shipwrecked Trojans, promises to repair their fleet and provide protection, and recognizes Aeneas as the son of Venus and Anchises. She leads Aeneas to her palace and hosts a splendid feast, while Venus plans to make her fall violently in love.

Cupid meantime assum’d his form and face,

Dido hosts the Trojan and Tyrian guests and presides over the feast. She is captivated by Cupid in Aeneas's form, drinks deeply of love for Aeneas, asks many questions about Troy and its heroes, and requests the full story of the war and Aeneas's wanderings.

BOOK IV

Dido falls deeply in love with Aeneas and tells Anna that his worth, suffering, speech, and appearance have revived feelings like those she had for Sichaeus. She calls her relationship with Aeneas a marriage after their sheltering together in the cave, neglects the construction of Carthage, and is denounced by Fame for abandoning her royal duties and reputation.

The pious prince was seiz’d with sudden fear;

Dido discovers Aeneas’s preparations and confronts him with grief, anger, and accusations of ingratitude and broken faith. She reminds him that she saved him and the Trojans, fed them, took him to her throne and bed, and restored his fleet. She asks first for his continued stay and later only for a short delay, but her pleas fail.

The wretched queen, pursued by cruel fate,

Dido is consumed by grief, rage, despair, and love after Aeneas leaves. She deceives Anna about the purpose of the funeral rites, curses Aeneas and the Trojans, and kills herself on the funeral pile.

BOOK V

Aeneas sees the blaze on the Punic shore and divines that it concerns Dido. He associates the fire with the destructive passions of an injured woman.

Ye realms, yet unreveal’d to human sight,

Dido appears among the spirits of the mournful fields, still bearing the wound in her bloodied bosom. Aeneas addresses her with tears and explains that he left Carthage unwillingly under divine command, but Dido refuses to answer and turns away to seek Sichaeus.

Dido in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Dido, daughter of Belus and sister of Pygmalion, flees Tyre and establishes Carthage as its queen. She welcomes Aeneas and loves him, offering home, kingdom, and marriage. When he leaves at Jupiter’s command, she kills herself. Later, among the dead of unrequited love, wounded and silent, she turns away from Aeneas’s sorrowful explanation.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter XXIV

Dido is the daughter of Belus, the sister of Pygmalion, and the queen who establishes Carthage after fleeing Tyre. She welcomes Aeneas and the Trojans, becomes deeply enamored of Aeneas, and appears ready to offer him a home, kingdom, and marriage. When Aeneas leaves at Jupiter’s command, Dido kills herself on a funeral pile.

Chapter XXV

Dido appears among those who died from unrequited love, with her wound still recent. Aeneas addresses her with sorrow and explains that he left her reluctantly under the commands of Jove, but she silently turns away from him.
