Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Achelous is diverted by Hercules into a channel that waters much of the Calydonian country, while the account connects this work with the poetic story of Hercules fighting him as a bull and cutting off one of his horns. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Achelous is a river god and king of the waters who shelters Theseus during a flood, recounts transformations, and later becomes a serpent and bull while competing with Hercules for Deïanira, losing a horn. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Achelous is a river flowing between and separating Acarnania and Ætolia whose inundations damage both regions until Hercules raises banks and straightens its course. In The Library, Achelous takes the form of a bull while contending with Hercules for Deianira, loses a horn, and later purifies Alcmaeon at his springs, gives him Callirrhoe as wife, and directs Alcmaeon's sons to dedicate a necklace and robe at Delphi. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Achelous is a river flowing through Ætolia and Acarnania whose silt creates land where Alcmæon finds refuge and marries Callirhoe, later helping form and separate the Echinades from the mainland. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Achelous is a river-god who becomes a serpent or bull, contends with Hercules for Dejanira, and loses one horn, which becomes the Cornucopia.

Acheloüs in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Acheloüs first appears

Abstract written by a model

Acheloüs is a river god, king of the waters, father of the Naiads, and associated with a rain-swollen river. He shelters Theseus in his pumice-and-sandstone grotto during a flood and recounts transformations. Later, competing with Hercules for Deïanira, he becomes a serpent and bull, is defeated, loses a horn, and hides his mutilation among waves and reeds.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLE V. [VIII.547-610]

Acheloüs, swollen with rain, warns Theseus about the danger of crossing the flooded river and receives him in his grotto. He recounts the transformations of the Naiads and Perimele, and later describes Proteus's many forms.

BOOK THE NINTH.

Acheloüs recounts that he and Hercules competed for Deïanira, arguing that he was a god and ruler of the waters. He transforms into a serpent and then a bull, but Hercules defeats him and breaks off one of his horns. Acheloüs later conceals his mutilated head among the waves, covering it with willow branches or reeds.

Abstract written by a model

Acheloüs is a river flowing between and separating Acarnania and Ætolia. Its inundations damaged both and confused their limits; Hercules raised banks, straightened its course, and helped establish peace between the neighboring nations.

BOOK THE NINTH.

The river Acheloüs flowed between Ætolia and Acarnania and often damaged the surrounding countries through inundation. According to the explanation, Hercules raised banks and made its course more uniform and straight, helping establish peace between the neighboring nations.

Achelous in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Achelous first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achelous is a figure who takes the form of a bull while contending with Hercules for Deianira, loses a horn, and exchanges Amalthea’s horn to recover it. Later, at his springs, he purifies Alcmaeon, gives him Callirrhoe as wife, and directs Alcmaeon’s sons to dedicate a necklace and robe at Delphi.

Achelous assumed the likeness of a bull while competing with Hercules for Deianira. Hercules broke off one of his horns, and Achelous recovered it by giving Amalthea’s horn in exchange.

Achelous in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Achelous first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achelous is a river flowing through Ætolia and Acarnania to the Echinades. By silting it creates new land, where Alcmæon finds refuge and marries its daughter Callirhoe. Later reduced deposits, after Ætolia is deserted, form and separate the Echinades from the mainland.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Achelous formed new land by silting, and Alcmæon settled there after learning from Delphi that he would be safe on ground created after his mother’s bloodshed. Alcmæon married Callirhoe, the daughter of Achelous, and the account attributes the separation of the Echinades from the mainland to changes in the river’s deposits.

Achelous in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where Achelous first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achelous is a river-god who can become a serpent or bull. Seeking Dejanira, he struggles with Hercules, is defeated while a bull, and loses one horn, Cornucopia.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter XVI

Achelous, the river-god, tells Theseus and his companions how Hercules defeated him. He says that he could take the forms of a serpent and a bull, but Hercules overthrew him as a bull and tore off one of his horns.
