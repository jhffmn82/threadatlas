Abstract written by a model

Georgiana Tidman, Mrs. Tidman, is the widowed younger sister of Augustin Jedd, the Dean, aunt of Salome and Sheba, and resident guest at the Deanery. A high-spirited but self-described feeble woman with a keen sense of right and wrong, she is devoted to horse racing, gambling on races, and her half-share in Dandy Dick. After her husband's death she continued his career as a horse-breeder and gentleman jockey under the racing name George Tidd, wearing crimson and black diamonds; she ultimately lost her horses and property. At the Deanery she arrives in masculine racing dress, declares her ownership interest in Dandy Dick, plans to bet every penny on him, and persuades others to stake money on the horse despite promising not to own one while among the Canons and Bishop. She serves tea while studying betting, rushes to save horses during the Swan Inn fire, shelters Dandy Dick in the Deanery stables, and worries over his singed tail during the storm. She presses the Dean to wager on Dandy Dick to aid the Spire, organizes food and champagne for the horse, and protects Salome and Sheba. She accompanies Sir Tristram in seeking the missing Dean, helps rally race-course associates to rescue him from the Hill, and wins fifteen hundred pounds on the Handicap. She becomes engaged to Sir Tristram and helps reveal Blore's strychnine in the bolus. At the end, she rebukes the Dean for deception and lost dignity, while offering George Tidd's thousand-pound loan for the Spire if he accepts being laughed at as a Sporting Dean.

DANDY DICK. / THE FIRST ACT.

Georgiana Tidman is Dr. Jedd's widowed sister and is coming to live at the Deanery of St. Marvells. Dr. Jedd says that she continued her late husband's career as a horse-breeder and gentleman jockey, became known as the Daisy of the Turf, and eventually lost her horses and property.

_GEORGIANA TIDMAN enters.

Georgiana arrives at the Deanery dressed in pronounced masculine style and behaving in a jovial, horsey manner. She explains that she was formerly known as George Tidd, a racing figure whose colors were crimson and black diamonds, and that she owns half of Dandy Dick through an arrangement with John Fielder. She agrees to bet on Dandy Dick and encourages Salome and Sheba to stake their money on him.

THE SECOND ACT.

Georgiana serves tea, mocks the dinner and the male guests, and studies her betting book. When the Swan Inn burns, she rushes to help rescue the horses, learns that Dandy Dick is safe but has a singed tail, and insists on bringing him into the Deanery stables.

GEORGIANA and SIR TRISTRAM enter by the window.

Georgiana helps shelter Dandy Dick in the Deanery stables and encourages Sir Tristram not to despair over the horse's injured tail. She urges the Dean to bet fifty pounds on Dandy Dick in the handicap race to help pay for the Spire, then orders Blore to prepare food and champagne for the horse's party on the Hill. During the storm, she prepares to sit with Dandy Dick because she fears for him.

The curtains at the window are pushed aside, and SALOME and SHEBA enter; both in

Georgiana scolds Salome and Sheba for going out without their aunt and reprimands Darbey and Tarver for encouraging the girls. She calls Sir Tristram to drive the outsiders into the road and opens the door when the gate bell rings.

Georgiana visits the police-station with Sir Tristram to report her brother Augustin Jedd's disappearance. She recognizes the Dean in the cell, seeks his release, and is told by Hannah that a whistle and Nick the fire-brigade horse will bring him safely back to the Deanery; she is then distressed to learn that Noah has taken Kitty instead of Old Nick.

_The second scene is the Morning Room at the Deanery again.

Georgiana helps Sir Tristram rescue the Dean from the Hill by rallying race-course associates to their aid. She reveals that she won fifteen hundred pounds over the Handicap and later agrees to marry Sir Tristram. She also helps expose Blore's addition of strychnine to the bolus.

Georgiana tells the Dean that his family influence has gone and his dignity has been crushed by deception. She says that George Tidd will lend him a thousand for the poor, innocent old Spire, provided he admits there is no harm in laughing at a Sporting Dean. She explains that the Dean himself has been riding the horse called Deception hardest.
