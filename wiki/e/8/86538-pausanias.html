Pausanias in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Pausanias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pausanias is presented as a Spartan/Lacedaemonian king and general. As tutor of the underage Leonidas, he commanded the Greek army at Plataea, helped win there, forbade taking Persian prisoners, gained the highest honor among Greek commanders, and punished leading Theban supporters of Persia. He then freed cities from Persian garrisons and captured Byzantium, but Persian luxury, money, and influence corrupted him: he acted tyrannically, secretly conspired to betray Greece with the Persian king, was exposed, and died sealed in the temple of Minerva Chalcidica. His alleged treachery, including an attempt to recruit Themistocles, remained politically important. Later records show him campaigning against the Elei, failing to capture Elis, reconciling Athenian exiles with citizens, and withdrawing from Phocis after arranging a truce with the Boeotians.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. HI.

Pausanias, described as the tutor of Leonidas, commanded the Greek army because Leonidas was under age. He helped lead the victory at Plataea, ordered that no Persian prisoners be taken, received the greatest honor among the Greek commanders, and later ordered the execution of the leading Theban supporters of Persia.

I.

Pausanias, the Lacedaemonian commander at Plataea, freed cities from Persian garrisons and took Byzantium. He later accepted Persian luxury, acted tyrannically, conspired with Artabazus to betray Greece, was exposed, and died after being sealed inside the temple of Minerva Chalcidica.

CHAP. VII.

Pausanias was remembered for treachery and was used by the Lacedaemonians in their accusations against Themistocles. He had allegedly solicited Themistocles to join him in betraying Greece to Xerxes.

CHAP. III.

Pausanias led a Lacedaemonian army against the Elei, captured Lasion, Threstus, Aulis, Eupagium, Opus, and Pylos, and camped near Elis. He failed to take the city, ravaged the surrounding country, fortified castles, and wintered in Dyme.

CHAP. VI.

Pausanias, then king of Lacedaemon, marched with a large army to Athens. Out of envy toward Lysander and concern about Greek hostility to Sparta, he reconciled the Athenian exiles and citizens.

CHAP. vm.

Pausanias, king of Sparta, was sent to Phocis with six thousand men. After hearing of the Lacedaemonian defeat at Haliartus, he made a truce with the Boeotians and returned to the Peloponnesus.

Pausanias in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Pausanias first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pausanias is presented both as an author-narrator and as a historical figure: regent for Plistarchus, leader of the Lacedæmonians at Platæa, sparer of a Coan woman, and later troubled by killing Cleonice at Byzantium. He is buried among the tombs of Pausanias and Leonidas. In his account, he describes Attica’s places and traditions with attention to inquiry, and Olympia’s statues and votive offerings rather than all victors. The records also distinguish a later Pausanias, son of Plistoanax, who opposed the Thirty Tyrants and retired to Tegea.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Description of Greece / BOOK I.--ATTICA.

Pausanias describes the places, monuments, temples, statues, paintings, traditions, and histories of Attica. He often distinguishes popular traditions from accounts based on closer inquiry.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Pausanias, son of Cleombrotus, served as regent for Plistarchus and led the Lacedæmonians at Platæa before crossing to the Hellespont. A later Pausanias, son of Plistoanax, opposed establishing the rule of the Thirty Tyrants at Athens, was tried and acquitted, and later went into voluntary exile at Tegea.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

Pausanias explains that his subject is not a complete catalogue of Olympic victors but an account of statues and other votive offerings. He records inscriptions, traditions, artists, athletic victories, and uncertainties concerning figures such as Oebotas and Aristotle.
