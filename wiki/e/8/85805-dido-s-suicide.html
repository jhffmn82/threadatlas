Dido's suicide in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Dido's suicide is her impending death, which she presents as caused by Aeneas's departure and refusal. Writing beside a Trojan sword, she says Aeneas provides both the cause and the weapon, watches tears fall on it, tells Anna she will soon make final offerings to her ashes, and expects Aeneas to be named its cause.

Dido’s impending death in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Dido’s death concludes Book IV. Her union with Aeneas foreshadows debate, death, and later woes, and when Aeneas cannot be persuaded to remain, she despairs, says she will die if he leaves, and seeks delay to prepare for grief. After his departure, she decides death is her only relief, laments and curses him, and kills herself with his sword. Her soul struggles until Iris releases it. Later, her death is reported true, and Aeneas sees her among mournful spirits, believing himself its cause.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK IV

The argument states that when Aeneas cannot be persuaded to remain, Dido contrives her own death, with the book concluding on that event. In the passage itself, her death is foreshadowed by the statement that her cave union leads to debate, death, and succeeding woes.

The pious prince was seiz’d with sudden fear;

Dido speaks of being a despairing queen and says that she will die if Aeneas leaves. She asks for only enough delay to prepare her soul for continued grief, while her curses invoke her future death and angry ghost.

The wretched queen, pursued by cruel fate,

Dido decides that death is her only relief and kills herself with Aeneas’s sword after lamenting his departure and cursing him. Her death is prolonged until Iris, sent by Juno, cuts the fatal hair and releases her struggling soul.

Ye realms, yet unreveal’d to human sight,

Dido’s death is reported as true, and Aeneas recognizes her among the spirits of the mournful fields with her breast bathed in blood. The text does not state the manner of her death, but Aeneas regards himself as its cause.
