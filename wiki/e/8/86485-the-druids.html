The Druids in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

The Druids were priests or ministers among the ancient Celtic nations, combining the roles of priest, magistrate, scholar, physician, and teacher. They taught one God, called Be'al and identified with the Sun, while also worshipping inferior Gods according to Latin writers. They used no images, met in sacred circles with Cromlech altars and Cairns, offered sacrifices, and reportedly practiced human sacrifice on great occasions. Their observances included Beltane, Samh'in and its judicial conclaves, and the sixth-day full moon, when they sought mistletoe. They taught morality and learning about stars, the world, lands, and gods, preserving doctrine, history, and poetry orally; Bards belonged to their hierarchy. They reached their height during the Roman invasion under Julius Caesar, then after persecution on the mainland retreated to Iona, where they resisted Columba's settlement and remained predominant until supplanted by St. Columba and Christianity.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter XXXIV

The Druids served as priests, magistrates, scholars, physicians, and teachers among the ancient Celtic nations. They worshipped a supreme deity associated with the Sun, held ceremonies in stone circles, observed festivals, practiced sacrifice, taught morality and natural philosophy, and preserved their history and poetry through oral tradition. During the Roman invasion they were persecuted, retreated to Anglesey and Iona, and were eventually supplanted by St. Columba and Christianity.
