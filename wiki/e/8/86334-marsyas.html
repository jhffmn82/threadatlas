Marsyas in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Marsyas is a satyr and musician, chiefly identified as Hyagnis’s son, though Hyginus names Œagrius as his father. He learns the flute from Hyagnis and becomes skilled enough to challenge Apollo in a trial of musical skill. Apollo overcomes him on the Tritonian reed and punishes his presumption by having him flayed alive. His body is delivered to Olympus for burial; one account instead says that, ashamed by defeat, he throws himself into the river later bearing his name.

Marsyas is a Satyr who challenges Apollo to a trial of skill on the flute. Apollo overcomes him, and Marsyas is flayed alive; his tears become the river Marsyas.

Marsyas was the son of Hyagnis, according to the main account, and learned flute playing from him. He became skilled enough to challenge Apollo, was defeated, and was flayed; some sources instead identify him as the son of Œagrius. Suidas says that, mortified by his defeat, he threw himself into the river that later bore his name.

Marsyas in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Marsyas, depicted as a handsome boy seated near Olympus, teaches him the pipe. Phrygians at Celænæ identify Marsyas with their river, credit him with inventing piping for Cybele, and say his water and melody helped repel the Galati.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXX.

Marsyas is depicted seated on a stone near Olympus, a handsome boy who is learning to play the pipe. The Phrygians at Celænæ say that the river flowing through their town was formerly Marsyas, that he invented piping in honour of Cybele, and that he helped them repulse the Galatian army with the river’s water and his melody.
