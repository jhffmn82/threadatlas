Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Neoptolemus is Achilles’ son brought from Scyros by Odysseus, who receives Achilles’ arms, kills opponents, takes Andromache, and reaches the Molossi. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Neoptolemus is Achilles’ son who must bring Philoctetes and Heracles’ bow to Troy, but ultimately rejects deceit and returns the bow. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Neoptolemus leads Polyxena to Achilles’ tomb, sacrifices her with pity, and orders her body honored. In Hecuba and other plays, Neoptolemus is Achilles’ son who takes Andromache as his wife, marries Hermione, and is killed and mutilated at Delphi through Orestes’ plot. In The Aeneid, Pyrrhus is a Greek warrior and commander of Scyrian infantry who assaults the Trojan palace, kills Polites and Priam, and is later killed by Orestes. In Heroides and Amores, Pyrrhus is Achilles’ son who possesses his father’s arms, kills Aegisthus, and forcibly takes the promised Hermione to his palace. In The Library, Neoptolemus, also called Pyrrhus, is Achilles’ son who fights at Troy, later rules the Molossians, fathers Molossus, and is killed in an alternative tradition at a sanctuary. In Description of Greece, Pyrrhus is the king of Epirus and Thesprotia, a conqueror of Antigonus, and an invader of Sparta who dies fighting at Argos.

In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Neoptolemus, also called Pyrrhus, is a relentless Greek killer of Trojans who is slain by Apollo’s priest and honored with annual funeral rites at Delphi. In The Fall of Troy, Neoptolemus is Achilles’ son who becomes a Greek warrior, commander, and leader in the war and sack of Troy. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, kills Polites before Priam and then kills Priam after Priam throws a spear at him.

Pyrrhus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Pyrrhus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Pyrrhus is a king of Epirus and Thesprotia, identified as the son of Achilles and grandson of Æacus. He was associated with a voyage from Scyrus to Sparta to marry Hermione, during which he was said to name the river Scyras; he was also linked to Pyrrhichus. After withdrawing from Italy by circulating a report of expected aid, he fought Antigonus, whose failure to send Italian reinforcements was given as the cause of their war. Pyrrhus defeated Antigonus’s forces, gained Upper Macedonia and Thessaly, and entered the Peloponnese at Cleonymus’s invitation. He invaded Sparta and nearly captured it, then died fighting at Argos. His body was burned there; his bones and shield were associated with Demeter’s temple, and he had a statue in the Altis.

A building in the Argive market-place was not a trophy over Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, as the Argives claimed, but a memorial marking the place where his body was burned. His bones were said to lie in the temple of Demeter, and his brazen shield hung over its entrance.

Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, was said to have given the river Scyras its name after landing there with his fleet on the way from Scyrus to marry Hermione. He was also associated with the naming of Pyrrhichus, and the daughters of Nereus were said to have climbed to Cardamyle to see him depart for Sparta.

Neoptolemus, son of Achilles, is also called Pyrrhus and was named by Phœnix because he was very young when he first went to war. Neoptolemus in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where Neoptolemus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Neoptolemus is Achilles’ son and Deidameia’s son, a prince living on Scyros who becomes a Greek warrior, Argive commander, and leader after Troy’s fall. He is repeatedly marked as resembling Achilles in appearance, stature, strength, courage, spirit, fame, prowess, and deeds; he inherits Achilles’ honor, renown, immortal horses, armor, chariot, and martial role. Though a beardless boy, he is battle-eager, warlike, valorous, and warrior-like. He is destined for the Elysian Plain after death. At first he awaits the Achaeans’ embassy on Scyros, where he practices dart, lance, and chariot-driving. The Achaeans send Odysseus and Tydeides/Diomede to summon him, and, despite Deidameia’s grief, he agrees to sail when called by their oracles. He leaves with Odysseus, Diomede, and twenty attendants selected by his mother. Achilles’ immortal horses, allotted to him by the Fates, await him by the ships. At Troy he wears armor made for Achilles that nonetheless fits him and protects him from missiles. Arriving while the Achaeans are hard pressed, he kills many enemies, helps save their ships, and is honored with gifts, feasting, and recognition as Achilles’ equal. He rallies and commands the Myrmidons, leads the Argives in assaults, and, assisted by Automedon driving Achilles’ chariot, repeatedly kills Trojan warriors. His major victory is defeating Eurypylus in single combat with the Pelian lance, driving the Trojans back into Troy. After battle, Thetis’ enchantment leaves him free of weariness. He mourns Achilles at his tomb, continues to lead counterattacks, and urges the Greeks not to retreat until Troy is taken and burned. He initially rejects deception in favor of open combat, but obeys Calchas after Zeus’s display of anger and enters the Wooden Horse first. During the sack he kills Priam’s sons, defeats Agenor, grants Priam death and kills him, and throws Astyanax from the wall. Finally, after Achilles’ shade urges honor, courage, courtesy, and kindness, he reports the shade’s demand to the Argives and sacrifices Polyxeina at Achilles’ tomb.
