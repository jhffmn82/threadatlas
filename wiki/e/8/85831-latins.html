Latins in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

The Latins are a people and attacking army who possess lands around the Tiber and a royal court ruled by Latinus. They preserve hospitable customs, yet war threatens them over Lavinia’s foreign marriage. Cast as enemies of Aeneas and the Trojans, they prepare arms, attack through an opened passage, and fight hand to hand; they also defend their city, reinforce its walls and passes, and seek aid. The war brings defeat, many deaths, mourning, and political division. After requesting a truce, they recover and bury their dead, while Drances opposes Turnus and others support continued resistance. Latinus proposes sharing Tiber land with the Trojans, but the dispute over peace persists. They help prepare sacred rites and lists for single combat, then, stirred by an omen and Juturna, break the treaty and resume fighting with the Rutulians. Heavy losses and the attack on their town produce flight, fear, and conflict between advocates of peace and war, intensified by the queen’s death. They finally stop fighting for the duel between Turnus and Aeneas. Under the peace settlement, they retain their name, language, and customs.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK VII

The Latins inhabit the lands around the Tiber and possess a royal court ruled by Latinus. They retain the hospitable customs attributed to Saturn’s reign, but their country is threatened with war because of Lavinia’s foreign marriage.

Now night had shed her silver dews around,

The Latins are represented as the enemy whom Aeneas is called to fight. Aeneas orders the Latian troops to prepare their arms.

Now rose the ruddy morn from Tithon’s bed,

The Latins attack through the opened passage and fight the Trojans hand to hand. Turnus kills several of them, including Antiphates, Aphidnus, Erymanthus, Meropes, and Bitias, while other Latin forces press the assault on the defenses.

BOOK XI

The Latins send suppliants with olive branches to request a truce and permission to recover their dead. They bury prominent leaders in the town, burn other dead in common heaps, and divide politically between Drances’s opposition to Turnus and Turnus’s supporters.

Thus, full of anxious thought, he summons all

The Latins are engaged in a costly war against the Trojans and have suffered deaths, mourning, and loss. Their king proposes that they share land along the Tiber with the Trojans, while Drances and Turnus debate whether they should seek peace or continue fighting.

While they debate, nor these nor those will yield,

The Latins arm in confusion when the Trojans approach. They reinforce the walls, dig trenches, raise a palisade, man the passes, and pray for aid against the enemy.

BOOK XII

The Latins participate in preparing the lists and sacred altars for the single combat. After Juturna’s agitation and the omen, they regret the treaty, take up arms again, and fight alongside the Rutulians.

When Turnus saw the Trojan quit the plain,

The Latins suffer heavy losses in the fighting and flee when Aeneas’s forces gain the advantage. Inside the city they divide between those seeking peace and those demanding war, then fall into fear and confusion after the queen’s death.

Now Turnus rolls aloof o’er empty plains,

The Latins fight against the Trojans while their town is attacked and later stop fighting to allow the duel between Turnus and Aeneas. Their king watches the champions contest the right to rule, and Jove decrees that the Latins will retain their name, language, and customs.
