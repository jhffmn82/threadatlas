Megara

place · 5 works · 11 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Megarb, Megareans, Megarians, Megaris, the city, the conquered city, them of Megara
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Megara is contested by Athens, Corinth, and Lacedaemon, briefly held by Athens, and restored to Lacedaemonian obedience by Brasidas.
  • In The Library, Megara receives Pandion after his expulsion from Athens, is given to him by Pylas, and is later captured by Minos while Nisus is king.
  • In Description of Greece, Megara is described as a place with disputed origins, Dorian inhabitants, annually chosen chief magistrates, and several citadels, temples, tombs, hero-chapels, settlements, and roads.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Megara is surrendered to the Romans without resistance after Alcamenes and his men flee to Corinth.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Megara is a city ruled by Nisus and besieged by Minos after Scylla removes its fated purple lock.

Megara in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Megara, a place contested by Athens, Corinth, and Lacedaemonians, is first subject to an Athenian sea-and-land trade embargo. Allied with Athens against Corinth, it sees Athenian victories. Athens’ decree prompts Megarean appeal to Sparta. Factional conflict follows: Athens briefly holds it, until Brasidas restores Lacedaemonian obedience.

Megara in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Megara is a place originally said to have belonged to the Athenians through a bequest to Pandion by Pylas, though evidence also numbered it among Athenians. When Codrus was king, Peloponnesians took it and gave it to Corinthians and other allies; after capture and resettlement, its inhabitants became Dorians in customs and dialect. Its name is attributed to Megareus in one tradition. After Hyperion’s death it replaced kingship with annually chosen chief magistrates. It contains Alcathous’s citadel, temples, tombs, hero-chapels, Nisæa, Pagæ, Ægosthena, and roads toward Corinth.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

Megara was said originally to have belonged to the Athenians through Pandion, but the Peloponnesians later took it and gave it to Corinthians and other allies, after which its inhabitants became Dorians in customs and dialect. The unit describes Megara's temples, monuments, rulers, traditions, and competing accounts of its name and early history.

CHAPTER XLII.

Megara contains a citadel named after Alcathous, temples of Athene, Apollo, Demeter, Dionysus, Aphrodite, and other deities, and numerous tombs and hero-chapels. Its traditions include the claim that Periboea was sent from Megara with Theseus to Crete, that Ino’s body was buried there, and that Iphigenia and Adrastus died there. Megara also possesses the towns and sites of Nisæa, Pagæ, and Ægosthena, and its road toward Corinth includes the Scironian road and the Molurian rock.
