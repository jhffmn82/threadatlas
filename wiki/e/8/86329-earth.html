Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Earth is the primeval goddess and mother of the gods, bearing many divine beings, aiding Zeus against the Titans, and bearing Typhoeus. In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Earth is a nursing mother who receives the dead and blesses Athens with flourishing life. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Earth nurtures all things and receives the armed host that arises from the dragon’s teeth when slaughter reunites it with her. In The Bacchae of Euripides, Earth is loved by the God of Heaven, brings increase, feeds children, and is associated with joy and Peace. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, heaven and earth are initially one mass with one form before being separated into the ordered world. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Terra is the posthumous divine name of Titea, mother of Uranus’s eighteen children and remembered for wisdom and beneficence. In The Library, Earth is initially without fire until fire is brought or returned, and it is later endangered by solar journeys that burn, boil, and crack it and kill many people. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Earth forms as a globe, produces and nourishes life, suffers the deluge and other catastrophes, gives oracles, and is scorched by Phaëton.

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, the world passes from its Golden Age under Innocence and Justice into successive ages of violence and corruption, while Ceres’s search harms its fertility. In The Library, Earth is a prophetic goddess and consort of Sky who bears the gods and monsters, helps Cronus overthrow Sky, foretells Zeus’s victory, and bears Typhon. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Earth is an ancient temple statue that once possessed the oracle at Delphi before passing it through Daphnis, Poseidon, Themis, and Apollo. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Earth is the heavy, flat, circular element beneath sea and heaven, later overwhelmed by Jupiter’s flood and scorched by Phaeton’s fire. In The Hungry Tiger of Oz, the Earth is a place inhabited by people, rulers, animals, and supposedly Princess Ozma, whose unfamiliar regions Atmos explores.

Earth begins without firmness, mixed with sea and air, then is separated from heaven and waters, settles beneath lighter elements, draws ponderous atoms, and is pressed together by its own gravity. It is at first free from harrow and ploughshare, producing grain and other sustenance of its own accord; later Ceres’s seeds are buried in furrows, land is divided by boundaries, and its entrails are mined for riches. Neptune opens its waters with his trident; sea covers its plains, hills, groves, fields, houses, and temples, leaving desolate silence. When waters subside, hills, ground, places, and woods reappear. As the great mother, Earth’s stones are understood as its bones. A fertile source of living forms, Earth produces animals when moisture, heat, and sun-warmed soil are properly mixed.
