Dr. Niblock and educated at Haileybury from 1839, he entered Indian civil administration after learning the language in Calcutta. His career included service at Agra and in Punjab from 1847, participation in the suppression of the Indian Mutiny, appointment as Commissioner of Rohilcund from 1858 to 1868, service on the Board of Revenue and Legislative Council of India, and office as Chief Commissioner of Oude from 1875 to 1877. Lord Lytton praised his character and abilities, though Inglis did not sympathize with Lytton’s Afghan policy. He retired after long and arduous service. In 1846 he married Harriet Louis Thompson. They had daughters, including Elsie Inglis, and he was a devoted husband and affectionate, deeply involved father. He sought the best education and equal opportunities for all three sisters, supported women’s intellectual freedom and equality in family and public life, and regarded wives as husbands’ equal companions and confidential friends. His outlook encouraged Elsie’s independence, and he calmly and practically helped her overcome obstacles when she chose medicine, supporting her professional beginnings. His faith was broad, simple, profound, and central to his life; he believed the afterlife meant continued growth and learning. After retirement, the family moved to Tasmania and then Edinburgh, where they lived at 70 Bruntsfield Place and later in Melville Street. Following Mrs. Inglis’s death, he relied increasingly on Elsie, who shared his home, walked with him, and carried his burdens. A great walker, chivalrous and high-minded, he was remembered as upright, straightforward, and highly principled. He became seriously ill while Elsie was in Dublin, welcomed her return, and died patiently on 13 March 1894. His death left Elsie unable to imagine life without him, though she continued her work and supported his extended household.

John Inglis in Dr. Elsie Inglis

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918

John Inglis was an Indian civil servant of the East India Company service, born in 1820. Taught by Rev. Dr. Niblock and educated at Haileybury from 1839, he entered Indian civil administration after learning the language in Calcutta. His career included service at Agra and in Punjab from 1847, participation in the suppression of the Indian Mutiny, appointment as Commissioner of Rohilcund from 1858 to 1868, service on the Board of Revenue and Legislative Council of India, and office as Chief Commissioner of Oude from 1875 to 1877. Lord Lytton praised his character and abilities, though Inglis did not sympathize with Lytton’s Afghan policy. He retired after long and arduous service. In 1846 he married Harriet Louis Thompson. They had daughters, including Elsie Inglis, and he was a devoted husband and affectionate, deeply involved father. He shared walks, breakfasts, letters, reading, and religious life with his daughters; sent Punch and Spectator weekly; and especially enjoyed an intellectual and affectionate fellowship with Elsie. He sought the best education and equal opportunities for all three sisters, supported women’s intellectual freedom and equality in family and public life, and regarded wives as husbands’ equal companions and confidential friends. His outlook encouraged Elsie’s independence, and he calmly and practically helped her overcome obstacles when she chose medicine, supporting her professional beginnings. His faith was broad, simple, profound, and central to his life; he believed the afterlife meant continued growth and learning. After retirement, the family moved to Tasmania and then Edinburgh, where they lived at 70 Bruntsfield Place and later in Melville Street. Following Mrs. Inglis’s death, he relied increasingly on Elsie, who shared his home, walked with him, and carried his burdens. A great walker, chivalrous and high-minded, he was remembered as upright, straightforward, and highly principled. He became seriously ill while Elsie was in Dublin, welcomed her return, and died patiently on 13 March 1894. His death left Elsie unable to imagine life without him, though she continued her work and supported his extended household.

John Inglis, born in 1820, studied at Haileybury and entered the Indian Civil Service after learning the language in Calcutta. He served at Agra and in the Punjab, took part in the suppression of the Indian Mutiny, became Commissioner of Rohilcund, served on the Board of Revenue, joined the Legislative Council of India, and was Chief Commissioner of Oude from 1875 to 1877.

Mr. Inglis was a devoted and affectionate father who shared walks, breakfasts, correspondence, and religious life with his daughters. He was especially close to Elsie, treated the three sisters equally, supported their mother’s authority, and sought equal opportunities and the best education for his daughters.

CHAPTER III THE LADDER OF LEARNING 1876-1885 ‘Hast thou come with the heart of thy childhood back: The free, the pure, the kind?

Mr. Inglis retired after serving as Chief Commissioner of Oude, having received high praise from Lord Lytton. He moved with his family to Tasmania and then Edinburgh, corresponded closely with Elsie during her Paris education, and shared a deep intellectual and affectionate fellowship with her. After Mrs. Inglis’s death, he relied on Elsie, who took on his burdens and remained his support until his death.

Mr. Inglis responded calmly and practically when Elsie Inglis announced her intention to study medicine, working to overcome the obstacles in her way. After Mrs. Inglis’s death, he moved with the family to rooms in Melville Street, where Elsie continued her studies from his home. He was a high-minded and chivalrous influence on Elsie, and she took long walks with him after her mother died.
