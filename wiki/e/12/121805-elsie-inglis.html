  • In Dr. Elsie Inglis: This is a biographical record of Elsie Maud Inglis, a woman doctor, suffrage organiser, founder and leader of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals, and wartime medical commander. Born at Naini Tal in August 1864, she grew up between India, Tasmania, Edinburgh, and Paris, close to her father John Inglis. From childhood she was intelligent, independent, observant, strong-willed, and inclined toward service. After her mother’s death she supported her father and recorded resolutions to be truthful, thorough, and responsible. She chose medicine, trained in Edinburgh and Glasgow, passed the Triple Qualification in 1892, worked at the New Hospital for Women in London and the Rotunda in Dublin, and later graduated M.B., C.M. from the University of Edinburgh. Her career combined practice, teaching, clinical study, and institution-building. In Edinburgh she practised with Dr. Jessie MacGregor, cared for poor and paying patients alike, lectured on gynecology, supported women’s medical education, founded Muir Hall for women students, and helped establish maternity and infant-welfare work. Her faith, family ties, self-command, and concern for justice informed her work. In politics she supported Liberalism, Home Rule, women’s suffrage, and women’s citizenship. As Honorary Secretary of the Scottish Federation of Suffrage Societies, she organised workers and funds, opposed militant suffragism on legal and constitutional grounds, and sacrificed professional prospects for the franchise cause. She was also an early advocate of women sanitary inspectors. At the outbreak of the First World War, after the War Office rejected women’s medical service, she founded the Scottish Women’s Hospitals with support from suffrage societies. She raised funds, organised units, and led service abroad, particularly in Serbia and later with the Serbian Army in Russia. She endured occupation, captivity, bombardment, retreats, evacuations, shortages, illness, and exhausting medical work, refusing to abandon the Serbian Army and insisting on care for patients and preservation of equipment. She died while returning to Britain in November 1917 after service connected with the Serbian evacuation from Russia. She was buried with military honors and remembered for courage, justice, kindness, pioneering leadership, and devotion to Serbia, Scotland, women’s opportunities, and medical service.

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918

This is a biographical record of Elsie Maud Inglis, a woman doctor, suffrage organiser, founder and leader of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals, and wartime medical commander. Born at Naini Tal in August 1864, she grew up between India, Tasmania, Edinburgh, and Paris, close to her father John Inglis. From childhood she was intelligent, independent, observant, strong-willed, and inclined toward service. After her mother’s death she supported her father and recorded resolutions to be truthful, thorough, and responsible. She chose medicine, trained in Edinburgh and Glasgow, passed the Triple Qualification in 1892, worked at the New Hospital for Women in London and the Rotunda in Dublin, and later graduated M.B., C.M. from the University of Edinburgh. Her career combined practice, teaching, clinical study, and institution-building. In Edinburgh she practised with Dr. Jessie MacGregor, cared for poor and paying patients alike, lectured on gynecology, supported women’s medical education, founded Muir Hall for women students, and helped establish maternity and infant-welfare work. Her faith, family ties, self-command, and concern for justice informed her work. In politics she supported Liberalism, Home Rule, women’s suffrage, and women’s citizenship. As Honorary Secretary of the Scottish Federation of Suffrage Societies, she organised workers and funds, opposed militant suffragism on legal and constitutional grounds, and sacrificed professional prospects for the franchise cause. She was also an early advocate of women sanitary inspectors. At the outbreak of the First World War, after the War Office rejected women’s medical service, she founded the Scottish Women’s Hospitals with support from suffrage societies. She raised funds, organised units, and led service abroad, particularly in Serbia and later with the Serbian Army in Russia. She endured occupation, captivity, bombardment, retreats, evacuations, shortages, illness, and exhausting medical work, refusing to abandon the Serbian Army and insisting on care for patients and preservation of equipment. She died while returning to Britain in November 1917 after service connected with the Serbian evacuation from Russia. She was buried with military honors and remembered for courage, justice, kindness, pioneering leadership, and devotion to Serbia, Scotland, women’s opportunities, and medical service.

Elsie attended school in Tasmania, the Charlotte Square Institution in Edinburgh, and later a school in Paris. She showed initiative by securing permission for girls to play in Charlotte Square gardens, edited the Edina, and was remembered for her confidence, intellect, compassion, and strong character. After her mother’s death, she became the main support of her father and resolved to be more truthful, thorough, and responsible.

Elsie Inglis recognised her vocation to study medicine and began her first medical teaching at the Edinburgh School of Medicine for Women. She opposed what she regarded as unjust treatment, including the expulsion of two students by Dr. Jex Blake, and secretly marked the examination list to refute claims that Student Christian Union members neglected their studies. During her student years she studied from her father’s home, worshipped with him, walked extensively, and developed a strong commitment to fair play and women’s causes.

Elsie Inglis went to Glasgow in 1891 to take the examination for the Triple Qualification and to obtain clinical experience unavailable to her in Edinburgh. She studied intensively, attended hospital wards and dispensaries, took part in the campaign for women’s access to mixed classes, and passed the examination with great success.

Elsie Inglis completed her clinical work in Glasgow, passed the Triple Qualification in 1892, and became house-surgeon at the New Hospital for Women in London. She worked energetically, managed wards and students, assisted with operations, wrote daily letters to her father, and hoped to establish a hospital in Edinburgh.

Elsie Inglis takes a three-month midwifery course at the Rotunda in Dublin and works extensively with patients and childbirth cases. She observes that women students are not deliberately excluded but are routinely overlooked when senior clinical work is assigned to men. She plans to return to Edinburgh, possibly to work with Miss MacGregor, but her father’s death shortly after her return leaves her feeling that life and work have lost their purpose.

Elsie Inglis supported women’s suffrage and Liberal politics and was a convinced Home Ruler. Through her medical work, she saw how husbands and laws could deny women necessary treatment, strengthening her belief that women needed the vote. She wrote and spoke about women’s rights, Home Rule, labour questions, and related political issues.

Dr. Elsie Inglis was an early woman doctor to speak at a Sanitary Congress, where she advocated employing women as sanitary inspectors. She became a leading organiser in the women’s suffrage movement, serving as Honorary Secretary of the Scottish Federation of Suffrage Societies, opposing militant suffragism, and giving her time and resources to the cause. Her colleagues remembered her as courageous, fair-minded, encouraging, disciplined, and devoted to helping the weak.

Dr. Elsie Inglis practised medicine in Edinburgh, worked in partnership with Dr. Jessie MacGregor, and graduated M.B., C.M. in 1899. She combined medical practice with political and suffrage work, founded and directed institutions for women and families, studied medicine abroad, and cared personally for poor patients and local households. She was described as indomitable, generous, practical, and deeply committed to improving women’s opportunities and public welfare.

Elsie Inglis founded and led the Scottish Women’s Hospitals, raised money for their work, and continued speaking publicly for their cause. She visited the hospital at Royaumont, later worked among the Jugoslavs in Russia, and was praised by R. W. Seton-Watson for her devotion, courage, and support for Southern Slav unity.

Dr. Elsie Inglis directed the equipment and overseas transport of the hospital units and received sustaining cooperation from the Admiralty and Foreign Office. After being refused permission to serve in Basra, she secured authorization for the Serbian enterprise and traveled with the unit via Archangel through Russia to Odessa and the Dobrudja. She continued directing the unit despite exhaustion and illness, and returned to England in 1917 after arranging its affairs and interviewing its members.

Elsie led and reported on the British medical unit during its journey through Russia, its deployment with the Serbian 1st Division, and its retreat from Medgidia. She organized hospital work, arranged evacuations, sought additional medical support, and helped care for wounded at Braila and Reni.
