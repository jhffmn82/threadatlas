Greek sculpture in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Greek sculpture is an artistic treatment of mythology and natural art that developed from primitive, decorative forms into highly natural, idealized work. Its major advances were bronze casting, marble sculpture, and chryselephantine sculpture, culminating in artists such as Myron, Polycleitos, and Pheidias. After Pheidias it moved toward elaboration, sensual feeling, characterization, action, and pathos, then declined after the Peloponnesian war and under Roman influence into dilettantism and weakness.

SKETCH OF THE HISTORY OF GREEK SCULPTURE

Greek sculpture developed from primitive and decorative forms into a highly natural and idealized art. Its major advances included bronze casting, marble chiselling, and chryselephantine work, followed by the achievements of artists such as Myron, Polycleitos, and Pheidias.

In Athens, after Pheidias, the greatest sculptures were those

Greek art moved from the limited, ideal, and reposeful expression of the golden age toward greater elaboration, sensual feeling, individual characterization, dramatic action, and pathos. After the spread of Greek influence and the conquest of Greece by Rome, it retained technical and academic perfection but lost much of its imaginative power.
