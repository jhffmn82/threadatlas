Abstract written by a model

Women’s medical education unfolds as a contested effort to secure training equal to men’s. In the 1880s and early 1890s, women faced refusal of mixed classes, restricted hospital access, difficult extra-mural arrangements, extra fees, inadequate teaching, separate lectures considered inferior, and insufficient laboratory practice. Women students nevertheless raised the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh’s required funds for clinical instruction. The movement expanded through new colleges, degrees, mixed classes, and the draft ordinance’s prospect of putting women’s education on the same footing as men’s, while remaining a subject of reform debate. Hospital training developed through service at the New Hospital for Women and the Royal Free, alongside discussion of making the former a qualifying hospital through men’s beds and a mixed staff. Elsie Inglis advocated a mixed school in Edinburgh, challenged University authorities, taught gynecology at a medical college for women, and supported institutions offering better opportunities.

Women’s medical education was hindered by the refusal of mixed classes, limited hospital teaching, difficult arrangements for extra-mural instruction, additional fees, and inadequate teaching by some professors. Women students nevertheless raised the funds demanded by the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh to obtain clinical instruction.

’ In 1891 Elsie went to Glasgow to take the examination for the Triple Qualifica

Women’s medical education was expanding through new colleges, degrees, and mixed classes. The text presents the movement as part of a broader change toward placing women’s education on the same footing as men’s.

CHAPTER V LONDON THE NEW HOSPITAL FOR WOMEN DUBLIN THE ROTUNDA 1892-1894 ‘We take up the task eternal and the burden and the lesson, Pioneers, O Pioneers.

Students from the School of Medicine served as dressers and clerks at the New Hospital for Women, although their principal hospital was the Royal Free. The hospital’s doctors discussed making it a qualifying hospital, which would require men’s beds and a mixed staff, while Elsie advocated a mixed school in Edinburgh.

Women’s medical education was affected by separate lectures that Dr. Inglis considered inferior to those offered to men and by women students’ lack of thorough laboratory practice before examinations.
