Sir John Colborne in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

Sir John Colborne is presented as commander of the forces in British North America and commander-in-chief during the Lower Canada rebellions. As militancy among the Patriotes grew in autumn 1837, he reported the threat, resolved to disperse gatherings by force, and organized a campaign against St Denis and St Charles. Despite having only a small number of troops at his disposal and facing the risk that wider revolutionary support could make it difficult to keep the flag flying, he commanded British regular forces in suppressing the rebellion. He later assembled more than two thousand troops, supported by eight field guns, for St Eustache, which he attacked on December 14. He threatened St Benoit with destruction if fired upon; although he did not directly order its burning, he was considered partly responsible through failure to control loyalist volunteers. After Gosford left Canada, Colborne took over the administration, appointed a provisional special council for Lower Canada, and left political prisoners' fate to Durham. When rebellion resumed, he advanced on and entered Napierville, sent detachments against remaining rebel bands, and suspended judges seeking civilian trials for prisoners.

Sir John Colborne, commander of the forces in British North America, reported the growing militancy of the Patriotes and determined to disperse their gatherings by force. He organized a campaign against St Denis and St Charles.

{89} But the end of rebellion was not yet in sight.

Sir John Colborne assembled more than two thousand troops and eight field guns to suppress the rebels at St Eustache. He threatened St Benoit with destruction if a shot were fired, did not order its burning, but was nevertheless judged partly responsible for failing to control the loyalist volunteers.

It is usual to regard the rebellion of 1837 in Lower Canada as no less a fiasco

Sir John Colborne had only a small number of troops at his disposal during the rebellion. If the revolutionary leaders had gained broader support, he might have found it difficult to keep the flag flying.

{104} CHAPTER X THE LORD HIGH COMMISSIONER The rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada profoundly affected public opinion in the mother country.

Sir John Colborne, commander-in-chief of the forces, took over the administration after Gosford left Canada. He appointed a provisional special council and left the fate of the political prisoners for Durham to decide.
