The Fall of Troy

The Fall of Troy is a poem by Quintus Smyrnaeus that narrates events between Hector's funeral in The Iliad and the taking of Troy. It covers the deaths of Penthesileia, Memnon, and Achilles; the funeral games and dispute over Achilles' arms; the arrivals of Eurypylus, the son of Achilles, and Philoctetes; and the deaths of Eurypylus and Paris. Its final books describe the Wooden Horse, the taking and sack of Troy, and the conquerors' storm-filled voyage home. The introduction discusses Quintus Smyrnaeus's date, sources, relationship to Homer and the Cyclic Poets, and differences from The Iliad and The Odyssey.

BOOK I:

After Hector's death, Penthesileia arrives at Troy from Thermodon with twelve Amazon princesses, seeking purification from the accidental killing of her sister Hippolyte and eager for battle. Priam welcomes her and hopes she will save Troy, while Penthesileia promises to defeat Achilles and destroy the Argive forces. Athena sends her a deceptive dream urging her to fight Achilles, and Penthesileia leads the Trojans and Amazons into battle after arming herself with weapons given by Ares. She kills several Argive warriors and drives the Greeks back, but Priam foresees that she will not return alive, and Achilles and Ajax have not yet heard of the fighting.

But on the grave-mound of Menoetius' son

Penthesileia leads the Amazons in a devastating assault that drives the Greeks back toward their ships. The women of Troy consider joining the fighting, but Theano persuades them to remain behind the walls. Achilles and Aias enter the battle, break the Trojan and Amazon ranks, and Achilles kills Penthesileia. Her death causes the Trojans to flee, while Ares descends to avenge her but is restrained by Zeus.

Then did the warrior sons of Argos strip

Achilles mourns the slain Amazon queen Penthesileia, but kills Thersites after he mocks his pity and love for her. Diomedes becomes angry because Thersites is his kinsman, and the two greatest Argive warriors nearly fight before their comrades reconcile them. Atreus' sons return Penthesileia's body to the Trojans, who burn and bury her with royal honors alongside Laomedon, while the Amazons and Argive dead receive graves. The Argives also honor Podarces with a great mound, bury Thersites separately, and feast in Agamemnon's tent through the night.

BOOK II

Troy fears destruction after Achilles kills Hector, but Priam urges the city to hold out for Memnon and his Aethiopian host. Memnon arrives, is welcomed by Priam, and leads the Trojans and their allies into battle against the Achaeans. Memnon kills Antilochus and drives the Argives back in a devastating slaughter, while Achilles also kills many Trojan warriors. The gods foresee great losses but are forbidden by Zeus to intervene.

Then to the side of Aeacus' mighty son

Nestor tells Achilles that Memnon has slain Antilochus, and Achilles confronts Memnon in battle. The two heroes fight with extraordinary strength until Achilles kills Memnon, whose body is carried away by the winds while his Aethiopian comrades are changed into birds. Eos mourns her son and threatens to withdraw her light, but Zeus's thunder and the Hours bring her back to her daily course. The Trojans and Argives continue grieving amid the slaughter, while Memnon's birds circle and lament around his tomb.

BOOK III

After Antilochus is buried, Achilles attacks the Trojans in vengeance and nearly reaches Troy before Apollo strikes him with a concealed shaft. Achilles removes the shaft but weakens and dies near the city, fulfilling the prophecy that he would perish by Apollo's arrows before the Scaean Gates. The Trojans attempt to seize his corpse, but Aias defends it and, aided by Odysseus and other Argives, drives the Trojans back to Troy. Paris is wounded and carried into the city, while Aeneas escapes wounded and the surviving Trojans leave the battlefield strewn with dead.

So the kings drew him from that stricken field

Achilles is mourned by the Achaeans after being slain by a god's arrow, and his body is washed, dressed, displayed, and lamented by warriors, captives, Thetis, the Daughters of Nereus, and the Muses. Aias, Phoenix, Agamemnon, and Briseis each grieve for Achilles while recalling his strength, kindness, family, or protection. The Achaeans burn Achilles with lavish offerings, gather his bones in a golden vase, and raise a barrow beside the Hellespont. Poseidon tells Thetis that Achilles will not remain among the dead but will become a god on an island in the Euxine Sea, while Achilles' immortal horses wait for Neoptolemus.

BOOK IV

After Achilles’ death, the Argives mourn beside their ships while the Trojans rejoice, though some Trojans fear the surviving Achaean champions. Zeus foresees Troy’s eventual destruction despite Hera’s reproach, and the Argives prepare to honor Achilles with funeral games. Thetis arrives from the sea, sets out athlete prizes, and oversees contests including a footrace, wrestling, and boxing. Aias of the Locrians wins the footrace, Aias son of Telamon defeats Diomedes in wrestling, and Epeius and Acamas fight to a draw before accepting silver bowls as prizes.

Then for the archery-test Oileus' son

At Achilles' funeral-feast, athletic contests continue, including archery, throwing the iron bar, leaping, javelin-casting, wrestling, chariot racing, and horse racing. Aias wins the iron-bar contest and receives the arms of Memnon, while Teucer wins the archery-test and receives the arms of Troilus. Agamemnon wins the chariot race, and Sthenelus's horse wins the horse race after swerving from the course. Thetis distributes many prizes, while the Argives celebrate the competitors and Laertes grieves that his wound prevents him from competing.

BOOK V

Thetis presents Achilles' immortal arms as a prize for the Argive warrior who rescued Achilles' body. Aias and Odysseus claim the arms, but Trojan captives judge Odysseus the victor after the two heroes argue over their battlefield deeds. Aias is overwhelmed by grief and madness, imagining vengeance against the Argives and Odysseus before Athena turns his fury aside. In his madness, Aias attacks and kills sheep, mistaking them for his enemies, until Athena removes the delusion from his mind.

Then Aias saw those sheep upon the earth

Aias realizes that the gods deceived his senses and made him slaughter sheep instead of his enemies, and he kills himself with Hector's sword. Teucer, Tecmessa, and the Danaans mourn him, while Agamemnon promises protection for Tecmessa and her son. Odysseus regrets his quarrel with Aias and attributes the disaster to wrath and fate. The Argives burn Aias's body, gather his bones in a golden casket, and bury them in a great mound near Rhoeteium.

BOOK VI

Menelaus tests the Achaean men by proposing that they flee Troy, but Tydeides rejects the counsel and Calchas urges that Achilles' son be brought from Scyros. Odysseus and Tydeides sail toward Scyros, while Eurypylus, grandson of Hercules and leader of a host from the Caicus region, arrives to aid Troy. Eurypylus leads the Trojans into battle and kills Nireus and Machaon, after which the Argives fight to recover their bodies.

By this to Podaleirius tidings came

Podaleirius learns that his brother has been struck down and leaves his healing work beside the ships to fight, killing Cleitus and Lassus. The Danaans rescue Machaon and Aglaia's son but suffer heavy losses before Eurypylus drives them back toward the ships. Aias, the Atreidae, and other leaders resist until reinforcements rally, but Eurypylus renews the Trojan assault and causes a widespread flight. The Trojans would have burned the ships if night had not intervened, and both sides withdraw after a day of severe slaughter.

BOOK VII

After Machaon and Nireus are buried, Eurypylus leads the Ceteians and Trojans in a devastating assault that drives the Argives behind the ships. Odysseus and Diomede travel to Scyros and persuade Achilles' son to come to Troy by promising him Achilles' armor, gifts, and a royal marriage. Deidameia grieves and warns her son against the war, while he insists that he must follow his destiny and win glory worthy of Achilles. Achilles' son finally leaves Scyros with Odysseus, Diomede, and twenty attendants, amid prayers for his safe return.

So came they to the heavy-plunging sea,

Neoptolemus sails to Troy with Odysseus, Diomede, and other Achaeans, arriving while Eurypylus and the Trojans are attacking the Achaean defensive wall. Wearing Achilles' armor, Neoptolemus leads a fierce counterattack, kills many Trojans, and forces Eurypylus's host to withdraw. Phoenix recognizes him as the image of Achilles, while the Achaeans honor him with gifts and a feast. Deidameia remains behind grieving as the ship departs, and Neoptolemus later mourns Achilles in his father's tent.

BOOK VIII

Neoptolemus leads the Argives into battle, wearing Achilles' armor and driving his father's immortal horses. He kills many Trojans and defeats Eurypylus, the giant son of Telephus, with the Pelian lance. Ares comes to the aid of Troy, but Athena confronts him and Zeus's thunder forces both gods to withdraw. The Argives then drive the Trojans into Troy and begin a fierce assault on its walls, with many killed on both sides.

Now had the Argives burst the gates, had breached

Ganymedes, fearing for Troy and his fatherland, pleads with Zeus, who veils Priam's city and its walls in a vast mist and signals his wrath with thunder and lightning. Neleus' son orders the Argives to withdraw to the ships, warning them of Zeus's power and recalling the destruction once inflicted on the Titans. The Argives bury their dead, wash in the Hellespont, and later praise Achilles' valiant son, while both armies keep watch through the night. Calchas' prophecy that Troy would fall in the tenth year gives the Argives hope of victory despite their retreat.

BOOK IX

The Greeks and Trojans renew their battle before Troy after the Trojans burn their dead, and Deiphobus rallies Troy's defenders. Neoptolemus devastates the Trojan ranks while Deiphobus slaughters many Argives, until Apollo rescues Deiphobus from Neoptolemus and Poseidon restrains Apollo from killing Achilles' son. Thetis rejoices in Neoptolemus' victories, while Zeus allows the fighting to continue but does not fulfill Antenor's prayer to remove Achilles' son. The battle remains murderous after Apollo and Poseidon withdraw, with countless warriors dead on both sides.

At last by Calchas' counsel Achaea's sons

At Calchas' counsel, the Achaean sons seek Philoctetes on Lemnos because Ilium cannot fall without his aid. Odysseus and Diomede find Philoctetes suffering in a cave, persuade him to forgive his abandonment, and bring him back to the Achaean host. Podaleirius heals Philoctetes' wound, and Agamemnon offers gifts and honors in atonement. Restored to strength, Philoctetes urges the Achaeans to prepare for war against Troy.

BOOK X

The Trojans leave Troy’s walls to fight the Achaeans, and Philoctetes kills Paris after wounding him with poisoned arrows. Paris goes to Oenone on Mount Ida, begging her to heal him, but she refuses because he abandoned her for Helen. Paris dies on Ida, while Oenone later repents and grieves for him. The battle continues until nightfall, and the text reveals further omens of Troy’s coming ruin.

Then, while her father and her maidens slept,

Oenone leaves while her father and maidens sleep and rushes through the mountains to find the pyre of Paris, also called Alexander. She leaps onto the burning pyre and dies clasping Paris, while the Nymphs and herdmen watch in astonishment. The Nymphs condemn Paris for abandoning his faithful wife for a woman who brought a curse on him and Troy. After the fire consumes them, their bones are placed in a golden vase beneath a mound marked by two pillars that turn away from each other.

BOOK XI

The Trojans and Achaeans fight fiercely outside Troy after Paris's death, with many warriors slain on both sides. Neoptolemus, Aeneas, Eurymachus, and other named fighters lead attacks, while Apollo strengthens Aeneas and Eurymachus and Athena helps the Greeks prevail. Aphrodite rescues Aeneas from the battle, and the Trojans retreat into Troy while the Greeks press them. The surviving warriors return wounded to Troy and the Greek camps as Fate continues to govern the war.

When o'er the streams of Ocean Dawn drove up

At dawn, the Argives attack Troy at several gates while the Trojans defend the city walls. Odysseus leads the Argives in a tightly locked shield-wall, but Aeneas, aided by Ares, breaks their formations and hurls stones against them. Aeneas kills Alcimedon and Toxaechmes, while Philoctetes' companion Medon is slain after an arrow aimed at Aeneas is diverted by Cytherea. The battle continues without respite as Achilles' son urges the Argives to persist and Aeneas rallies the Trojans to defend their homes.

BOOK XII

Calchas advises the Achaeans to abandon a direct assault and use a stratagem, and Odysseus proposes constructing a great Wooden Horse to conceal warriors while the army sails to Tenedos. With Athena's aid, Epeius builds the Horse, and Achilles' son, Menelaus, Odysseus, and other leading Achaeans hide inside it while Nestor and Agamemnon oversee the fleet's departure. Sinon remains beside the Horse and deceives the Trojans, while Laocoon warns them to destroy it but is blinded by Athena. The Trojans drag the Horse into Troy, breaking down part of their walls to admit it, despite Laocoon's continued warning.

But then a yet more hideous punishment

Athena sends two serpents from Calydna to Troy, and they kill Laocoon's sons while Laocoon is left helpless and blind. Omens throughout Troy signal impending destruction, but the Trojans ignore them and continue their final feast. Cassandra correctly recognizes the danger of the Argive Horse and tries to destroy it, yet the Trojans mock her and prevent her. The Achaeans hidden inside the horse rejoice at the Trojans' disbelief, while Cassandra flees in anguish over Troy's fate.

BOOK XIII

Sinon signals the Argive forces, who emerge from the Trojan Horse and open the way for the returning Danaans to enter Troy. The Danaans and Trojans fight amid widespread fire, slaughter, and enslavement, while Priam, Astyanax, and many others are killed and Andromache is taken captive. Aeneas escapes the burning city carrying Anchises and leading his son, protected by Aphrodite and spared at Calchas's warning. Antenor's household is spared because of his former hospitality to Menelaus and Odysseus.

Then also Menelaus in Helen's bower

Menelaus kills Deiphobus in Helen's bower and nearly kills Helen, but Aphrodite removes his jealousy and his brother persuades him to spare her. The Argives ravage Troy, whose people die in battle, fire, collapsing houses, suicide, and despair, while the Gods and Fate allow the city to fall. Demophoon and Acamas recognize Aethra, their grandmother, and promise to return her to Greece. Laodice is swallowed by the earth, while Electra hides herself from the Pleiades after Troy's destruction.

BOOK XIV.

The Argives sack Troy, enslave its women, celebrate victory, and prepare to sail home with their plunder. Menelaus reconciles with Helen, while Achilles' son receives a command from Achilles' shade to sacrifice Polyxeina at Achilles' tomb. After the sacrifice, the sea becomes calm, but Calchas warns that danger awaits the Argives near the Capherean Rocks. The Argive ships leave Troy and reach the waters of Hellas, while Athena's wrath threatens their return.

When nigh Euboea's windy heights they drew,

Athena asks Zeus to punish the Achaeans because Oileus' son violated her temple and wronged Cassandra, and Zeus gives her his weapons. Athena summons a devastating storm through Aeolus, while Zeus and Poseidon help destroy the Achaean fleet and its crews near Euboea and Caphereus. Aias survives the wreck for a time, but Poseidon destroys the rock to which he clings and buries him. Nauplius also deceives and destroys Achaeans on the reefs, and Poseidon, Zeus, and Apollo later erase the Achaean walls at Troy.
