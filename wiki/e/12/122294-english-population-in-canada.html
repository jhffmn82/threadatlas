English population in Canada in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

The English population in Canada was a largely commercial group that, after the conquest, generally lived with French Canadians in peace and amity for a hundred and fifty years. In 1805, its minority supported a direct land tax, opposed extra customs duties, and protested what it saw as French-Canadian domination. During the War of 1812–1814, English Canadians fought beside French-Canadian militiamen. They supplied most members of the two councils and dominated the governing class called the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique, prompting French-Canadian fears of favoritism and subordination under the Union Bill of 1822. During the Rebellion, the groups opposed one another; many English Canadians were described as arrogant and brutal toward French Canadians and lawless in crushing the rebellion.

{1} CHAPTER I CANADIANS, OLD AND NEW The conquest of Canada by British arms in the Seven Years' War gave rise to a situation in the colony which was fraught with tragic possibilities.

English Canadians generally lived peacefully with French Canadians after the conquest. During the War of 1812–1814, they fought alongside French-Canadian militiamen, but during the Rebellion in Canada the two groups were arrayed against each other.

{13} CHAPTER III 'THE REIGN OF TERROR' During the session of 1805 the Assembly was confronted with the apparently innocent problem of building prisons.

The English Canadians, whose interests were generally commercial, advocated a direct tax on land and opposed the French proposal for additional customs duties. Their minority accused the French Canadians of imposing the burden of taxation and demanded an end to French-Canadian domination.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

The English population in Canada supplied most of the members of the two councils and dominated the governing class described as the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique. French Canadians believed that the English element was being favored in government appointments and that the Union Bill of 1822 threatened to subordinate them to it.
