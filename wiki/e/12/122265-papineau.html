  • In The 'Patriotes' of '37: Papineau was a French-Canadian political leader, former militia major, and leading figure of the Patriote party in Lower Canada. He entered the House of Assembly in 1812 at twenty-six, became speaker three years later after J. A. Panet resigned, and guided the Patriotes from the speaker’s chair. Contemporaries credited him with eloquence, while the records portray him as highly influential, unbending, domineering, and lacking compromise. Although Christie said he moved with French Canadians rather than led them, he acquired a large, powerful following and dominated the Assembly through his majority and personal ascendancy. He opposed the government in budget struggles, declined a seat on the Executive Council despite protesting the lack of French-Canadian executive representation, helped defeat the Union Bill of 1822, and attacked Lord Dalhousie and Lord Aylmer. As conflict intensified, he used the Assembly to expel or imprison opponents, opposed judicial independence legislation after amendments, rejected the constitution of 1791, admired the American constitution, and made an elective Legislative Council the chief Patriote demand. He inspired the Ninety-Two Resolutions, whose revolutionary language and call for Aylmer’s impeachment weakened their appeal before the British government. Strengthened by election support and backed by seventy members as speaker, he rejected a royal commission as an insult, refused a permanent civil list, denounced the Russell resolutions, and called for Canadian rights. He sought emancipation from British rule and republican government, but in 1835 disclaimed civil war and repeatedly hesitated to endorse armed rebellion. Younger associates’ enthusiasm carried him along in late 1837. Subject to an arrest warrant after leaving Montreal, he took refuge near the Richelieu, was present when the battle at St Denis began, departed for St Hyacinthe, and escaped to Vermont. During the rebellion he was reported to have conveyed information about Wolfred Nelson and gave Amury Girod responsibility for the Patriote cause in the north. After exile, much of it spent in Paris, he returned to Canada in 1845, entered parliament in 1847, and served until 1854. He was out of harmony with responsible government, later encouraged radical and anti-clerical ideas, retired to La Petite Nation at Montebello, and retained an enduring love for Canada as he believed it should be loved.

Papineau in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

Papineau was a French-Canadian political leader, former militia major, and leading figure of the Patriote party in Lower Canada. He entered the House of Assembly in 1812 at twenty-six, became speaker three years later after J. A. Panet resigned, and guided the Patriotes from the speaker’s chair. Contemporaries credited him with eloquence, while the records portray him as highly influential, unbending, domineering, and lacking compromise. Although Christie said he moved with French Canadians rather than led them, he acquired a large, powerful following and dominated the Assembly through his majority and personal ascendancy. He opposed the government in budget struggles, declined a seat on the Executive Council despite protesting the lack of French-Canadian executive representation, helped defeat the Union Bill of 1822, and attacked Lord Dalhousie and Lord Aylmer. As conflict intensified, he used the Assembly to expel or imprison opponents, opposed judicial independence legislation after amendments, rejected the constitution of 1791, admired the American constitution, and made an elective Legislative Council the chief Patriote demand. He inspired the Ninety-Two Resolutions, whose revolutionary language and call for Aylmer’s impeachment weakened their appeal before the British government. Strengthened by election support and backed by seventy members as speaker, he rejected a royal commission as an insult, refused a permanent civil list, denounced the Russell resolutions, and called for Canadian rights. He sought emancipation from British rule and republican government, but in 1835 disclaimed civil war and repeatedly hesitated to endorse armed rebellion. Younger associates’ enthusiasm carried him along in late 1837. Subject to an arrest warrant after leaving Montreal, he took refuge near the Richelieu, was present when the battle at St Denis began, departed for St Hyacinthe, and escaped to Vermont. During the rebellion he was reported to have conveyed information about Wolfred Nelson and gave Amury Girod responsibility for the Patriote cause in the north. {21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

Louis Joseph Papineau entered the House of Assembly for Lower Canada in 1812 at age twenty-six and was elected speaker three years later. He guided the Patriote party from the speaker's chair, opposed the government during the budget struggles, helped defeat the Union Bill of 1822, and later attacked Lord Dalhousie. The text portrays him as eloquent and influential but also unbending and lacking a spirit of compromise.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity.

Papineau became increasingly violent and domineering in the Assembly of Lower Canada, using his majority to expel or imprison opponents and threatening Quesnel with the loss of his seat. By 1832 he had rejected the constitution of 1791, admired the United States constitution, and made an elective Legislative Council the central demand of his party. He inspired the Ninety-Two Resolutions, though their revolutionary language and demand for Lord Aylmer's impeachment weakened their case before the British government.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

Papineau's position was strengthened by the election, and he was supported by seventy members as speaker of the Assembly. He attacked Lord Aylmer, rejected confidence that Britain would redress Canadian grievances, opposed granting a permanent civil list, and did not yet advocate civil war.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid.

Papineau addressed major Patriote meetings at St Ours, St Laurent, and St Charles, denouncing the Russell resolutions and calling for the preservation of Canadian rights. He desired emancipation from British rule and republican government but repeatedly hesitated to endorse armed rebellion.

] {69} CHAPTER VIII THE DOGS OF WAR As the autumn of 1837 wore on, the situation in Lower Canada began to assume an aspect more and more threatening.

Louis-Joseph Papineau attended public meetings and was escorted by large crowds, while opposition to his movement provoked hostility toward loyalists and Chouayens. After leaving Montreal for St Hyacinthe, he became subject to an arrest warrant and took refuge near the Richelieu with other Patriote leaders. He was present at St Denis when the battle began but left for St Hyacinthe and later fled to the United States.

{89} But the end of rebellion was not yet in sight.

Papineau was said to have reported that Wolfred Nelson was entrenched with a considerable force at St Cesaire. Amury Girod also appears to have received credentials from Papineau assigning him responsibility for the Patriote cause in the north.

{128} CHAPTER XII A POSTSCRIPT The rebellion of 1837 now belongs to the dead past.

Papineau returned to Canada in 1845 after spending much of his exile in Paris, where he encountered the red republicans who later supported the revolution of 1848. He entered parliament in 1847 and sat until 1854, but was out of harmony with responsible government; later, he encouraged radical and anti-clerical ideas, retired to La Petite Nation at Montebello, and expressed his enduring love for Canada.
