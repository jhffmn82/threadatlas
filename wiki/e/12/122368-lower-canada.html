Lower Canada in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

Lower Canada was a predominantly French province and British colony, created in 1791 alongside Upper Canada. Its first Parliament met in 1792, and its elected Assembly recognized French as well as English in its proceedings and publications. Yet it was governed through a governor, Executive Council, and Legislative Council that were not responsible to that Assembly, leaving constitutional power, public money, and appointments at the center of political conflict between French Canadians and an English-dominated governing class. Early disputes over prison funding, the rise of Le Canadien, and Sir James Craig’s repression of French-Canadian opposition revealed these tensions. Under Sir George Prevost’s conciliatory policy, it showed less disloyalty than Upper Canada during the War of 1812. After 1830, racial and political conflict sharpened amid criticism of the Legislative Council, public offices, wild lands, finances, and elections. Lord Gosford’s 1835 commission did not resolve the crisis: the Assembly rejected compromise on revenues and the civil list after learning that the British government opposed an elective Legislative Council. In 1836-37, the Assembly withheld supplies while the Patriotes demanded major constitutional change. Meetings, boycotts, and armed companies spread agitation. By autumn 1837, military drills, intimidation of loyalists, rioting, the Chambly–Longueuil skirmish, and fighting at St Denis and St Charles led to rebellion. A second rebellion followed, including an intended independent republic proclaimed by Robert Nelson, with activity at Napierville, Lacolle, Odelltown, and Beauharnois.

Lower Canada was the French province where the engagement at Chateauguay was fought. It was also the setting for the later fighting between French-Canadian rebels and British forces at St Denis, St Charles, and St Eustache.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

Lower Canada was governed through institutions in which the governor, Executive Council, and Legislative Council were not responsible to the elected Assembly. Its political life was marked by conflict between French Canadians and the English-dominated governing class over constitutional power, finances, and appointments.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity.

Lower Canada experienced increasing racial and political conflict after 1830. Its government and institutions were criticized for abuses involving the Legislative Council, public offices, wild lands, finances, and elections.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

Lower Canada received Lord Gosford as governor and chief royal commissioner in 1835. Its Assembly rejected the proposed compromise over revenues and the civil list after learning that the British government opposed an elective Legislative Council.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid.

Lower Canada faced a political and financial crisis in 1836-37 as its Assembly refused supplies and the Patriotes demanded major constitutional changes. Agitation spread through the province, including meetings, boycotts, and the organization of armed Patriote companies.

] {69} CHAPTER VIII THE DOGS OF WAR As the autumn of 1837 wore on, the situation in Lower Canada began to assume an aspect more and more threatening.

Lower Canada became increasingly threatening during autumn 1837 as the Patriotes held military drills, armed bands intimidated loyalists, and plans for rebellion advanced. Conflict escalated through rioting, the Chambly–Longueuil skirmish, and fighting at St Denis and St Charles.

Lower Canada experienced a rebellion in 1837 that was judged no less a fiasco than the rebellion in Upper Canada. The movement might have become more serious if it had received broader support.
