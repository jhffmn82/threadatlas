  • In Dr. Elsie Inglis: Scottish Women’s Hospitals was a women-run wartime medical organisation for foreign service, created through Scottish suffrage organisation and run as a Scottish Federation scheme. Formed after the War Office rejected Elsie Maud Inglis’s offer of service, it organised skilled women, raised funds, and planned efficient medical units, motor ambulances, and rest stations wherever needed. The National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies supported it through appeals, press notices, and fundraising, although public speakers were sometimes asked not to mention the suffrage cause that had supplied its organisation and workers. Its units, equipped and staffed entirely by women, sent doctors, nurses, supplies, personnel, and equipment overseas during the First World War. They worked in France, Belgium, Serbia, Corsica, and Russia, including at Royaumont, and provided help to Jugoslavs. In Serbia they served in hospitals, infectious-disease units, and disinfecting centres amid typhus, typhoid, famine fevers, and other suffering. Members continued work during the typhus epidemic despite illness and deaths in one unit. The organisation was identified by the Union Jack displayed with the red, white and green flag. Dr. Elsie Inglis organised the Hospitals and personally led work in Serbia in 1915. The organisation gave her intense surgical experience, including long periods at the operating table, and employed her abroad during wartime. Its work depended on home committees handling finance, personnel, equipment, transport, government relations, and other logistical obstacles. Its final enterprise was a hospital unit and transport section for the Serbian Army, initially staffed by eighty women. It operated a hospital at Reni for eight months before that hospital closed and the unit rejoined the Serbian Division. The Hospitals then served with the Serbian army in Russia. Inglis’s final message urged the London Committee to ensure that the Serbs received their hospitals and transport. Dedicated to Serbia and described as having served and loved its brethren from 1914 to 1917, the Scottish Women’s Hospitals became one of Inglis’s living memorials and were represented at her funeral by colleagues, officials, and units she had led.

Scottish Women’s Hospitals in Dr. Elsie Inglis

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918

Scottish Women’s Hospitals was a women-run wartime medical organisation for foreign service, created through Scottish suffrage organisation and run as a Scottish Federation scheme. Formed after the War Office rejected Elsie Maud Inglis’s offer of service, it organised skilled women, raised funds, and planned efficient medical units, motor ambulances, and rest stations wherever needed. The National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies supported it through appeals, press notices, and fundraising, although public speakers were sometimes asked not to mention the suffrage cause that had supplied its organisation and workers. Its units, equipped and staffed entirely by women, sent doctors, nurses, supplies, personnel, and equipment overseas during the First World War. They worked in France, Belgium, Serbia, Corsica, and Russia, including at Royaumont, and provided help to Jugoslavs. In Serbia they served in hospitals, infectious-disease units, and disinfecting centres amid typhus, typhoid, famine fevers, and other suffering. Members continued work during the typhus epidemic despite illness and deaths in one unit. The organisation was identified by the Union Jack displayed with the red, white and green flag. Dr. Elsie Inglis organised the Hospitals and personally led work in Serbia in 1915. The organisation gave her intense surgical experience, including long periods at the operating table, and employed her abroad during wartime. Its work depended on home committees handling finance, personnel, equipment, transport, government relations, and other logistical obstacles. Its final enterprise was a hospital unit and transport section for the Serbian Army, initially staffed by eighty women. It operated a hospital at Reni for eight months before that hospital closed and the unit rejoined the Serbian Division. The Hospitals then served with the Serbian army in Russia. Inglis’s final message urged the London Committee to ensure that the Serbs received their hospitals and transport. Dedicated to Serbia and described as having served and loved its brethren from 1914 to 1917, the Scottish Women’s Hospitals became one of Inglis’s living memorials and were represented at her funeral by colleagues, officials, and units she had led.
