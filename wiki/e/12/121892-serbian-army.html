Serbian army in Dr. Elsie Inglis

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918

The Serbian army is an army closely linked to the Scottish Women’s Hospitals and Dr. Elsie Inglis. It had defeated its enemy in 1914 and was regarded by the Serbs as uniquely successful among the Great Powers’ forces. In 1915, facing Austrians, Germans, Magyars, and Bulgarians at about four times its numbers, it shortened its defensive line but could not resist German guns and the combined invasion. During the retreat, 160,000 soldiers escaped, while hospitals filled with wounded and streams of soldiers, stragglers, and refugees moved on. The Scottish Women’s Hospitals served the army with a fully equipped unit, treating wounded during the retreat; Inglis chose to return with the Serbian Army rather than leave it. On the Rumanian Russian front, Serbian wounded were evacuated to Odessa, and Russian officials and officers praised the soldiers’ courage and endurance. Soldiers in the Division were described as magnificent, disciplined fighting men able to stiffen Russian troops, while hospital orderlies were quick, devoted sanitaries. Later fighting after the Rumanians broke caused severe losses: 11,000 men from a division of 15,000. Serbian representatives travelled with Inglis and her unit and were to be brought away from the Russian front.

The Serbian army was fighting on the Bulgarian front when the National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies supported sending Elsie Maud Inglis with a fully equipped unit. The Scottish Women’s Hospitals later provided medical service connected with Serbia.

opening

The Serbian army had defeated its enemy in 1914 and was regarded by the Serbs as the only army among the Great Powers’ forces to have done so. In 1915 it shortened its defensive line but could not withstand the German long-distance guns; 160,000 men escaped during the retreat.

’ As the fever died out, a worse enemy came in.

The Serbian army was unable to resist the combined Austrians, Germans, Magyars, and Bulgarians, who were about four times its numbers. Its retreat left hospitals full of wounded and produced streams of soldiers, stragglers, and refugees.

CHAPTER X RUSSIA 1917 ‘Even so in our mortal journey, The bitter north winds blow, And thus upon life’s red river, Our hearts as oarsmen row.

The Serbian Army was served by the Scottish Women’s Hospitals unit on the Rumanian Russian front. During the retreat, the unit treated wounded soldiers, and Dr. Inglis insisted that she would return with the Serbian Army rather than leave it.

‘NEARING ODESSA, ‘_Sep.

The Serbs received strong support and admiration in Russia, and Serbian officers and soldiers helped the unit during its journey and operations. Serbian wounded were evacuated to Odessa, while Russian officials and officers praised the Serbian soldiers for their courage and endurance.

‘_On Active Service.

Inglis says that Serbs, including the soldiers in the Division, are regarded as magnificent, disciplined fighting men whose presence could stiffen Russian troops. Serbian orderlies in the hospital are described as quick, devoted, and a great comfort.

’ CHAPTER XI THE MOORINGS CUT ‘Not I, but my Unit.

The Serbian Army suffered severe losses in fighting after the Rumanians broke, with 11,000 men lost from a division of 15,000. Representatives of the Serbian Army travelled with Dr. Inglis and her unit, and she insisted that they be brought away from the Russian front.
