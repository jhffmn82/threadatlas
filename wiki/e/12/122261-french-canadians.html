  • In The 'Patriotes' of '37: French Canadians are a Roman Catholic population in Canada whose position under British rule, political struggles, and defense of national identity shape the record. After Canada’s conquest by British arms, they were governed by people differing in language, religion, laws, temperament, and political outlook. Comparatively few left; they received guarantees of property, civil rights, and free religious exercise, and for a hundred and fifty years generally dwelt with English Canadians in peace and amity. They remained at least passively loyal to British rule during the invasion, resisted appeals from the Continental Congress, supported British rule during the Napoleonic wars, and were loyal to the British crown in the wars of 1775 and 1812. As they became more jealous of their national identity, they pressed for wider liberties. Mainly agrarian, they opposed a direct tax on land and secured extra customs duties instead. They created Le Canadien in 1806 to express their majority position in the House of Assembly and defend their language, institutions, and laws. Politically dominant over English Canadians in the province and a majority in the House of Assembly, they sought protection for their laws, religion, privileges, and national existence. They opposed the Union Bill of 1822, demanded an elective Legislative Council, and resented discrimination by an English-dominated governing class, including financial abuses, favoritism, and obstruction by the Legislative Council. Racial conflict intensified after 1830. French Canadians made up the three killed and two wounded in the 1832 Montreal riot. Though some backed Papineau, moderates known as Chouayens rejected his extreme course. Lord Gosford addressed them with promises against discrimination in appointments and for their native tongue, but British instructions undermined these assurances. Their agitation sought greater autonomy, and some speakers advocated separation; Daniel O'Connell defended them in parliamentary debate as victims of Anglo-Saxon tyranny and bigotry. Yet the majority opposed the rebellion of 1837 in obedience to the Church, helping make it easily suppressed. Finally, Durham’s proposed union threatened to make the French population a minority and anglicize it by absorption, endangering French-Canadian national identity.
