Patriote party in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

The Patriote party was a French-Canadian political movement representing the French-Canadian majority in the Assembly against the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique. Guided by Papineau, it resisted permanent financial settlements that would restrict Assembly control and demanded administrative reform. It included French- and English-speaking supporters, including Louis Hippolyte LaFontaine, Augustin Norbert Morin, and Wolfred Nelson, but divisions emerged: its extreme wing adopted the Ninety-Two Resolutions, while Neilson, Cuvillier, Quesnel, Stuart, Bedard, and Parent broke from Papineau. The party won the subsequent general election, defeating several members who had opposed the Resolutions. By 1836–37, it organized meetings throughout Lower Canada, denounced the Russell resolutions as coercion, boycotted English goods for Canadian products, and moved toward republicanism, separation, and independence from British rule. It formed the Fils de la Liberte in Montreal and volunteer companies, then continued military drills and musketry practice despite prohibition. Armed bands operated in the countryside, resisted arrests, intimidated loyalists, and fought under leaders including Wolfred Nelson and Thomas Storrow Brown. They defeated Colonel Charles Gore at St Denis, though some Patriotes murdered Lieutenant Weir and Chartrand. After collapse at St Charles, refugees gathered at Swanton, planned and launched an armed invasion on December 6, about two hundred strong, with two field-pieces and muskets intended for recruits. They were promised American arms, supplies, and men, though much aid did not arrive. Patriotes also formed the main rebel movement at St Eustache, where many inhabitants were coerced into joining them, and preparations continued for a renewed rising.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

The Patriote party represented the position of the French-Canadian majority in the Assembly and opposed the power of the Bureaucrats or Chateau Clique. Under Papineau's guidance, it resisted permanent financial settlements that would limit the Assembly's control and demanded reforms in the administration of Lower Canada.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity.

The Patriote party became increasingly associated with Papineau's revolutionary and republican views, although it included both French- and English-speaking supporters. Its extreme wing adopted the Ninety-Two Resolutions, while Neilson, Cuvillier, Quesnel, Stuart, Bedard, and Parent later broke away from Papineau's leadership.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

The Patriote party won the general election after the Ninety-Two Resolutions. Members who had opposed the Resolutions, including Neilson, Cuvillier, Quesnel, and Stuart, were defeated.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid.

The Patriotes organized meetings throughout Lower Canada, denounced the Russell resolutions, boycotted imported English goods, and promoted Canadian products. They adopted increasingly republican and separatist language, formed the Fils de la Liberte, and organized volunteer companies of armed men.

] {69} CHAPTER VIII THE DOGS OF WAR As the autumn of 1837 wore on, the situation in Lower Canada began to assume an aspect more and more threatening.

The Patriotes continued to gather for military drill and musketry exercises despite the governor's proclamation forbidding such meetings. They intimidated loyalists, displayed the tri-coloured flag and eagles, resisted arrests, and formed armed groups under Wolfred Nelson and Thomas Storrow Brown. They defeated Colonel Charles Gore's force at St Denis, though some Patriotes murdered Lieutenant Weir and Chartrand.

{89} But the end of rebellion was not yet in sight.

After the defeat at St Charles, a number of Patriote refugees gathered at Swanton and planned an armed invasion of Canada. Patriotes also formed the main rebel movement in St Eustache, where many local inhabitants were coerced into joining them.
