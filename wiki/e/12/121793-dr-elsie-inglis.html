  • In Dr. Elsie Inglis: *Dr. Elsie Inglis*, by Lady Frances Balfour, is a memoir of the Scottish doctor, suffragist, institution-builder, and wartime medical leader Elsie Maud Inglis (1864–1917). Dedicated to Serbia and the Scottish Women’s Hospitals, it draws on family, friends, patients, and hospital Units to portray a woman whose courage, religious faith, devotion to justice, and practical service shaped her life. The book begins with the Inglis family’s Scottish roots and far-reaching lives in America and India, then follows Elsie’s childhood in India. Her father, John Inglis, gave his daughters equal opportunities, encouraged intellectual companionship, and deeply influenced her sense of fairness and independence. After the family’s moves through Tasmania, Edinburgh, and Paris, her mother’s death placed household responsibilities on Elsie while she was still young. Elsie pursued medicine despite entrenched barriers against women students. She studied in Edinburgh and Glasgow, fought exclusions from clinical teaching, obtained the Triple Qualification, worked at the New Hospital for Women in London, and trained in midwifery at the Rotunda in Dublin. Her father’s death in 1894 was a profound loss. She later graduated M.B., C.M. in 1899, developed surgical skill, and worked to expand women’s medical education. Alongside medicine, Inglis championed women’s suffrage, Liberal politics, Home Rule, and women’s citizenship. She helped organize the Scottish Federation of Suffrage Societies, opposed militancy, and became known as a fair, disciplined leader. In Edinburgh she helped establish Muir Hall of Residence and founded the Hospice in the High Street, serving mothers, infants, students, and poor families with both medical skill and personal care. Her faith, family, friendships, and disciplined habits sustained her. World War I redirected her work on an international scale. After the War Office rejected her offer of service, she organized the Scottish Women’s Hospitals through suffrage networks; French and Serbian authorities accepted them. The hospitals served in France, Serbia, and Russia. In Serbia, Inglis and her colleagues confronted typhus, famine, invasion, overcrowding, and shortages. Some Units remained with patients during the 1915 occupation, were captured, and later sent through Austria to Zürich. Returning to Britain, Inglis organized a final Unit for the Serbian Army on the Rumanian Russian front. Despite illness, revolution, retreats, disrupted communications, and severe shortages, she led work at Medgidia, Bulbulmic, Braila, and Reni, caring for Serbian, Rumanian, and Russian patients. Gravely ill, she helped evacuate the Serbian Army and her Unit from Russia, reached Newcastle, and died on November 26, 1917. Her funeral in Edinburgh united Scottish, Serbian, Allied, medical, and charitable representatives, while her final message urged continued support for Serbian hospitals and transport.

Dr. Elsie Inglis

This unit concerns Dr. Elsie Inglis and identifies a work about her titled *Dr. Elsie Inglis*. Lady Frances Balfour is named as the author, with previous works including *The Life of Lady Victoria Campbell* and *Life and Letters of Rev. James Macgregor, D.D.* The book is dedicated to Serbia and the Scottish Women’s Hospitals that served and loved their brethren from 1914 to 1917, and it includes an illustration of Dr. Elsie Inglis from 1916.

’ PREFACE The story of Elsie Inglis needs little introduction.

The preface introduces Elsie Inglis as a courageous and principled woman who consistently followed her convictions and used every opportunity to serve others. It describes her as honest, affectionate, devoted to liberty, and representative of the spirit of her nation and race. The memoir is based on accounts from her family, friends, patients, and Units, and aims to present a faithful portrait of her. It also asks readers not to forget the patients in the Hospice and the Scottish Women’s Hospitals, which are described as her living memorials.

This unit traces the Inglis family from Kingsmills, Inverness-shire, through Alexander Inglis’s emigration to Carolina, David Inglis’s career in India, and John Inglis’s distinguished service in the Indian colonial administration. Alexander Inglis’s loyalty to the British Government led to the confiscation of his Charleston estates, and he later died from wounds received in a duel. David Inglis served the East India Company in India, while John Inglis became a senior administrator in the Punjab, Rohilcund, Oude, and the North-West Provinces. The unit also describes Harriet Louis Thompson’s family life and religious work, the faithful Indian nurse Sona, the Indian Mutiny, and the family’s recurring combination of Scottish heritage, activity, travel, and religious faith.

CHAPTER II ELSIE MAUD INGLIS 1864-1917 ‘Lo, children are an heritage of the Lord: and the fruit of the womb is His reward.

This unit describes Elsie Maud Inglis’s birth and childhood in India, including her early travels, vivid personality, intelligence, and strong will. It portrays her close companionship with her father, Mr. Inglis, and the religious and family life fostered by both parents. Her sisters’ memories and family letters record her imaginative play, independent character, education, and early interest in the world around her. The unit presents Mr. Inglis’s commitment to giving his daughters equal opportunities and links Elsie’s upbringing to her later service to mankind.

CHAPTER III THE LADDER OF LEARNING 1876-1885 ‘Hast thou come with the heart of thy childhood back: The free, the pure, the kind?

This unit describes the Inglis family’s move from India to Tasmania, Edinburgh, and Paris between 1876 and 1885, focusing especially on Elsie Inglis’s education and developing character. Mr. Inglis retired from service after serving as Chief Commissioner of Oude and later supported Elsie through correspondence and intellectual companionship. Elsie attended school in Tasmania and at the Charlotte Square Institution, where she showed initiative, edited the school magazine, and secured permission for girls to play in Charlotte Square gardens. After returning from Paris in 1883, Elsie’s mother died of scarlet fever, and Elsie assumed responsibility for her father and household, recording resolutions for her conduct.

’ CHAPTER IV THE STUDENT DAYS 1885-1892 EDINBURGH--GLASGOW ‘Let knowledge grow from more to more, But more of reverence in us dwell; That mind and soul, according well, May make one music as before, But vaster.

This unit describes Elsie Inglis’s student years from 1885 to 1892, when she began medical study at the Edinburgh School of Medicine for Women and continued her studies from her father’s home. It explains the barriers facing women in higher and medical education, including restricted clinical teaching, extra fees, poor instruction, and exclusion from mixed classes. Elsie Inglis strongly opposed what she considered unjust treatment of students, including Dr. Jex Blake’s expulsion of two students, while remaining influenced by her father’s commitment to equal justice. The unit also records aspects of her student life, including her religious observance, long walks with Mr. Inglis, strong sense of fair play, and encouragement of women studying medicine.

’ In 1891 Elsie went to Glasgow to take the examination for the Triple Qualifica

Elsie Inglis went to Glasgow in 1891 to study for the Triple Qualification, because she could not take surgery in Edinburgh and Glasgow offered better clinical teaching. At the Medical School in Glasgow, she trained in hospital wards and dispensaries under doctors including Dr. MacEwan while living at the Y.W.C.A. Hostel. She and the other women students fought the Glasgow Infirmary Managers’ attempt to exclude them from mixed classes and eventually secured access to the classes. Elsie passed her examination with great success and continued toward a career in medicine.

CHAPTER V LONDON THE NEW HOSPITAL FOR WOMEN DUBLIN THE ROTUNDA 1892-1894 ‘We take up the task eternal and the burden and the lesson, Pioneers, O Pioneers.

After completing her clinical work and passing the Triple Qualification in Glasgow in 1892, Elsie Inglis became house-surgeon at the New Hospital for Women in London. She described the hospital’s wards, staff, patients, operations, outpatient department, and daily demands in detailed letters to her father. Elsie worked with Garrett Anderson, Mrs. Scharlieb, and other women doctors, while observing the difficulties and opportunities facing women in medical practice and education. She hoped eventually to establish a hospital in Edinburgh and supported a mixed medical school there.

The speaker says that if they do not get into the Infirmary in Edinburgh, they intend to build a hospital for themselves like the hospital they are currently discussing. They plan to build it where the Cattle Market is, at the head of Lady Lawson Street, which they consider convenient for women in Fountainbridge, the Grassmarket, and Cowgate and comparatively high. They also intend initially to rent Eva’s hall as a dispensary.

’ The next course Elsie decided on taking was one of three months in Midwifery i

Elsie Inglis undertakes a three-month midwifery course at the Rotunda in Dublin, where she gains extensive practical experience but finds that women are not given the same opportunities as men. She considers continuing her training in Paris and beginning a medical practice in Edinburgh with Miss MacGregor, potentially including a dispensary and hospital for female students. During her stay she discusses Irish politics, women’s suffrage, medical work, and the difficulties faced by women doctors. She returns home shortly before her father, John Inglis, dies on March 13, 1894, leaving her deeply bereaved.

’ CHAPTER VI POLITICAL ENFRANCHISEMENT AND NATIONAL POLITICS ‘Well done, New Zealand!

This unit describes Elsie Inglis’s political and public life, especially her support for women’s suffrage, Liberal politics, and Home Rule. It presents John Inglis’s progressive view of equality and mutual respect within marriage and his influence on Elsie’s independent thought. Elsie connects the vote to women’s ability to challenge abusive husbands and improve laws affecting women’s health and welfare. The unit also records her views on the Home Rule question, Ireland, the Labour movement, and the Representation of the People’s Bill, which marked the achievement of political enfranchisement.

’ Another friend writes:-- ‘I should like to send you a recollection of her in t

This unit portrays Dr. Elsie Inglis as a pioneering doctor, organiser, and leader in the women’s suffrage movement. It describes her work for women as sanitary inspectors, her organisational leadership in the Scottish Federation of Suffrage Societies, and her opposition to militant suffragism. Her suffrage colleagues depict her as fair-minded, encouraging, disciplined, and devoted to extending women’s citizenship. The unit also connects the organised support of Scottish suffrage societies with the later creation of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals and Inglis’s wartime service.

”’ CHAPTER VII THE PROFESSION AND THE FAITH ‘Run the straight race through God’s good grace, Lift up thine eyes and seek His face; Life with its way before us lies, Christ is the path, and Christ the prize.

This unit describes Dr. Elsie Inglis’s medical practice, political work, and development of institutions serving women, mothers, infants, students, and poor local families in Edinburgh. She graduated M.B., C.M. from the University of Edinburgh in 1899, studied surgical and clinical methods abroad, and later acquired substantial surgical skill through the Scottish Women’s Hospitals. She campaigned for women’s medical education and enfranchisement, helped establish the Muir Hall of Residence, and founded the Hospice in the High Street of Edinburgh as a maternity and infant-welfare centre staffed by medical women. The unit also records her extensive personal care for patients and local families, whom she treated with practical authority, generosity, and affection.

Sorrow and disease are much the same whether they come to the rich or the poor,

This unit concerns Dr. Elsie Inglis’s medical work, family life, spiritual development, recreation, and disciplined daily habits. She treated rich and poor alike, combining trained skill with sympathy, and drew increasing strength from her faith. Her sisters, nieces, friends, and household formed an important part of her life, while holidays, family prayers, and social gatherings gave her rest and companionship. After her father’s death, she found a settled spiritual home in the Church of Scotland through the ministry and friendship of Dr. Wallace Williamson, and she faced the coming international crisis with steadfastness.

ELSIE INGLIS, 1916] CHAPTER VIII WAR AND THE SCOTTISH WOMEN ‘God the all-terrible King, Who ordainest Great winds Thy clarion, the lightnings Thy sword, Show forth Thy pity on high where Thou reignest, Give to us peace in our time, O Lord.

This unit describes how the outbreak of World War I transformed opportunities for women and enabled Elsie Maud Inglis to establish the Scottish Women’s Hospitals. The War Office rejected her offer of medical service, but French and Serbian authorities later welcomed the hospitals. Inglis mobilised women from the Women’s Suffrage movement, formed a committee through the National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies and the Scottish Federation, and sought funds for hospital units serving the Allied nations. By October 1914, the Scottish Women’s Hospitals scheme had been adopted and was being organised for service with the Serbian army.

’ Space utterly forbids our following the fortunes of the Scottish Women’s Hospi

This unit describes Elsie Inglis’s leadership of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals during the First World War, including the establishment of a hospital at Royaumont in France and later work in Serbia and Russia. At Royaumont Abbey, the hospital operated under the French Red Cross and was prepared by its staff despite severe shortages and delays. The unit also recounts Inglis’s identification with Joan of Arc and her support for Southern Slav unity through medical work among the Jugoslavs on the Eastern front. R. W. Seton-Watson praises Inglis as a heroic figure whose work in Serbia and Russia advanced humanitarian and national causes.

CHAPTER IX SERBIA ‘Send thine hand from above; rid me, and deliver me out of great waters, from the hand of strange children.

This unit describes the Scottish Women’s Hospitals’ work in Serbia and Russia during the war, including their efforts against typhus, typhoid, famine fevers, and other suffering. Dr. Elsie Inglis directed the hospitals’ organization and later took personal charge of the unit in Serbia, while Dr. Eleanor Soltau led the First Serbian Unit until illness forced her to stop. The Foreign Office and Admiralty supported the hospitals’ equipment, transport, and safety, despite the dangers faced by the women. Serbia commemorated Dr. Inglis and the Scottish Women’s Hospitals with a fountain at Mladenovatz after her death.

The unit recounts Dr. Elsie Inglis’s accounts of the typhus epidemic, the German, Bulgarian, and Austrian invasion of Serbia, and the Serbian retreat of 1915. It describes the medical work of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals for Foreign Service and other British and American units, especially at Kragujevatz, and records the deaths and illnesses caused by typhus. It also relates the capture, imprisonment, and eventual liberation of the Scottish Women’s Hospitals unit, as well as Inglis’s determination and leadership. Letters from 1915 describe the unit’s hospitals, its work among Serbian patients, and conditions in Serbia.

’ As the fever died out, a worse enemy came in.

This unit concerns Dr. Elsie Inglis and the Scottish women who continued nursing Serbian wounded during the Austro-German invasion and occupation of Serbia in 1915. The Scottish hospital units chose whether to evacuate or remain with their patients; the unit at Krushieevatz stayed and treated as many as 1,200 people under severe overcrowding, cold, hunger, and scarcity. The women also undertook sanitation, laundry, bathing, and infection-prevention work, avoiding a recurrence of typhus. In February 1916, the remaining unit was sent under guard through Austria to Zürich, after which Dr. Inglis returned to Britain determined to advocate for Serbia.

CHAPTER X RUSSIA 1917 ‘Even so in our mortal journey, The bitter north winds blow, And thus upon life’s red river, Our hearts as oarsmen row.

Dr. Elsie Maud Inglis returned to England in 1916 and led efforts to organize a final Scottish Women’s Hospitals enterprise for the Serbian Army on the Rumanian Russian front. After the War Office refused her request to serve in Basra, she organized a hospital unit and transport section of eighty women, which traveled via Archangel and Russia toward Odessa and the Dobrudja. The unit served wounded Serbian forces during the retreat, while the Russian Revolution endangered the Serbian Army and disrupted communications. Inglis continued working despite severe illness and returned to England in 1917, leaving the Serbian Division as the subject of her last recorded praise.

‘NEARING ODESSA, ‘_Sep.

Elsie describes the British medical unit’s journey from Archangel through Moscow and Odessa to the Serbian 1st Division, and its work during the fighting and retreat from Medgidia. The unit established hospitals at Medgidia and Bulbulmic, treated and evacuated wounded, and then helped care for thousands of neglected patients at Braila. Elsie reports strong Russian and Serbian support, the bravery of the Serbs, and severe shortages of medical staff and facilities. By January 1917, the unit was operating a hospital at Reni and trying to secure more Serbian patients and organize Serbian hospital work.

’ ‘IN AN AMBULANCE TRAIN, ‘NEAR ODESSA, _Jan.

Elsie writes from an ambulance train near Odessa and from the hospital at Reni about her medical unit's work among Serbian, Rumanian, and Russian patients during the war. She describes harsh weather, refugees, shortages, religious services, hospital inspections, and the unit's receipt of St. George Medals. She reports enthusiasm among Russian officers and patients for the changes in Petrograd and describes the unit's Easter observances. She also recounts a spy incident in which the unit was suspected but ultimately cleared and thanked for its conduct.

‘_On Active Service.

These letters record Elsie Maud Inglis’s work with the Scottish Women’s Hospitals at Reni in 1917, caring for Russian and Serbian personnel during World War I. She describes the hospital’s relations with Russian soldiers and officials, its shortages, patients, staff, and wartime activities. In August the Reni hospital closed and the unit rejoined the Serbian Division at the front, while Inglis praised the discipline and fighting qualities of the Serbs. By October the Division was preparing to move, and in November Inglis wrote from Archangel that she was on her way home.

’ CHAPTER XI THE MOORINGS CUT ‘Not I, but my Unit.

This unit recounts Dr. Elsie Inglis’s final work leading her unit during the Serbian evacuation from Russia and her return voyage to England. She insisted on preserving the equipment, supporting the Serbian Army, and bringing the Serbian soldiers safely away from the Russian front. Gravely ill, she organized the journey, continued working aboard the transport, and reached Newcastle, where she died surrounded by her relatives and members of the unit. Her final words present death as a passage into a new life and an encounter with God.

”’ As ‘We’ waited those anxious weeks for the news of the arrival of Dr.

Elsie Inglis returned to Edinburgh gravely ill after leading medical work for Serbia during the war and died on November 26, 1917. She was buried on November 29 in St. Giles’, with Allied flags, military honors, and representatives of Scotland, Serbia, her medical units, and her charitable work present. Crowds followed the procession across Edinburgh, and Serbian representatives carried her coffin to the grave. Her final message urged the London Committee for the Scottish Women’s Hospitals to support Serbian hospitals and transport.
