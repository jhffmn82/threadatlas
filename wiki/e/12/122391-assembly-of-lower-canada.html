Assembly of Lower Canada

group · 1 work · 10 quoted facts · 6 connections
Also called: Assembly, House, House of Assembly, House of Assembly for Lower Canada, Lower-Canadian Assembly, Lower-Canadian legislature, the Assembly, the House, the legislature, the new Assembly, this House

Abstract written by a model

The Assembly of Lower Canada was a legislative assembly elected by the people of Lower Canada. It had a French-Canadian majority, thirty-four French-Canadian members to sixteen English members, some elected by French-Canadian voters, and was controlled in membership by French Canadians. It chose Jean Antoine Panet as speaker and gave French equal standing with English in its records and publications, while French became the practical language of debate. Although elected, the Assembly lacked control of the government: the governor and Executive Council answered to the Colonial Office, and the Legislative Council could reject its measures. Its central struggle was therefore over revenue, supplies, and influence over the executive. It asserted that it could and would fund civil expenses, gained control of revenue apart from Crown dues and certain Imperial duties after the 1818 concession, and voted the civil list annually rather than for the sovereign's lifetime. It repeatedly rejected a permanent civil list, including Lord Goderich's 1831 proposal, and demanded redress of grievances before voting necessary supplies. After 1830, Papineau's supporters dominated it for many purposes. The Assembly expelled members and imprisoned an opponent; though refusal of supply failed in 1833, it passed the Ninety-Two Resolutions in 1834 by fifty-six votes to twenty-four. Following an election, it became increasingly confrontational, granted only six months' supply in 1836, and withheld further supplies pending Patriote demands. Its claim to control the executive through the purse was challenged by the Russell resolutions.

{7} CHAPTER II THE RIGHTS OF THE DEFEATED The British did not treat the French inhabitants of Canada as a conquered people; not as other countries won by conquest have been treated by their victorious invaders.

The Assembly of Lower Canada had thirty-four French-Canadian members and sixteen English members, with the English members owing their election to French-Canadian voters. It elected Jean Antoine Panet as speaker and placed French on a level with English in its records and publications, making French the language of debate for practical purposes.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

The House of Assembly was elected by the people of Lower Canada but lacked control over the Executive and could have its measures rejected by the Legislative Council. It gradually sought control over the province's revenues and repeatedly refused to vote a permanent civil list, producing a long deadlock with the government.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity.

The Assembly of Lower Canada was dominated for many purposes by Papineau's supporters. Papineau used its majority to expel members and imprison an opponent, but his proposal to refuse supply was defeated in 1833; in 1834 the Assembly passed the Ninety-Two Resolutions by fifty-six votes to twenty-four.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

The Assembly showed strong support for Papineau and adopted an increasingly confrontational position toward the executive government. In 1836 it refused to grant more than six months' supply and threatened to withhold further supplies unless the Patriote demands were met.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid.

The Assembly of Lower Canada refused to vote supplies until its demands for redress and radical political changes were satisfied. Its claim to control the executive through the power of the purse was directly challenged by the Russell resolutions.
