Achaia

place · 3 works · 14 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Acbaia, Achaia., Achaians

Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Achaia is described as the region where Helice and Bura were destroyed or greatly damaged by earthquakes and inundations, and whose cities Demetrius freed after their governors surrendered. In Description of Greece, Achaia is described as the Peloponnese's second division, formerly called Ægialus and inhabited by Ionians until the Achæans displaced them. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Achaia is described as a country and region of Greece that became the foremost Greek power before Rome reduced Greece.

Achaia in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Achaia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achaia was the region where Helice and Bura were destroyed or greatly damaged by the earthquakes and inundations. Demetrius took Bata by assault, received the surrender of Scyros, and then freed the remaining cities of Achaia. The governors of the cities surrendered when Cassander and Polyperchon sent them no assistance and Demetrius approached with a powerful army and large engines.

Achaia in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Achaia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achaia, the Peloponnese’s second division, was formerly Ægialus, inhabited by Ionians until Achæans displaced them. After Heraclidæ expelled Tisamenus and his sons from Lacedæmon and Argos, they arrived with an army; it borders Elis at Larisus, once said to be Araxus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVIII.

Tisamenus and his sons went to what was then called Achaia with their army after the Heraclidæ expelled them from Lacedæmon and Argos.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Achaia is identified as the second division of the Peloponnese. It was formerly called Ægialus, where the Ionians lived before the Achæans displaced them.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Achaia borders Elis at the river Larisus in the writer's time. In ancient times, the boundary was said to have been the promontory Araxus near the sea.

Achaia in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Achaia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Achaia is a country and region of Greece, called Ægialus in ancient times, lying between Elis and Sicyonia on the Corinthian Gulf; the Larisus and Dyme mark its boundary with Elis. Its inhabitants gave it its name. It rose with difficulty to great eminence and became the foremost Greek power through which Rome reduced Greece. Its rulers’ folly stopped its growth: Diæus frustrated Metellus’s plan to settle it, its general meetings were suppressed, and Greece was governed by a prætor of Achaia; Augustus assigned Dyme to Patræ.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

Description of Greece, Volume II / BOOK VII.--ACHAIA. / CHAPTER I.

Achaia is the name used in Pausanias's day for the country between Elis and Sicyonia bordering the Corinthian Gulf. Its inhabitants gave the country this name.

CHAPTER XV.

Metellus wished to settle Achaia himself, but his intention was frustrated by Diæus’ folly.

CHAPTER XVI.

Achaia was described as the foremost Greek power through which Rome reduced Greece. The general meetings in Achaia were suppressed, and the Roman official sent to govern Greece was called the prætor of Achaia.

CHAPTER XVII.

Achaia grew with great difficulty to considerable eminence in Greece, but the folly of its rulers stopped its growth. Its boundary with Elis was marked by the river Larisus and Dyme, and Augustus assigned Dyme to Patræ.
