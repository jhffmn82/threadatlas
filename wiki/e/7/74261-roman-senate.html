Roman Senate in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Roman Senate first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Roman Senate is a governing group that directs Roman domestic affairs, war, diplomacy, provincial administration, finance, and decisions affecting allies and foreign peoples. During a recruitment crisis for Marius, it issues a slave-emancipation decree and promises Roman citizenship to Italians, while also being urged to grant favor to Latins and allies rather than be compelled by armed demonstration. It funds Appius Claudius’s water project, though conflict follows over his inclusion of libertines, and it prepares Lucius iEmilius’s funeral. It rebukes Saturninus, removes his authority in Ostia, and transfers the province to another administrator. Abroad, it seeks Grecian support against Antiochus, negotiates with him, threatens freedom for Greeks in Asia, confirms peace, and promises freedom and self-government to Grecians. After conquest, it frees Macedonian cities, treats Macedonians with clemency, permits Illyrians their own laws, and reduces tribute; it also declares war on Perseus. It sends embassies to Jugurtha, manages many other embassies, honors or restrains rulers and communities, imposes terms on Aetolians, Carthaginians, and Cretans, redistributes Asian territories, and renews or confirms alliances.

Roman Senate in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Roman Senate first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Roman Senate is an institution that receives accusations, issues orders, appoints representatives, and determines disputes involving the Achæans, Lacedæmonians, Athenians, and other communities. It first supersedes Otilius after the destruction of Hestiæa and Anticyra, then hears accusations against the Achæans from Areus, Alcibiades, and Metellus. It appoints Appius and later Roman ambassadors to mediate or arbitrate between the Lacedæmonians and Achæans, while ordering Athens and Ætolia to restore Messenian and Achæan exiles to their rights. Its decisions favor limits on compulsory membership in the Achæan League: it holds that the Lacedæmonians, Argos, Heraclea under Mount Œta, and Orchomenus should not be forced into it; the chapter record also includes Corinth. It authorizes Gallus to release towns from the League, directs Sicyon to fine Athens for harm to Oropians, then reduces that fine from 500 to 100 talents. It also directs the Lacedæmonians to try non-capital cases before the Achæan League.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IX.

The Roman Senate heard accusations against the Achæans from Metellus, Areus, and Alcibiades. It sent Appius and others to settle the dispute, later authorized a plenipotentiary mission, and ordered Athens and Ætolia to restore Messenian and Achæan exiles to their rights.

CHAPTER XI.

The Roman Senate ordered the Sicyonians to impose on the Athenians a fine proportionate to the harm done to the Oropians. At the Athenians’ request, it remitted the fine from 500 talents to 100 talents.

CHAPTER XII.

The Roman Senate told the Lacedæmonians to try all but capital cases before the Achæan League. It also received the dispute involving the Spartan exiles and Menalcidas, and answered that it would send ambassadors to arbitrate between the Lacedæmonians and Achæans.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Roman Senate judged that the Lacedæmonians and Corinth should not be forced into the Achæan League, and likewise excluded Argos, Heraclea under Mount Œta, and the Arcadians of Orchomenus because they had no ancestral connection with the Achæans.
