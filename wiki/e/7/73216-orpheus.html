Orpheus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Orpheus is a Thracian, son of Oeagrus, renowned beyond recorded predecessors for music, poetry, ancient theology, divinity, and sacred mysteries. He studied the rites and ceremonies of the Idæi Dactyli, joined the Argonauts, and traveled to Egypt despite a prohibition on strangers, apparently to improve himself. Egyptian priests said he was initiated into the mysteries of Bacchus or Dionysius and saw a lake-crossing ceremony. He was especially esteemed by Thebes and by Greeks for his musical skill, theology, and sacred institutions. He brought Egyptian rites and ceremonies to Greece, instituted mysteries there in honor of Dionysius or Osiris, altered the Orgia, and transferred Bacchus’s birth story. Accounts also credit him with Greek teachings about the underworld, punishment of the wicked, Elysian fields, and Mercury as conductor of souls; he imitated Osiris and Isis in funeral and underworld accounts. At the end of the narrative, he descends into hell for Proserpine and persuades her to let him take her back. His music was said to draw wild beasts and trees after him.

Orpheus in The Argonautica

Apollonius Rhodius · -0299/-0200

Orpheus is a singer, musician, seer, and son of Calliope, also called the son of Oeagrus. His lyre and songs have magical power, making music central to his role among Jason’s heroes aboard Argo. He carries Apollo’s sacred tripod, sings and plays at sacrifice, urges the Argonauts to honor Apollo of the Dawn, and leads their hymn. On the voyage, he ends Idas and Idmon’s quarrel by singing of creation and accompanies the rowing heroes. When the Sirens sing, his Bistonian lyre overcomes their voices and keeps the crew from landing. Near Atlas’s garden, he recognizes a divine portent and prays for water; the nymphs answer by bringing vegetation from the earth.

Orpheus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Orpheus is a Rhodopeïan and Thracian bard, poet, king, high priest, and teacher, traditionally called the son of Apollo and Calliope. He excels in poetry and music, plays and possesses Orpheus’s lyre, teaches astronomy, introduces worship, rites, and the Thesmophoria, and is associated with Orphica and Eleusinian mysteries. After Eurydice’s death, he mourns her and descends to the Infernal Regions. His music and words persuade Pluto and Persephone to permit her return, but he looks back before reaching the upper earth and loses her again. He withdraws to Thrace, avoids or is indifferent to women, and charms animals, trees, rocks, and other nature with his singing. Women of Thrace, instigated by Venus, attack, tear apart, and kill him. His head and lyre are carried to Lesbos; as a ghost he descends under the earth, finds Eurydice, and walks with her in the fields of the blessed.

BOOK THE TENTH.

Orpheus mourns Eurydice, descends to the Infernal Regions, and persuades Pluto and Persephone to allow her return through the power of his music and words. He looks back before reaching the upper earth, loses Eurydice a second time, and later withdraws to the mountains of Thrace, where his music attracts animals, trees, and rocks.

BOOK THE ELEVENTH.

Orpheus sings to his lyre on Mount Rhodope, charming woods, rocks, birds, serpents, and wild beasts. The women of Thrace attack and kill him, after which his head and lyre are carried to Lesbos. His ghost descends under the earth, where he finds Eurydice and walks with her.

FABLE II. [XI.85-145]

Orpheus is identified as the victim of the Thracian women's murder, for which Bacchus punishes them. He had entrusted the mysterious orgies of Bacchus to Midas and the Cecropian Eumolpus.

Orpheus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Orpheus is a Thracian poet, described as the son of Pierus, though Greeks supposed him to be the son of Calliope. He excelled earlier poets in arranging poems and wrote verses to Eros for the Lycomidæ’s mysteries. His greatest influence came from being credited with inventing mysteries, purifications, cures, and means of turning away divine wrath. Traditions connect him with Eurydice and attribute his death variously to Thracian women, lightning, or suicide. He does not compete at Delphi because of his solemn relation to mysteries and elevation of soul. At Helicon he appears with Telete and animals listening to his singing; another scene places him on a hill in Proserpine’s grove, holding a harp, touching and leaning against a willow, and dressed Greek rather than Thracian.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXX.

Orpheus is represented at Helicon with Telete beside him and animals listening to his singing. He is said to have excelled his predecessors in arranging poems and to have gained influence by being credited with inventing mysteries of the gods, purifications, cures, and means of turning away divine wrath. Several traditions concern his death, his connection with Eurydice, and the locations of his tomb and remains.

CHAPTER XXX.

Orpheus is depicted sitting on a hill in what appears to be the grove of Proserpine, touching the branches of a willow-tree while holding a harp. His dress is Greek rather than Thracian, and he is shown with a hat that is not Thracian.

Orpheus in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Orpheus is a poet and musician, son of Apollo and Calliope, whose lyre, given by Apollo, charms mortals, beasts, trees, and rocks. After Eurydice dies, he enters the underworld and wins her release, but loses her by looking back before reaching the upper air. Resisting Thracian maidens, he is torn apart and buried at Libethra; afterward he finds Eurydice in Tartarus, plays in the Elysian fields, and his lyre is placed among the stars.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter XVII

Orpheus was the son of Apollo and Calliope, and Apollo gave him a lyre whose music charmed mortals, wild beasts, trees, and rocks. After Eurydice died, he entered the underworld and persuaded Pluto and Proserpine to release her on the condition that he not look back, but he turned before reaching the upper air and lost her again. He later resisted the advances of the Thracian maidens, who tore him limb from limb, and after death he found Eurydice in Tartarus.
