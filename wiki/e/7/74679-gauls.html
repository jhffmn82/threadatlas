Gauls in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Gauls are portrayed chiefly as formidable, often destructive soldiers employed or invading across several conflicts. Sent by Dionysius from Sicily, two thousand aided the Lacedemonians, fought bravely against Boeotians and confederates, received rewards, and returned to Sicily. Under Brennus they invaded Macedonia and Greece to plunder Delphi, but losses, Greek attacks, hunger, cold, and fighting annihilated the army. They served Apollodorus as a cruel life-guard; a garrison left by Pyrrhus plundered Macedonian kings’ sepulchres at Aegae. They offered Perseus aid but departed when payment was refused; Eumenes then subjugated them. They also invaded Italy, defeated Romans at the Allia, captured Rome, and were later routed by Marcus Furius at Trausium.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. VIIL

Two thousand Gauls were sent by Dionysius the tyrant from Sicily to assist the Lacedemonians. They fought bravely, helped rout and kill many Boeotians and their confederates, received rewards from the Lacedemonians, and returned to Sicily at the end of the summer.

BOOK XXII.

The Gauls under Brennus invaded Macedonia and Greece with a very large army and intended to plunder the temple at Delphi. After suffering heavy losses, they retreated, but Greek attacks, hunger, cold, and further fighting destroyed the army so completely that none returned home.

A body of Gauls crossed the river Ister to assist Perseus, but their general demanded the promised payment of about five hundred talents. Because Perseus refused to pay, the Gauls returned home, causing him to lose their assistance; Eumenes later brought the whole nation of the Gauls into subjection.

BOOK XIV.

The Gauls invaded Italy, defeated the Romans at the Allia, and captured Rome while the Romans were besieged in the Capitol. They were later routed by Marcus Furius and cut off in the plains of Trausium.

Gauls in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Gauls are a people and warlike nations, divided into tribes and unequal-population nations in Gaul and other countries. They are described as tall, pale and sweaty, naturally and artificially red-haired, and skilled in fighting with chariots, swords, and saunians; they challenge enemies to single combat and preserve notable enemies’ heads. They wear gold ornaments and party-coloured Bracse, yet leave gold in temples. They produce gold, lack wine because of the cold air, drink barley-based Xythus, and eagerly consume imported wine. They chiefly worship Pollux and Castor, have Bards, and believe in transmigration of souls. They crossed the Alps, occupied the land between the Apennine hills and the Alps, expelled the Tyrrhenians, defeated Romans at the Allia, captured and destroyed Rome, and besieged the Capitol before being defeated or ambushed on their return. They are also described as subdued by Julius Caesar, whose victory enlarged the Roman empire to the British isles.

Galati in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Galati, called Celts, came from remotest Europe, overran Illyria, Macedonia and Thessaly, and invaded Greece. Defeated near Delphi by local forces amid storms and falling rocks, many crossed into Asia Minor; others died on a Nile desert island by swords and famine.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

Description of Greece / BOOK I.--ATTICA.

The Galati came from the remotest parts of Europe and invaded Greece after overrunning Illyria, the lands between Illyria and Macedonia, Macedonia, and Thessaly. They were defeated near Delphi by the Delphians, Phocians, and Ætolians with the aid of storms, falling rocks, and heroic warriors, and many later crossed into Asia Minor.

Celts in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Celts are barbarians whose expeditions and invasions extend beyond their borders. Under Cambaules, their first expedition reaches Thrace but goes no farther. In a second expedition with infantry and cavalry, they invade Macedonia, Thrace, Pæonia, and Greece, defeat Macedonians after Ptolemy falls, outrage Thessaly, and advance near Magnesia and Phthiotis. Crossing the ocean and then the Sperchius despite destroyed bridges, they invade Greece but suffer heavy losses at Delphi, compounded by winter, rocks, panic, and starvation. The surviving forces in Macedonia and Thrace return to Asia Minor the following year.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIX.

The Celts first crossed beyond their borders under Cambaules but did not advance beyond Thrace. In the second expedition, they defeated the Macedonians in battle after Ptolemy fell, though they did not proceed farther into Greece and returned home.

CHAPTER XX.

The Galatians have crossed the ocean and are advancing through the region near Magnesia and Phthiotis. They cross the Sperchius by swimming through a marshy section and using their shields as rafts after the Greeks destroy the ordinary bridges.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Celts suffered severe losses in battle, from the winter conditions, from falling rocks, from panic, and from starvation. The text states that the Celts crossed back to Asia Minor in the following year, while a footnote explains that this refers to the Celtic forces remaining in Macedonia and Thrace rather than Brennus’s destroyed army.

Galati in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Galati were a barbarian Celtic army and migrating force that moved from Europe toward Asia, crossing the Hellespont and later entering Asia Minor by ships. They repeatedly campaigned toward Greece, initially retreating from Thrace as too few, then returning in greater strength under divided commanders against several peoples. Their forces included 152,000 infantry and 20,400 horse; each horse-soldier had two mounted servants who could replace casualties, making the actual cavalry count 61,200. They were marked by desperate valour, cruelty, limited skill in war, and shields as their only defensive armour. They plundered the treasures at Delphi, ravaged near Heraclea, killed people in the fields, and attempted temple plunder, but failed to take Heraclea and suffered defeats at Thermopylæ and Mount Œta. A 40,800-man expedition devastated Callion, for which the Ætolians fined them; Brennus also led 40,000 picked Galati against the Phocians. They did not habitually mourn or seek burial for their dead. Prophecy portrayed their crossing into Asia, destruction of cities, and eventual destruction by Attalus. In Pessinus, they avoided pork.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

The Galati crossed into Asia Minor in ships after passing through the undefended route at Thermopylæ. Their migration contributed to a situation in which no Greek state was clearly dominant.

CHAPTER VII.

The army of the Galati was among those who enriched themselves most and longest by plundering the treasures of the god at Delphi.

CHAPTER XV.

The Galati are described as a Celtic army that would cross from Europe into Asia and destroy cities there. Phaennis foretold that they would ravage Asia and that Attalus would bring them a day of destruction.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Galati made repeated expeditions toward Greece, first turning back from Thrace because they considered themselves too few, and later returning in greater strength. Their army was divided among commanders for campaigns against the Thracians, Triballi, Pæonians, Macedonians, and Illyrians. They later mustered 152,000 infantry and a cavalry force whose riders were supported by two mounted servants each.

CHAPTER XXI.

The Galati ravaged the country around Heraclea and killed men found in the fields, but they did not capture the city because the Ætolians defended it. At Thermopylæ they fought with great fury despite inferior armour and difficult terrain, but were defeated and suffered heavy losses during their confused retreat. They did not ask permission to bury their dead and appeared indifferent to whether the bodies received burial.

CHAPTER XXII.

The Galati tried to cross Mount Œta near Heraclea and plunder a temple of Athene, but Telesarchus and his garrison defeated them. A force of 40,000 infantry and about 800 cavalry later devastated Callion and returned toward Thermopylæ, suffering heavy losses from the Ætolians and their allies. Brennus also led 40,000 picked Galati through a pass over Mount Œta, where they defeated the Phocians.
