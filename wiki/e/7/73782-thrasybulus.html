Thrasybulus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Thrasybulus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Thrasybulus appears in two political and military careers. As brother and successor of Hiero, he was king of Syracuse for one year, but his cruel rule of killings, false charges, banishments, and confiscations provoked a revolt; he surrendered and went into exile at Locri. As an Athenian citizen, general, admiral, and commander, he led fleets at Dardanium and Arginusae, sailed with thirty ships against Ephesus, brought cities in Thrace under Athenian power, and joined operations against Byzantium. Banished by the Thirty Tyrants, he led Athenian exiles, seized Phila, defeated the tyrants' forces, took Munychia and the Piraeus, refused shared power, and helped restore citizens' liberties. Later, while commanding forty warships in Ionia and nearby regions, he was killed at Aspendus after his soldiers plundered the countryside.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. XIII.

Thrasybulus succeeded Hiero but ruled with cruelty, ordering killings, false accusations, banishments, and confiscations. A general revolt forced him to surrender the kingdom and depart into exile at Locri.

CHAP. V.

Thrasybulus commanded the Athenian right wing at Dardanium. At Claros he attacked the enemy's land support, initially endured heavy pressure, and later received Theramenes's assistance before the combined Athenian forces prevailed.

Thrasybulus sailed from Athens with thirty ships and an army and assaulted Ephesus, but the townsmen drove him back after killing four hundred Athenians. He later brought cities in Thrace under the power of the Athenians and joined Alcibiades and Theramenes in the operations against Byzantium.

CHAP. XIV.

Thrasybulus was the Athenian admiral with chief command on the day of the battle. He commanded the right wing with Pericles the younger and was later among the commanders involved in the controversy over the unburied dead.

CHAP. VI.

Thrasybulus, surnamed Tyrius and an Athenian citizen, fled the Thirty Tyrants and, with Theban assistance, seized Phila. He defeated the tyrants' forces, took Munychia and the Piraeus, rejected their offer to share power, and helped restore the Athenian citizens to their former liberties.

CHAP. XL

Thrasybulus commanded forty Athenian warships and operated in Ionia, the Chersonesus, Lesbos, and Rhodes. After sailing from Lesbos to Aspendus, some of his soldiers plundered the countryside; the Aspendians attacked at night and killed him.
