Dionysius the younger in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Dionysius the younger first appears

Abstract written by a model

Dionysius the younger was tyrant and ruler of Syracuse. After Dionysius the tyrant died, he succeeded him, called a senate, sought popular goodwill, held a ceremonial funeral, and secured his throne. He built cities in Apulia to protect shipping and made peace with the Carthaginians, but was portrayed as inactive, slothful, cowardly, and neglectful of military training; his careless war against the Lucanians contributed to losing his dominion. He sent gold-and-ivory statues toward Olympus and Delphi, then sold them for the army after their interception. He regained Syracuse’s Island and castles, but Hicetas took the rest. He surrendered the castle to Timoleon for safe departure to Peloponnesus and died in poor circumstances.

Dionysius sent golden and ivory statues to Olympus and Delphi, but his vessels were intercepted. After the Athenians advised him to sell the sacred objects to support the army, he did so and wrote condemning them for sacrilege against Apollo and Jupiter.

He later surrendered the castle to Timoleon in exchange for safe departure to Peloponnesus with his goods and possessions, and spent the rest of his life in poor circumstances.

Dionysius the Younger acted in Sicily and other regions during the conflicts involving Dion and Syracuse. He was expelled by Timoleon.

Dionysius in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Dionysius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Dionysius is repeatedly identified as tyrant and ruler of Syracuse, reigning for thirty-eight years and father of Dionysius the younger. He strengthened his sovereignty in Sicily by dispersing the Syracusans and fortifying Epipolae, while conducting wars against the Rhegines and Carthaginians. His forces included thirty thousand foot, three thousand horse, and three hundred sail; he captured Motya, Selinus, Entella, and Eryx, razed Caulonia and moved its inhabitants to Syracuse, but failed at Lilybaeum. He defeated the Carthaginians at Cabala, was defeated at Cronium, and made peace for a payment of a thousand talents. He also sought Adriatic and Ionian control for routes to Epirus and ship harbours, allied with Illyrians through Alcetas, helped establish Paros and built Lissus, and aided the Lacedaemonians with Gauls and Spaniards. After peace, he wrote poetry, whose Olympic mockery led to suspicion, executions, and banishments, though Leptines and Philistus were restored. He lost Philistus, offered Dion the kingdom, secretly escaped rather than surrendering his treasure, and his forces later recovered Syracuse. Amazed by Damon and Phintias, he pardoned Phintias and wished to join the Pythagorean society. He ultimately died after a later war, following illness associated with excessive feasting and drinking.

Dionysius, tyrant of Syracuse, devoted himself to writing poetry after being freed from war with the Carthaginians. His verses were mocked at the Olympic games, and his resulting suspicion and melancholy led him to execute some friends and banish others, although Leptines and Philistus were later restored.

CHAP. n.

Dionysius, tyrant of Syracuse, sought control of the cities on the Adriatic Sea to secure access to Epirus and harbors for his ships. He supported the Illyrians, helped establish Paros and Lissus, raided a wealthy temple near Agylla in Etruria, and used the proceeds to prepare for war against the Carthaginians. He defeated the Carthaginians at Cabala but was defeated at Cronium, after which he made peace and paid them a thousand talents.

CHAP. VIIL

Dionysius sent two thousand Gauls and Spaniards to assist the Lacedemonians and later attacked the Carthaginians in Sicily with a large army and fleet. He captured Selinus, Entella, and Eryx, but failed to take Lilybaeum; after the Carthaginians recovered many ships, he died following a violent illness caused by excessive feasting and drinking.

CHAP. IV.

Dionysius lost Philistus, his most valiant friend, and offered Dion first half and then all of the kingdom. He agreed to surrender the castle if he and his mercenaries could leave for Italy with their treasure, but he secretly sailed away with his household goods. His forces later recaptured Syracuse under Nypsius.

Dionysius in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Dionysius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Dionysius is presented in two connected portrayals: as an immortal god, also called Bacchus and identified with Osiris, and as the tyrant and ruler of Syracuse and Sicily. In the divine account he is credited with discovering wine, teaching vine planting and wine-making, inventing Zythus for lands without vines, civilizing nations, being raised by nymphs at Nysa under Jupiter's instruction, and leading an expedition into India. The records also distinguish an older Dionysius, son of Jupiter and Proserpina, from a later one, son of Semele, while assigning similar deeds to both. In the political and military account, Dionysius survives wounds during Hermocrates' attempted return because friends report him dead, then usurps tyranny at Syracuse. He secures power through walls, a castle, foreign troops, mercenaries, disarmament of citizens, gifts, land distributions, and suppression, killing, banishing, or replacing opponents. He commands Syracuse's army and fleet, fortifies Epipolae and the city, and expands control over Sicilian communities. His rule is dominated by long war with Carthage and campaigns in Sicily and Italy. He prepares major forces, besieges and captures Motya, defends Syracuse after setbacks, makes and breaks strategic peace arrangements, recruits and settles mercenaries, and fights at places including Catana, Abacene, Taurominium, Rhegium, and Helorus. He is accused of tyranny, sacrilege, military failure, and prolonging war to deny liberty, yet retains authority through force and patronage. His later campaigns bring the destruction of Caulonia and the fall of Rhegium after an eleven-month famine siege, followed by enslavement and cruel punishment of its people and leaders.

Dionysius is identified with Osiris and Bacchus. Greek mysteries are said to have taught that he was begotten by Jupiter through Semele, although the Egyptian account treats this as a later Greek alteration of Osiris's history.

DIODORUS SICULUS- / BOOK IV.

Dionysius is presented as a god who discovered wine, taught people to plant the vine, made barley drink for regions unsuitable for vines, and civilized many nations. The text distinguishes an older Dionysius, the son of Jupiter and Proserpina, from the later Dionysius, the son of Semele, although both were credited with similar actions.

CHAP. X.

Dionysius was among those badly wounded in Hermocrates' attempted return. His friends reported him dead to protect him from the immediate fury of the people, and he later usurped the tyranny.

CHAP. XVI.

Dionysius gathered forces from Syracuse, Italy, and his confederates to relieve Gela, but his attack on the Carthaginian camp failed and he withdrew during the night. He ordered the inhabitants of Camarina to remove to Syracuse, provoking anger among his soldiers and subjects. He then hastened back to Syracuse, forced entry through the gates, killed or banished opponents, and made peace with Amilcar.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK XIV.

Dionysius, tyrant of Sicily, strengthened his control by fortifying the island and arsenal of Syracuse, distributing land and houses, and campaigning against free Sicilian communities. When the Syracusans revolted and besieged him, he sought aid from the Campanians, defeated the divided rebels, and then rewarded and dismissed the Campanians.

CHAP. III.

Dionysius strengthened his tyranny at Syracuse by disarming the citizens, building another wall around the castle, hiring foreign troops, and preparing whatever was necessary to secure his rule. He conquered or subjugated Enna, Catana, Naxos, and Leontium, and later planned war against Carthage while fortifying Epipolae.

CHAP. VII.

Dionysius made peace with the Rhegians and Messanians before undertaking extensive preparations for war against Carthage. He assembled a large army and fleet, gained the submission or support of most Sicilian cities, declared war against the Carthaginians, and besieged Motya.

Dionysius prevented Himiico from bringing his ships out through Motya's narrow harbor and had them drawn overland into the sea at a safer distance. He then completed the siege works, captured Motya, ordered the surviving citizens to seek refuge in the Greek temples, and returned to Syracuse after placing a garrison in the city.

Dionysius invaded Carthaginian territory, but Himilco's arrival forced him to withdraw toward Syracuse. He strengthened the castles and forts around Syracuse, freed slaves to man ships, recruited mercenaries from Lacedaemon, and sought assistance from the Greeks. After the naval defeat near Catana, he returned to Syracuse rather than risk leaving the city without a strong garrison.

Dionysius was absent when the Syracusans defeated part of the Carthaginian fleet, then praised their courage before the assembly. Theodorus accused him of tyranny, military failures, sacrilege, and deliberately prolonging the war to prevent the Syracusans from regaining their liberty. Dionysius dissolved the assembly after Pharacides opposed the proposal against him, courted the people with gifts and feasts, and later led a surprise attack on the Carthaginian camp and fleet.

The Carthaginians being thus routed both by sea and knd, seat

Dionysius refused to let the entire Carthaginian army leave Sicily, though he permitted Carthaginian citizens to depart secretly at night. He captured or dispersed the remaining forces, made a league with the Spaniards, gave the common soldiers and baggage to his troops as plunder, and later settled about ten thousand mercenaries at Leontium. He also recruited other mercenaries and used them, together with members of his own family, to protect his government.

CHAP. IX,

Dionysius fortified Messana, fought Rhegium and the Sicilians, and besieged Taurominium. He nearly captured its castle by a night assault but was driven out, losing more than six hundred men; he later defeated Mago at Abacene and unsuccessfully attacked Rhegium.

CHAP. XL

Dionysius gathered Syracusans and mercenaries against Mago and secured the support of Agyrus by promising him adjoining territory. He avoided an immediate battle, made peace with the Carthaginians after the Syracusans deserted, and took possession of Taurominium, where he replaced many Sicilians with mercenaries.

CHAP. XU.

Dionysius of Syracuse commanded large armies and fleets in expeditions against Italy. He suffered a naval defeat near Rhegium, later defeated the Italian Greeks at the river Helorus, spared more than ten thousand surrendered prisoners, destroyed Caulonia, and continued his siege of Rhegium. He sent lavish chariots, tents, singers, and poems to the Olympic games, where his verses were mocked.

CHAP. XIII.

Dionysius besieged Rhegium for eleven months and cut off all means of relief. After Rhegium surrendered through famine, he enslaved many inhabitants and inflicted cruel punishments on Philo and his family.
