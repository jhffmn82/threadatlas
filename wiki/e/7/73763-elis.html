Elis in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Elis is a Peloponnesian people and country, originally settled from Calydon and other parts of Ætolia; its inhabitants were successively called Epeans and Eleans. Formerly considered especially orderly, it is fertile in fruits and notably in flax, including flax said to grow nowhere else in Greece. It contains Samicum, has Cyllene as a harbor, and includes a city with civic, athletic, and religious sites. Its people are associated with a longstanding dislike of horses and with a supposed curse affecting mares bred with he-asses. What matters most is Elis's control of Olympia and the Olympian games. It preserves Olympian rites, sacrifices, altars, temples, offerings, victor records, and statues; controls admission and rules for contests; imposes athletic fines; proclaims victors; and changes the programme of events. It repeatedly contests Pisa's attempts to conduct the games, refuses to recognize the 104th Olympiad conducted by Pisæans and Arcadians, and organizes umpires according to its tribes. Its religious life includes worship of Sosipolis and Ilithyia and sacrifices to Zeus, Pelops, Hercules, and others. Its history begins with settlement, conflicts involving Hercules, disputes with Pisa and Arcadians over Olympia, the settlement of Oxylus, and Iphitus's revival of the games. Elis participates in the Trojan war and the Persian invasion, supplies the Messenian colony at Cyllene, and reluctantly allies with the Lacedæmonians in Attica. It later fights the Lacedæmonians alongside Mantineans and Athenians. After losing territory to Arcadians, its tribes fall from twelve to eight. It joins Macedonians under Philip, attacks the Lacedæmonians, then fights Macedonians after Alexander's death. Corruption connected with Philip brings faction, followed by a Messenian intervention. Aristotimus briefly rules with Antigonus's support before being deposed and killed. Finally, after hardship and Lacedæmonian invasions, Elis yields Lepreum and other subject communities, destroys its fortifications, and admits the Lacedæmonians to Olympia.

Narrative, section by section

CHAPTER VI.

Elis resisted the Lacedæmonian demand that Lepreum and other subject communities be allowed to live under their own laws. After invasions, internal conflict, and severe wartime hardship, Elis agreed to free its subject cities, destroy its fortifications, and admit the Lacedæmonians to Olympia.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Elis, formerly regarded as orderly, fell into faction after influential people were corrupted by Philip, the son of Amyntas. About 1,000 Messenians entered Elis using Lacedæmonian colors on their shields, expelled the Lacedæmonian party, and entrusted the city to their own friends.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Elis was originally settled by people from Calydon and other parts of Ætolia, and its inhabitants were successively called Epeans and Eleans. Its history includes wars with Hercules, disputes with Pisa and the Arcadians over Olympia, the settlement of Oxylus, and the revival of the Olympian games by Iphitus.

CHAPTER V.

Elis was ruled by Aristotimus, son of Damaretus and grandson of Etymon, with assistance from Antigonus, but Aristotimus was deposed and killed after six months by Chilon, Hellanicus, Lampis, and Cylon. Elis was known for flax that grew there and nowhere else in Greece, and for the supposed curse preventing mares from bearing foals to he-asses within its borders. The people of Elis fought wars including the conflict with Pisa and later claimed to have recovered Scillus.

CHAPTER VIII.

Elis controlled the games and repeatedly altered their programme, instituting some contests and discontinuing others. Its people are said to have disliked the horse, and they later organized the umpires of the games according to the number of tribes in Elis.

CHAPTER XI.

Elis preserves the rites and traditions of Olympia, including sacrifices to Zeus, Pelops, and many other deities. Its people use only white poplar wood for sacrifices to Zeus, maintain the great altar, and are said to have received the recovered bone of Pelops and made its finder Damarmenus and his descendants custodians.

CHAPTER XV.

Elis has a Town Hall within Altis, where sacrifices, hymns, and banquets for victors at Olympia are conducted. Its people consult the oracle at Libya and honor Greek, Libyan, and heroic figures.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Elis imposed fines on athletes who bribed opponents, arrived too late, or otherwise violated the rules of the games. Its people also restored the temple of Hera, maintained records of Olympic victors, and erected statues from athletic fines.

CHAPTER XXII.

Elis contains and maintains many of the statues and votive offerings described at Olympia. The people of Elis dedicated the largest brazen statue of Zeus in Altis after their war with the Arcadians, and they also brought a statue of Hercules to Olympia.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

Elis supplied many athletes and horsemen whose statues stood at Olympia, and its people controlled the recording and administration of the games. The Eleans excluded their own people from the Isthmian games and did not record the 104th Olympiad because it was conducted by the Pisæans and Arcadians.

CHAPTER XII.

Elis was associated with the dedication of a statue of Areus by its men. Timon, the son of Ægyptus, was also a native of Elis and had a brazen chariot at Olympia.

CHAPTER XIII.

Elis controlled admission to contests and proclaimed victors at Olympia. Its people rejected Pherias as too young in the 78th Olympiad, later allowed him to wrestle among the boys, and proclaimed Phidolas victorious after his mare Aura completed the race without its rider.

CHAPTER XVII.

Elis had many victorious athletes represented by statues at Olympia and maintained the worship of Sosipolis and Ilithyia. Its people defeated an invading Arcadian army after the transformation of the offered child into a dragon, and they removed the Hesperides from the treasury of Epidamnus to the temple of Hera.

CHAPTER XXI.

Elis controlled Olympia and repeatedly opposed Pisa's attempts to conduct the Olympian games. Its notable sites include an old gymnasium, several athletic enclosures, a market-place, temples, porticoes, the Umpires' Hall, and sanctuaries of Aphrodite, Pluto, Fortune, and other gods.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Elis has a theatre and temple of Dionysus, a temple of Athene in its citadel, and the harbor of Cyllene about 120 stades away. Its country is fertile, especially for flax, and its present boundary with Achaia is the river Larisus.

Elis in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Elis is a Peloponnese place opposite the Echinades, bordering Messenia, the Achæans, and Heræa. Its boundary with Heræa is disputed: Arcadians name the Erymanthus, while Elis names the tomb of Corœbus. It features notable athletes and equitable umpires connected with Arrhachion's Olympia victories.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXVI.

The route from Heræa to Elis crosses the Ladon and then reaches the Erymanthus. The boundary between Heræa and Elis is identified as the Erymanthus by the Arcadian account, while the people of Elis identify it as the tomb of Corœbus.

CHAPTER XL.

Elis was associated with the umpires whose equity helped establish Arrhachion's earlier victories at Olympia.
