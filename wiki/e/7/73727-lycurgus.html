Lycurgus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Lycurgus, an eminent virtuous orator, established laws and good government for Lacedaemon, honored by the Delphos oracle; observance raised Lacedaemonians to long Greek sovereignty, while neglect brought sloth, luxury, and loss of authority.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

*BOOKVI.

Lycurgus was eminent for virtue, and the oracle at Delphos saluted him with verses. While the Lacedaemonians observed his laws, they rose from a low condition to great power and held sovereignty over Greece for more than four hundred years; when they neglected those laws and became slothful and luxurious, they lost their sovereign authority.

Lycurgus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Lycurgus is a Lacedaemonian law-maker said to have claimed Apollo gave him his laws, traveled to Egypt, and borrowed Egyptian laws for Sparta; he is also described as king of Thrace and, by some poets, Arabia.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

• And now it is necessary for us to speak of the legislators of Egypt^

Lycurgus is said to have used the same device as the Egyptian lawgivers by claiming that Apollo gave him his laws. He is also reported to have traveled to Egypt and borrowed some of the laws he established in Sparta.

Lycurgus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lycurgus is a legislator for the Lacedæmonians who, according to Herodotus, first served as regent for Labotas during his minority. Traditions traced his laws to Crete or connected them with Delphi. He legislated for the state and for youths’ fights, and changed human sacrifice at Artemis’s altar into the flogging of young men. After Alcander attacked him and he lost an eye, he was said to have built Athene the Eye-preserver’s temple. The Lacedæmonians honored him as a god with a temple, and his statue stood at Platanistas.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Lycurgus served as regent for Labotas during Labotas’ minority, according to Herodotus, and later legislated for the Lacedæmonians. Some accounts said he derived his laws from Crete, while others connected them with Delphi.

CHAPTER XIV.

A statue of Lycurgus stands beside one of the bridges leading into Platanistas, opposite a statue of Hercules. Lycurgus legislated not only for the state generally but also for the fights of the youths.

CHAPTER XV.

The Lacedæmonians built a temple to Lycurgus as to a god, and the tomb of his son Eucosmus stood behind it. Lycurgus changed the human sacrifice formerly offered to Orthian Artemis into the flogging of young men at the goddess’s altar. He was also said to have built the temple of Athene the Eye-preserver after losing one eye when Alcander attacked him.
