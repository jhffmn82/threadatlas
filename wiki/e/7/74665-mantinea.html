Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Mantinea is a wealthy, well-defended city that was besieged, captured, and later became the site of a Theban victory over the Lacedaemonians. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Mantinea is an Arcadian city that supported the Argives, fought the advancing Lacedaemonians, and was forced under Lacedaemonian power after their victory. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Mantinea is an Arcadian town whose geography, history, conflicts, monuments, temples, and traditions are described.

Mantinea in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Mantinea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Mantinea is a wealthy, well-defended city near the Lacedaemonians. After refusing their demand to demolish its walls and return to five villages, it was besieged, flooded when its river was dammed, captured, and its inhabitants were ordered back to their villages. Later, a factional dispute there helped cause a war. Though briefly vulnerable, it was secured by six thousand Athenian auxiliaries under Gegelocus. Mantinea then became the site of a Theban success: the Lacedaemonians were routed, but Epaminondas was killed after winning a famous victory.

The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian / BOOK XV.

Mantinea was a wealthy and well-defended city near the Lacedaemonians. The Lacedaemonians demanded that it demolish its walls and return to five villages, but when Mantinea refused, they marched against it and began a siege.

Mantinea in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Mantinea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Mantinea is a town in Arcadia, founded by Mantineus, son of Lycaon, and transferred to its present site by Antinoe, daughter of Cepheus. It lies by the Ophis and is reached from Argolis through Holm-Oak, Ladder, or the pass over Artemisium; Melangea supplies drinking water, and the Inachus marks its boundary with the Argives. Its boundaries and roads connect it with Tegea, Pallantium, Methydrium, Orchomenus, and Megalopolis, while old Mantinea survives in ruins as The Town. Its history centers on disruption, restoration, and conflict. Agesipolis captured it by diverting the Ophis through its unbaked-brick walls, razed most of it, and dispersed its inhabitants among villages. The Thebans, and specifically Epaminondas, later restored the Mantineans. It was renamed Antigonea under Antigonus, then Adrian restored the name Mantinea. It fought the Lacedæmonians with Elis and Athenian reinforcements, sent an expedition to Sicily from friendship with the Athenians, joined the Achaean League, defeated Agis with Achaean aid under Aratus, and supported Augustus off Actium. Mantinea contains temples, tombs, a theatre, gymnasium, horseracing place, and games to Antinous. Its Poseidon temple barred entry; Æpytus entered, was blinded, and died. It also offered a brazen Apollo at Delphi.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

8 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

The temple of Poseidon at Mantinea forbade entry both then and later. Æpytus entered it, was struck blind, and died not long afterward.

CHAPTER VI.

Mantinea was approached from Argolis by two passes called Holm-Oak and Ladder, as well as by the narrower pass over Artemisium. Melangea supplied its people with drinking water, and the Inachus marked its boundary with the Argives while it flowed along the mountain road.

CHAPTER VIII.

Mantinea was founded by Mantineus, the son of Lycaon, and later transferred to its present site by Antinoe, the daughter of Cepheus. Agesipolis captured it by diverting the Ophis through its unbaked-brick walls, leaving part habitable and razing most of it; the Thebans later restored the Mantineans from surrounding villages. The city was renamed Antigonea under Antigonus, but Adrian later restored the name Mantinea.

CHAPTER IX.

Mantinea contains the temples, monuments, and tombs described in the unit, including the tomb of Arcas near the theatre and a temple of Antinous. It has a Gymnasium room richly decorated with precious stones and paintings, many depicting Antinous, and it contains an imitation of the painting at Ceramicus showing the action of the Athenians at Mantinea.

CHAPTER X.

Mantinea has a horseracing place near its walls, a course where games to Antinous take place, and the nearby temple of Poseidon Hippius. Its forces, commanded by Podares, fought on the right wing against the Lacedæmonians, and the Mantineans attributed their victory partly to Poseidon.

CHAPTER XI.

Mantinea has roads leading to Tegea and Pallantium through or alongside the oak plantation called Pelagos. Its people say that the daughters of Pelias lived there after their father’s death, that Machærion killed Epaminondas, and that Gryllus received a public funeral and statue for his bravery.

CHAPTER XII.

Mantinea has roads leading to the village of Methydrium and to Orchomenus. Near one road are the ruins of old Mantinea, a place still called The Town, and the boundary between Mantinea and Orchomenus is at Mount Anchisia; Petrosaca marks its boundary with Megalopolis.

CHAPTER XIV.

Epaminondas restored the Mantineans, who had been dispersed into villages by Agesipolis, to Mantinea.
