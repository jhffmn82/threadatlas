Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

This is a wide-ranging collection of records from Diodorus Siculus’s Historical Library, chiefly Books XV–XX and later surviving books and fragments. It traces wars, revolutions, tyrannies, successions, and moral examples across Greece, Persia, Egypt, Sicily, Italy, Macedonia, and Rome. Book XV begins with Artaxerxes’ war against Evagoras in Cyprus and Spartan aggression, then follows the rise of Thebes under Epaminondas and Pelopidas, Sparta’s defeat at Leuctra and Mantinea, and recurring Persian attempts to impose peace. Book XVI shifts toward Philip of Macedon, whose victories, wealth, diplomacy, and conquest culminate in Chaeronea, leadership of Greece, preparations against Persia, and assassination; alongside this are the Sacred War, Dion’s struggle in Syracuse, and Timoleon’s liberation and reorganization of Sicily. Book XVII follows Alexander’s accession, destruction of Thebes, invasion of Asia, victories over Darius at Granicus, Issus, and Arbela, conquest of Tyre, Egypt, Persepolis, and India, and his eventual death at Babylon. Supplements add episodes of Alexander’s severity, remorse, adoption of Persian customs, marriage to Roxana, and Indian campaigns. Book XVIII opens the struggle after Alexander’s death: Philip Arrhidaeus and Perdiccas nominally rule, while Ptolemy, Antipater, Craterus, Eumenes, Antigonus, Cassander, Olympias, and others fight for provinces and kingship. Perdiccas is killed, Eumenes is betrayed and executed, Olympias is killed, Cassander murders Roxana and Alexander’s son, and the royal line ends. The later narrative centers on Antigonus, Demetrius, Ptolemy, Seleucus, Lysimachus, Cassander, and Agathocles of Syracuse. Demetrius wins Cyprus and besieges Rhodes but makes peace; Seleucus recovers Babylon; Agathocles invades Africa, defeats Carthaginian forces, then suffers reverses and makes peace. Later books and fragments continue with Pyrrhus, Rome’s wars with Carthage, Hannibal, Macedonian and eastern kingdoms, slave wars, Roman civil conflicts, and repeated reflections that cruelty, ambition, impiety, greed, treachery, and luxury bring ruin, while justice, discipline, clemency, courage, and good government earn praise.

Artaxerxes sent a large Persian army and fleet under Orontas and Teribazus against Evagoras of Salamis in Cyprus. Evagoras initially received aid from Acoris of Egypt and other allies, but after losing a battle at sea and being besieged, he agreed to pay tribute while retaining Salamis as a king subject to Artaxerxes. The Lacedaemonians violated the Peace of Antalcidas by attacking Mantinea, while Dionysius of Syracuse devoted himself to poetry. After Orontas accused Teribazus of treason, Teribazus was tried and acquitted, and the Cyprian War ended with a settlement between Evagoras and Orontas.

The Carthaginians defeated Dionysius at Cronium after his earlier victory at Cabala, and peace was made on terms favorable to Carthage. In Greece, the Lacedaemonians seized the Cadmea at Thebes and continued their war against the Olynthians, suffering a defeat under Teleutias before renewing their preparations.

A plague and internal disorder weakened Carthage, though the Carthaginians later subdued the Africans and recovered Sardinia. The Boeotian War began when the Lacedaemonians detained the Cadmea and drove many Thebans into exile; aided by Athens, the exiles returned, and the Thebans and Athenians forced the Lacedaemonian garrison to surrender. Athens then helped organize a confederacy against the Lacedaemonians, with a general diet or senate meeting at Athens and one representative from each allied city.

Artaxerxes promoted a general peace in Greece so that he could recruit mercenaries against Egypt, but the Thebans rejected its terms. Epaminondas defended the Theban position, and renewed seditions and conflicts spread through Peloponnesus and other Greek cities.

CHAP. V.

The king of Persia sent Pharnabazus and Iphicrates with a large army and fleet to suppress the Egyptian revolt, but their disagreement allowed the Egyptians to strengthen Memphis and forced the Persians to withdraw. Iphicrates later returned to Athens, where his military reforms and skill as a commander were praised. Political conflicts drew the Lacedaemonians and Athenians into struggles over Zacynthus, Corcyra, and Plataea; the Athenians eventually relieved Corcyra under Ctesias. The unit also recounts the murder of Evagoras in Cyprus, devastating earthquakes and inundations in Peloponnesus, and the appearance of the Fiery Beam comet, which was viewed as a sign of the fall of Lacedaemonian sovereignty.

Under Epaminondas, the Thebans defeated the Lacedaemonians at the battle of Leuctra, where Cleombrotus was killed and the Spartan army was routed. Jason of Pherae later persuaded the Thessalians to seek the sovereignty of Greece, while Argos was devastated by a violent civil sedition called Scytalism. Jason was eventually assassinated, and Polydorus succeeded him at Pherae.

Arcadian exiles seized Lassia in Triphylia, leading to war between the Arcadians and Elians, in which the Elians were defeated near Lassia and the Arcadians captured several cities. The Pisates, aided by the Arcadians, took control of the 104th Olympiad and conducted the Olympic games, though the Elians rejected that Olympiad as unlawful. CHAP. X.

The Arcadians divided between the Tegeans and Mantineans, leading the Tegeans to seek Boeotian aid and the Mantineans to seek Athenian and Lacedaemonian support. Epaminondas led the Boeotian alliance, attempted unsuccessfully to surprise Sparta, and then fought the allied forces at Mantinea. The Boeotians routed the Lacedaemonians after Epaminondas killed their general, but Epaminondas was mortally wounded and died after learning that his side had won. The exhausted Greeks then ended the war through a general offensive and defensive league, although the Lacedaemonians refused to join because of their hostility toward the Messenians.

BOOK XVI.

This unit introduces Philip son of Amyntas, who became king of Macedon after Perdiccas was killed fighting the Illyrians. Philip strengthened the Macedonians, defeated an Athenian force supporting Argæus, made peace with Athens, subdued the Pæones, and defeated the Illyrians under Bardyllis. The Illyrians then surrendered the Macedonian cities they held and made peace, while Philip prepared Macedon for the later expedition against Persia that his death prevented him from undertaking.

CHAP. 11.

Dionysius the younger neglected military affairs, made peace with Carthage, fought carelessly against the Lucanians, and ultimately lost his Sicilian dominion. Dion fled Sicily to Corinth, gathered mercenaries, and returned with the aim of restoring liberty to Syracuse and other Sicilians. Civil war in Euboea ended in an agreement, while the Athenians fought the three-year Social War against Chios, Rhodes, Cos, and Byzantium, with Chabrias dying in a naval battle. Philip captured Amphipolis and Pydna, allied with the Olynthians, developed Philippi and its gold mines, and strengthened Macedonia through wealth and military power.

CHAP. III.

Dion landed in Sicily with a small force, gathered supporters, and entered Syracuse after the Syracusans chose him and Megacles as generals with absolute power. The Syracusans celebrated their liberation from Dionysius, but Dionysius soon returned, deceived them with negotiations, and attacked the wall from sea to sea. Dion defeated the Dionysian forces at the wall, after which Dionysius withdrew into the castle and continued seeking provisions and peace. In Greece, Alexander of Pherae was murdered by his wife and two brothers, while Philip subdued the Thessalian tyrants and restored the cities to liberty.

CHAP. IV.

Runaway servants and others in Italy formed a powerful community called the Brutii after capturing several cities. In Sicily, Philistus unsuccessfully invaded the Leontines and later lost a sea fight against the Syracusans, while Dionysius's castle remained under his supporters. Factional conflict and unpaid soldiers weakened Syracuse, and Nypsius's surprise attack allowed Dionysius's forces to capture and sack the city. Dion then returned with his mercenaries, defeated the attackers, relieved Syracuse, and was granted supreme authority by the people.

CHAP. V.

The Social War continued between the Athenians and the Chians, Rhodians, Coians, and Byzantines, with both sides preparing for a naval battle. Chares, Iphicrates, and Timotheus commanded the Athenian forces, but Iphicrates and Timotheus refused to fight during a storm and were fined and removed from command after Chares accused them of treason. Chares later joined Pharnabazus against the Persian king's forces and defeated them, but the Persian king's complaints and the rumored promise of Persian aid to Athens's enemies led the Athenians to make peace with the revolters, ending the war after four years. In Macedonia, Philip defeated the allied Thracians, Paeones, and Illyrians before they could assemble their forces and forced them under the Macedonian kingdom.

CHAP. VI.

The Sacred War began when Philomelus and the Phocians seized the temple at Delphos after the Amphictyons condemned the Phocians for cultivating land claimed by the oracle. Philomelus defeated the Locrians, fortified Delphos, destroyed records of the Amphictyonic decrees, and sought support from Athens, Lacedaemon, and other Greek cities. The Boeotians and Locrians opposed him in defense of the oracle. The unit also recounts the reported discovery of the Delphic oracle, the origin of the tripod, and Philomelus's coercion of Pythia into giving an ambiguous prophecy.

CHAP. VII.

Philomelus raised Phocian forces, defeated the Locrians at the Rocks of Phaedra, and later used the sacred treasures to recruit mercenaries for the Phocian war. The Boeotians, Thessalians, Locrians, and other allies opposed the Phocians, while the Athenians and Lacedaemonians supported them. Philomelus was defeated by the Boeotians and killed himself by leaping from a precipice, after which Onomarchus became general. Onomarchus renewed the war, achieved several successes, and was ultimately defeated and killed by Philip of Macedon in Thessaly.

CHAP. Vlll.

Phayllus raised mercenaries and secured support from several cities for the Phocians, but suffered defeats against the Boeotians before dying of a lingering illness. Phalaecus succeeded him as Phocian general, suffered defeats at Chaeronea, and later took and abandoned Chaeronea. The Lacedaemonians and Megalopolitans fought a war involving their allies, while the Thebans intervened on the Megalopolitans' side. The Thebans later sought and received three hundred talents from Artaxerxes to support their efforts in the Phocian war.

Artaxerxes, surnamed Ochus, renewed the Persian war against the Egyptians and later campaigned against Phoenicia and Cyprus. The Phoenicians, led by Sidon, and the nine Cypriot kingdoms rebelled against Persian rule, but Persian forces and their allies recovered the territories. Mentor the Rhodian and Temnes betrayed Sidon, which fell after Artaxerxes ordered the deaths of its leading citizens and supplicants. Many Sidonians burned themselves and their households, while the other Phoenician cities surrendered to the Persians.

CHAP. IX.

Artaxerxes marched against Egypt with Persian, Greek, and allied forces, while Nectanebus defended the country with Egyptian, African, and Greek troops. After Persian victories at Pelusium and elsewhere, Mentor used stratagems to bring Bubastis and other Egyptian cities into submission. Nectanebus abdicated, fled to Ethiopia, and left Artaxerxes in possession of Egypt, which he plundered and whose cities he partly demolished. Artaxerxes rewarded Mentor, while Bagoas became his powerful viceroy in the higher parts of Asia.

CHAP. X.

Philip captured several cities in the Hellespont, including Olynthus, after bribing Olynthus's chief magistrates, and sold its citizens into slavery. The Athenians, urged chiefly by Demosthenes, opposed Philip and encouraged Greek cities to defend their liberties, but Philip claimed that bribery expanded his power more effectively than warfare. The Phocians continued the Sacred War while their leaders and soldiers used the sacred treasures of Delphi, provoking accusations of sacrilege and eventual divine punishment. The war ended when Phalaecus and his mercenaries withdrew and the Phocians surrendered to Philip, after which the Amphictyonic Council imposed severe penalties on Phocis and admitted Philip and his posterity to the council with two votes.

The Syracusans, divided by intestine seditions and oppressed by many tyrants, asked Corinth to send a general to govern Syracuse and restrain ambition. Corinth chose Timoleon, who had killed his brother Timophanes after Timophanes acted as an absolute tyrant and appeared to seek sovereignty. While Timoleon's trial was still unsettled, the Corinthian senate sent him to Syracuse, declaring that his conduct there would determine whether he was judged a tyrant-killer or a brother-murderer. Timoleon later subdued the Carthaginians, rebuilt the destroyed Grecian cities, and restored Sicily to liberty.

CHAP. XI.

Timoleon the Corinthian sailed from Corinth to Sicily with mercenaries, escaped a Carthaginian blockade at Rhegium, defeated Hicetas near Adranum, and entered Syracuse. The Carthaginians brought a large army and fleet into Sicily but withdrew after Timoleon received reinforcements. Timoleon gained control of Syracuse, secured the surrender and exile of Dionysius, demolished the tyrant's strongholds, and established laws for a democracy. Philip invaded the Illyrians and Thracians from Macedonia and strengthened his influence among the Greek cities.

CHAP. XII.

Timoleon defeated or expelled tyrants in Sicily, liberated several cities, and gained the submission of many Greek and Sicilian communities. Carthage responded by raising troops, hiring foreign mercenaries, collecting money, and preparing a large fleet against him. Philip of Macedon conducted a prolonged and technically formidable siege of Perinthus, but Persian and Byzantine aid strengthened the defenders. Philip then divided his army, leaving part at Perinthus while marching with the rest to besiege Byzantium.

CHAP, XIII.

Athens, joined by Chios, Coos, Rhodes, and other Greeks, sent a fleet to aid Byzantium against Philip, who then raised his sieges and made peace. The Carthaginians invaded Sicily with a large army and fleet, but Timoleon defeated them in a battle aided by a sudden storm, killing or capturing many and taking their equipment. Timoleon then subdued tyrants in Sicily, freed the cities, resettled lands, revised Syracuse's laws, and established peace and prosperity. Syracuse and other Sicilian cities were enlarged by new colonists, while Argyra received inhabitants and public buildings.

CHAP. XIV.

Philip of Macedon captured Elatea and prepared to invade Attica, causing alarm in Athens. Demosthenes persuaded the Athenians to seek Boeotian assistance, and the Athenians and Boeotians united at Chaeronea against Philip. Philip and his son Alexander defeated them in the Battle of Chaeronea, after which Philip made peace with Athens and Boeotia. Lysicles was executed by the Athenians, while Philip was appointed general of all Greece and planned a war against Persia; Timoleon also died in Syracuse after governing there for eight years.

CHAP, XV.

Philip began a war against the Persians and sent Attalus and Parmenio into Asia to free the Greek cities there. He consulted the Delphic oracle, whose ambiguous answer he interpreted as predicting victory over the Persian king, and he celebrated the marriage of his daughter Cleopatra to Alexander, king of Epirus. At the festivities, Neoptolemus recited verses that further encouraged Philip in his Persian expedition. During a ceremony at the theatre at Aegae, Pausanias of Orestis stabbed Philip and was killed while attempting to flee.

BOOK XVII.

After Philip, the son of Amyntas, died, Alexander secured his accession by punishing his father's murderers, winning support in Thessaly and Greece, and compelling resistance by force. Athens sought pardon, Alexander was recognized as general of Greece, and the Greeks authorized aid against Persia. Attalus conspired against Alexander and was killed by Hecateus on the king's orders, while Bagoas murdered Persian kings before Darius Codomannus forced him to drink poison. Darius prepared Persia for war, while Memnon of Rhodes and other commanders conducted operations in Asia against Macedonian forces.

Alexander subdued disturbances in Greece and neighboring regions before returning to Macedonia when Thebes and other Greek cities rejected his sovereignty. He besieged and captured Thebes after the Thebans resisted with outside aid, and the city was plundered, its people killed or enslaved, and its walls razed by decree of the Greek council. Alexander later pardoned the Athenian orators demanded from Athens and returned to Macedonia, where he began preparations for an expedition into Asia against the Persian monarchy.

CHAP. It.

Alexander crossed from Europe into Asia with a Macedonian army and claimed possession of Asia after landing at Troas. At the Granicus, the Macedonians defeated the Persian forces; Alexander killed Spithrobates and survived an attack by Rhosaces. Alexander then took Sardis and Miletus, dismissed most of his fleet, restored Ada to the rule of Caria, and besieged Halicarnassus. After fierce fighting, the Persian commanders abandoned Halicarnassus, and Alexander razed the city while the citadel remained defended.

Darius fled, while his mother, wife, son, and daughters were captured, and the Macedonians took the Persian camp and its treasures.

CHAP. IV.

After the battle of Issus, Alexander marched toward Egypt and besieged Tyre, which refused him entry and resisted for seven months. The Macedonians built a mole to Tyre and eventually captured the city after overcoming extensive Tyrian defenses and naval resistance. The Tyrians suffered heavy losses, enslavement, and the execution of surviving young men, while Ballominus, a poor man of royal lineage, was made king of Tyre. Amyntas attempted to raise opposition to Alexander in Egypt but was defeated and killed, and the Greeks later sent ambassadors to congratulate Alexander on his victories, including the capture of Gaza.

Alexander marched into Egypt, where the cities submitted to him, and then undertook a journey to the temple of Ammon. During the journey, rain, stored water, and crows helped Alexander and his companions cross the desert and reach the grove of Ammon. At the temple, the oracle declared Alexander divinely favored, promised him victory, and confirmed that Philip’s murderers had been punished. Alexander then returned to Egypt, founded Alexandria, and arranged its streets, walls, palace, and defenses.

Darius assembled a vast army, equipped it with longer weapons and hooked chariots, and marched from Babylon toward Nineveh, encamping at Arbela. He made two peace offers to Alexander, but Alexander rejected them and demanded that Darius submit or fight for the empire. At the battle of Arbela, the Persian chariots initially terrified the Macedonians, but Alexander's countermeasure caused many to turn back or be destroyed. Alexander eventually routed Darius, although Mazaeus severely pressured the Macedonian wing before Parmenio rallied it; the Persians suffered over ninety thousand deaths, while the Macedonians lost about five hundred.

CHAP. VI.

After Alexander's victory at Arbela, many Grecians conspired to revolt and resolved to aid Darius with money for foreign soldiers. Memnon rebelled in Thrace, prompting Antipater to settle matters there and march into Greece. The Lacedaemonians formed a confederate league with many Peloponnesians and others, raised an army under Agis, and fought Antipater. Agis was killed, and the Lacedaemonians and their allies were defeated and retreated toward Sparta.

CHAP. VU.

After defeating Darius at Arbela, Alexander entered Babylon, distributed money to his troops, and marched through Sitacana, Susa, and the country of the Usians toward Persepolis. He overcame the defenders of the Susian Rocks, received the surrender of Persepolis, rewarded nearly eight hundred maimed Greek captives, and gave the city to his soldiers for plunder. Alexander seized the immense Persian treasury and the treasures of the Persepolis citadel, then held a feast at which Thais persuaded him to burn the palace. Darius fled toward Bactria and was treacherously murdered by Bessus before Alexander found his body and had it honorably buried.

The Lacedemonians were routed by Antipater in a great battle and were forced to send ambassadors to him. Antipater postponed their appeal until the general assembly of Greece met at Corinth. After disputes on both sides, the matter was left to Alexander's decision. Antipater received the chiefest of the nobility of Sparta as hostages, and the Lacedemonians sent ambassadors into Asia to beg pardon for their late revolt.

CHAP. VIII.

Bessus, Nabarzanes, and Barzaentes flee to Bactria after Darius's death, and Bessus raises an uprising by persuading the Bactrians to claim their liberty and recognize him as king. Alexander rewards and dismisses the Greek auxiliaries, campaigns through Hyrcania and the Mardian country, recovers his war horse, and receives Thalestris, queen of the Amazons. Alexander adopts elements of Persian royal dress and courtly customs before dealing with rebellions and a plot involving Dimnus, Ceballinus, Philotas, and others. He then campaigns against the Arimaspi, appoints Teridates over the Arimaspi and Gedrosians, and subdues Arachosia.

CHAP. IX.

Artaxerxes ordered Tithraustes to seize and execute Tissaphernes, after which Tithraustes made a six-month truce with Agesilaus. In Greece, the Phocians and Lacedaemonians fought the Boeotians and Athenians at Haliartus, where Lysander was killed; Conon then went to Babylon and secured Persian support to ruin the Lacedaemonian fleet.

CHAP. IX,

The unit describes the confederate war against the Lacedaemonians, including the battles at Aricas, the river Nemea, and Coronea, and the Persian fleet's victory over the Lacedaemonian fleet at Cnidus under Conon and Pharnabazus. It recounts the weakening of Lacedaemonian power, the rebuilding of Athens and the Piraeus walls, and the revolts of several cities against Lacedaemon. It also covers the Corinthian War and sedition, including fighting at Lechaeum and the actions of Iphicrates. The later section concerns Dionysius's conflicts with Rhegium and the Sicilians, Mago's campaign in Sicily, and the Carthaginian defeat at Abacene.

CHAP. XL

Thrasybulus led an Athenian fleet to Ionia, the Chersonesus, Lesbos, and Rhodes, but later died after an attack by the Aspendians. The Carthaginians under Mago invaded Sicily with a large army, but Dionysius used Agyrus and the Agyrinenses to isolate them and then concluded peace with Carthage. Rhodes defected from Athens and accepted Lacedaemonian assistance. Evagoras became king of Salamis and expanded his power in Cyprus, provoking Persian intervention under Artaxerxes, while the Lacedaemonian general Thimbron was defeated and killed by Struthius in Asia.

CHAP. XU.

Dionysius of Syracuse led expeditions into Italy, first suffering a naval defeat near Rhegium and later defeating the Greek forces at the battle at the river Helorus before besieging Rhegium. The Lucanians defeated the Thurians after the Thurians invaded Lucania without waiting for their confederates, but Leptines rescued about a thousand survivors and helped establish peace. Dionysius besieged Caulonia, destroyed the city, and transported its inhabitants to Syracuse. He later pursued the siege of Rhegium and sent lavish chariots, tents, and poems to the Olympic games, where his poetry was ridiculed.

CHAP. XIII.

The Lacedaemonians, exhausted by war, made peace with Artaxerxes through Antalcidas, on terms requiring the Greek cities in Asia to submit and the other Grecians to govern themselves. Artaxerxes then prepared a Cyprian War against Euagoras, while Dionysius captured Rhegium after a prolonged famine and cruelly punished Philo and his son. The Gauls invaded Italy, defeated the Romans at the river Allia, and sacked Rome, forcing the Romans to take refuge in the Capitol and Veii. Marcus Furius later defeated the Volsci and Gauls, while Marcus Manlius helped repel a Gallic attempt to enter the Capitol.
