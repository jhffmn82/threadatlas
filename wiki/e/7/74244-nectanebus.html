Nectanabis in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Nectanabis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Nectanabis, king of Egypt and commander of its forces, relied on difficult entrances and natural defenses, fortifying Pelusium and guarding Nile crossings. Tachos's son, he joined a revolt against his father, won commanders and soldiers with gifts and promises, took Egypt, then led over one hundred thousand men against Tachos and was defeated by Agesilaus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. V.

Nectanabis, king of Egypt, knew the strength of the Persian forces and relied on Egypt's difficult entrances and natural defenses. He especially fortified Pelusium and protected the Nile crossings and fords.

CHAR XL

Nectanabis, Tachos's son, participated in the revolt against his father and drew Egyptian commanders and soldiers to his side with gifts and promises. He took control of Egypt and led more than one hundred thousand men against Tachos, but his forces were defeated by Agesilaus.

Nectanebus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Nectanebus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Nectanebus is king of Egypt during war with the Persians. He commands Egyptian, African, and Greek forces, fortified passages, garrisons, river-boats, and positions; he also sends four thousand Greek mercenaries to aid Phoenician confederates. After Persian advance, defeat, and the fall of cities, he withdraws to Memphis, abdicates, gathers treasure, and flees to Ethiopia.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

In Asia, the Persian king having invaded ligypt some years before

Nectanebus was king of Egypt while it was at war with the Persians. The Phoenicians sent messengers to him and asked to be received as confederates, and he supplied four thousand Greek mercenaries to aid them.

CHAP. IX.

Nectanebus, king of Egypt, commanded twenty thousand Greek mercenaries, as many Africans, and sixty thousand Egyptian warriors, besides numerous river-boats and fortified positions. After the defeat of his forces and the fall of several cities, he withdrew to Memphis, abdicated his kingdom, gathered much treasure, and fled to Ethiopia.
