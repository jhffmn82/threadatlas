Olympias in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Olympias is a queen of Macedonia, daughter of Neoptolemus, wife of Philip, mother of Alexander the Great and Alexander IV, and sister of Alexander of Epirus. Her influence is defined by royal guardianship, conflict, and a violent struggle for Macedonia. She envied and threatened Hephaestion, then quarreled with Antipater; her accusations helped estrange him from Alexander. After withdrawing to Epirus, she was invited by Polyperchon to return as queen-regent and care for Alexander’s young son. She sought Eumenes’s support for herself and the kings, though he advised remaining in Epirus until war ended, and she ordered Nicanor to surrender Munychia and the Piraeus to the Athenians. Returning with Polyperchon and Alexander’s son, she gained custody of both kings and the kingdom without bloodshed. She then had Philip III Arrhidaeus killed, forced Eurydice’s death, killed Nicanor and one hundred Macedonians associated with Cassander, and became hated for cruelty. As Cassander advanced, she commanded Aristonus and occupied Pydna with Alexander’s son, Roxana, her household, and kindred. Siege, famine, failed relief, and weakening allies led her to surrender despite terms preserving her person. She refused an offer to flee, was condemned by the Macedonian assembly in her absence, and was killed by relatives of her victims.

CHAP. XII. /

Olympias envied Hephaestion and accused and threatened him in letters. Hephaestion replied with severe rebukes, asserting that Alexander would judge between them. She later quarreled with Antipater, and her accusations contributed to Alexander’s estrangement from him.

CHAP. IV.

Olympias had withdrawn to Epirus because of her quarrels with Antipater. Polyperchon invited her to return to Macedonia, care for Alexander’s son, and reside there as queen-regent.

Cassander, though he was excluded from the chief command of

Olympias, Alexander's mother, was in Epirus because of fear of Cassander. Polyperchon urged her to return quickly to Macedonia and care for Alexander's little son until he reached the age to govern.

CHAP. V.

Olympias urged Eumenes to assist her and the kings, whom she regarded as needing his faithful service. She asked whether she should remain in Epirus or return, and Eumenes advised her to remain there until the war ended.

While these things were acting, Nicanor (who held Muayohis)

Olympias wrote to Nicanor commanding him to deliver Munychia and the Piraeus to the Athenians. The Athenians hoped that her restored royal authority would help secure their liberties and planned to honor her publicly.

Eurydice being queen-regent in Macedonia, as soon as she heard

Olympias returned to Macedonia with Polyperchon and Alexander's son and gained custody of both kings and the kingdom without bloodshed. She treated Eurydice and Philip cruelly, had Philip killed, and compelled Eurydice to take her own life; she then killed Nicanor and one hundred Macedonians associated with Cassander, causing many Macedonians to hate her.

As for the affairs of Europe, Cassander, while he lay at the siege of

Olympias appointed Aristonus to fight Cassander and withdrew into Pydna with Alexander's son, Roxana, and other relatives. Her position worsened as Cassander surrounded Pydna, Aeacidas failed to relieve her, the Epirotes allied with Cassander, and Polyperchon's army was weakened by bribery.

Olympias endured the siege and famine at Pydna before surrendering to Cassander on terms preserving her person. She refused Cassander’s offer to flee, was condemned in her absence by the Macedonians, and was killed by the relatives of men she had executed.
