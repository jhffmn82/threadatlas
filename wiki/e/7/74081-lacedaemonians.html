Lacedaemonians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Lacedaemonians were a Greek people and Spartan polity, the citizens of Sparta, whose city was Sparta. They were credited with laws and good government under Lycurgus and with holding sovereignty over Greece for more than four hundred years while observing those laws. At their height they ruled Greece by sea and land and were regarded as populous, expert, persistent soldiers. They ruled Messenia after subduing the Messenians and promoted oligarchies in Greek cities. Their power was expressed through interventions and wars: they captured Mantinea by diverting its river, aided Zacynthus, fought the Olynthians, seized and forcibly detained the Cadmea, and violated the Peace of Antalcidas by attacking Mantinea and intervening in other cities. Ambitious to recover or retain sovereignty, they broke peace with Athens, invaded Boeotia, prepared war against Thebes and its allies, and demanded autonomy for Boeotian cities and the rebuilding of Plataea and Thespis. Defeats progressively weakened them. They suffered defeat at Naxos, lost about four thousand men at Leuctra, appealed to Athens for help, defended Sparta, and were defeated or routed in conflicts at Tegea and Mantinea. Their sovereignty over Greece was said to have fallen after major defeats at Leuctra and Mantinea. Lacedæmonians in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Lacedæmonians are a Dorian, Spartan people whose city is Sparta. They maintained two royal houses, followed the laws associated with Lycurgus, preserved temples, monuments, and traditions, and at times received kings’ bodies and buried Agesilaus with unmatched honours. Their history is dominated by warfare, expansion, changing alliances, and later reversals. They established Sparta and expanded against neighboring peoples. In the wars with Messenia, they secretly prepared, swore not to end the war before winning, and captured Amphea in a night attack. After raids, battles, defeat, renewed war, and the capture of Ithome, they imposed tribute and mourning obligations on the Messenians. In the later struggle they fought Aristomenes, bribed Aristocrates before the Great Trench, besieged Eira for eleven years with Corinthian and Cretan support, captured it, incorporated captives among the Helots, conquered and divided Messenia, and granted Mothone to the people of Nauplia. They also fought widely in Argolis, Elis, Bœotia, Athens, and elsewhere. Under Nicander they invaded Argolis with Asine; they later held Thyrea briefly, but Philip son of Amyntas confined them to their original boundaries. They delayed at Marathon by custom, defeated Athenians at Tanagra, were routed at Leuctra, and suffered defeat at Sellasia. They supported Athenians, fought or intervened in conflicts involving Thebes, Elis, Argos, and Messenia, and bred horses with exceptional attention after the invasion of the Medes. Their power was repeatedly challenged: Pyrrhus nearly took Sparta despite Argive and Messenian allies; Antigonus and the Achæans captured Sparta; and, after Cleomenes’s reign, they adopted a republican government. Yet they recovered their old polity after the capture, retained Sparta, and later had friendly relations with the Messenians when those people aided them against Pyrrhus.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Lacedæmonians had formerly refused to admit that they could be conquered by a land army, despite earlier reverses. When Pyrrhus invaded, they fought with allies from Argos and Messene, but Pyrrhus defeated them and came close to taking Sparta.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

The Lacedæmonians delayed marching against the Persians at Marathon because their custom was not to campaign until the moon was full. They later requested aid from the Athenians during the Helot revolt but sent Cimon's picked men back under suspicion. At Tanagra they defeated the Athenians after the Thessalians betrayed them, but they were later completely routed by the Bœotians at Leuctra.

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians and Agis seized Pellene but withdrew after Aratus defeated them. Antigonus and the Achæans later restored their old political constitution after capturing Lacedæmon.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Lacedæmonians repeatedly fought the Argives and seized territory in Argolis until Philip the son of Amyntas confined them to their original boundaries. They were defeated by the Argive people after the outrage committed by Bryas, and they later withdrew from their attack on Argos when Telesilla and the armed Argive women resisted them.

CHAPTER XXXV.

The Lacedæmonians, under their king Nicander, invaded Argolis, and the people of Asine joined them in ravaging Argive territory. They later defeated the Argives at Thyrea and held the district for a time before giving it to the Æginetans.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

The Lacedæmonians established Sparta, maintained two royal houses, and expanded through wars against neighboring peoples including the Cynurians, Achæans, Argives, Arcadians, Messenians, Tegeans, and others. They founded colonies, practiced the laws associated with Lycurgus, fought at Thermopylæ and Platæa, and later intervened in Athens, Bœotia, Argolis, and Chalcidice.

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians fought the Bœotians at Leuctra, intervened in conflicts involving Athens, Argos, Messenia, Elis, and Thebes, and repeatedly disputed royal succession. They later sent Agesilaus to Asia Minor and fought the Corinthian War against the Thebans and their allies.

CHAPTER X.

The Lacedæmonians buried Agesilaus with greater honours than they had shown to any of their other kings. During the reign of Archidamus, they publicly offered aid to the Phocians, ostensibly from friendship but, according to the text, really in hostility to the Thebans. They also received the bodies of their kings and maintained numerous temples, monuments, and traditions in Sparta.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Lacedæmonians preserve traditions about Therapne, Menelaus, Helen, fountains, temples, and the surrounding landscape. They say that Menelaus and Helen were buried at Therapne, that certain fountains had ancient names, and that Hercules was hidden at the Eleusinium on Mount Taygetus while Æsculapius healed his wound. They also previously imposed their rule on the maritime communities later known as the Eleutherolacones.

BOOK IV.--MESSENIA.

The Lacedæmonians participated in the settlement of Messenia, later disputed traditions concerning Teleclus and the sanctuary of Artemis Limnas, and went to war against Messenia. They secretly prepared for war, swore not to abandon it until Messenia was won, and captured Amphea under Alcamenes.

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians raid Messenia, carry off cattle, grain, and fruit, and attack some towns without capturing them. They fight the Messenians in two major battles and later resume the war after hearing of the oracle.

CHAPTER XI.

The Lacedæmonians suffered a major defeat in the battle described in Chapter XI and lost many excellent warriors. They unsuccessfully used supposed deserters and attempted to win over the Messenian allies, later captured Ithome, imposed tribute and other obligations on the Messenians, and fought the renewed Messenian rising in the second Messenian War.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Lacedæmonians fight Aristomenes and are repeatedly routed, but they recover after he loses his shield and later defeat the Messenians at the Great Trench by bribing Aristocrates. They besiege Eira, decree that Messenia and nearby parts of Laconia should not be cultivated, and use Corinthian forces and Cretan bowmen against the Messenians. Their siege ultimately benefits from a deserter's knowledge that the citadel guard has abandoned its posts.

CHAPTER XXI.

The Lacedæmonians captured Eira after prolonged fighting and incorporated the captured Messenians into the Helot population. Their forces used a rotation of fighting and resting to outlast the exhausted Messenians, and later plundered Messenian property.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Lacedæmonians conquered and divided Messenia, excepting the territory of the Asinæi, and gave Mothone to the people of Nauplia. They later provoked the revolt of the Messenian Helots through the killing of suppliants at Tænarum, resisted the restoration of the Messenians, and eventually fought them again after the Thebans withdrew from the Peloponnese.

CHAPTER XXIX.

The Lacedæmonians had friendly relations with the Messenians after the latter aided them against Pyrrhus. They were later opposed by the Achæans, defeated at Sellasia with Messenian participation, and ruled successively by Machanidas and Nabis after Cleomenes had been removed.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

The Lacedæmonians gave Mothone to the people of Nauplia after they had been expelled from their city for their Laconian sympathies. After the restoration of the Messenians, the Lacedæmonians did not prevent the people of Nauplia from remaining at Mothone.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

The Lacedæmonians joined Elis, reluctantly, in invading Attica, but later fought Elis after Elis allied with the Mantineans, Argives, and Athenians. They were defeated by Elis at Olympia during an incursion under Agis, and Elis later joined Philip in attacking them because of their ancient hostility.

CHAPTER XXII.

The Lacedæmonians are listed first among the states that fought at Platæa and dedicated a statue of Zeus after their second war with the Messenians. They also dedicated a 12-foot statue of Zeus bearing an inscription, and their citizens included the sculptors Aristo and Telestas.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

After the invasion of the Medes, the Lacedæmonians devoted themselves more than other Greeks to breeding horses. They erected statues of Spartan horse-breeders and sent a statue of Archidamus to Olympia; they also invaded Elis under Agis because of the treatment of Lichas.

Lacedæmonians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Lacedæmonians are a Greek people of Sparta, repeatedly defined by war, shifting alliances, internal upheaval, and eventual subordination within the Achæan League. They fought widely: they besieged and captured Platæa, invaded Tegea, Arcadia, Phigalia, Mantinea, and Megalopolis, and at times defeated opponents or captured their cities. They also suffered major reverses, including defeat at Tegea, defeat and heavy losses at Leuctra, where Epaminondas kept them shut up before permitting their departure, and later weakening by the Achæans. Their power had first been reduced by Epaminondas and afterwards through war with the Achæans; after Leuctra, their decemvirates and Harmosts were removed from towns liberated by the Thebans. Their relations with neighboring peoples repeatedly changed: Arcadians once served with them but later joined the Thebans; Mantinea was alternately conquered, allied, and hostile; and they were uniquely described as Greeks openly unfriendly to the Achæans. Under Cleomenes they changed from the Spartan kingdom, fought the Achæans and Antigonus at Sellasia, and later captured Megalopolis. Under Philopœmen, their light troops won at Mantinea but their hoplites were routed; he subsequently forced them into the Achæan League. Their impending civil war brought the exile of about 300 leaders and the sale of about 3,000 Helots. After Nabis was overthrown, they became their own masters but were brought into the League. Achæan measures destroyed Sparta’s fortifications and replaced their youth training, while the Romans later restored their national training. Disputes over jurisdiction, land, exiles, fines, and alleged Roman decisions led them to appeal to Metellus and the Roman Senate, send embassies, and seek relief while acknowledging they could not fight the Achæans. They nevertheless took up arms, were defeated with about 1,000 losses, and later violated a truce to sack Iasus. The records also preserve their killing of suppliants at Tænarum, followed by a violent earthquake, alongside an episode in which Athenian protection allowed Lacedæmonian suppliants to depart safely.

CHAPTER VII.

The Lacedæmonians were the only Greeks described as unfriendly to the Achæans and openly took up arms against them. Their forces suffered defeat at Sellasia at the hands of the Achæans and Antigonus after Cleomenes broke his word.

CHAPTER VIII.

After Nabis was overthrown, the Lacedæmonians became independent again but were later brought into the Achæan League. The Achæans destroyed Sparta’s fortifications, prevented its youths from training according to Lycurgus, and made them train in the Achæan way; the Lacedæmonians then sought the support of Metellus and his colleagues.

CHAPTER IX.

The Lacedæmonians were the subject of a dispute with the Achæans. Roman intervention restored exiles to Sparta, remitted certain fines, left them in the Achæan League, and allowed them to send envoys to Rome.

CHAPTER XI.

The Lacedæmonians were involved in disputes with the Argives that Gallus was sent to arbitrate. Gallus treated them with great contempt, and Lacedæmonians in the Achaean army opposed making an inroad into Attica.

CHAPTER XII.

The Lacedæmonians consulted the Roman Senate about disputed land and disputed Diæus's account of the Senate's decision. Knowing they could not fight the Achæans, they sent embassies to the cities and spoke privately to Diæus. Menalcidas later deceived them by claiming that Rome had removed them altogether from the jurisdiction of the Achæan League.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Lacedæmonians took up arms to defend their country but were defeated, losing about 1,000 men in the battle before the rest fled into Sparta. Later, Menalcidas persuaded them to violate the truce and sack Iasus.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Lacedæmonians were among the peoples whom the Roman Senate said should not be forced into the Achæan League. Lacedæmonians residing at Corinth were attacked and plundered by the Achæans, and those arrested were later imprisoned.

CHAPTER XXV.

Some Lacedæmonians entered Athens by night during the reign of Codrus, then fled to the Areopagus and the altars of the August when their army withdrew. The Athenians allowed these suppliants to leave safely. Earlier, Lacedæmonians who killed suppliants in the temple of Poseidon at Tænarum were followed by a long and violent earthquake.

CHAPTER V.

The Lacedæmonians, under Charillus, invaded the district around Tegea but were defeated by the men and women of Tegea. They later fought against the Messenians, while the Arcadians supported the Messenians.

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians fought against the Arcadians and had the Arcadians serve under them against the Athenians and in Agesilaus's expedition to Asia Minor. After their defeat at Leuctra, the Arcadians abandoned them and joined the Thebans; the Arcadians also feared that they would ravage Arcadia if the Arcadian young men went to fight at Thermopylæ.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Lacedæmonians fought against Mantinea at various times. Under King Agesipolis they invaded and captured Mantinea, while later Mantinea made an offensive and defensive alliance with them against the Thebans. Mantinea subsequently became hostile to them, defeated Agis with Achaean assistance, and helped the Achæans weaken their power.

CHAPTER X.

The Lacedæmonians extended their battle line to avoid being outflanked, with Agis and his staff in the centre. They pressed Aratus’s division fiercely, but after Aratus withdrew and the Arcadians attacked their flanks, they were surrounded and suffered heavy losses.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The Lacedæmonians tried to prevent the colonization of Megalopolis and later repeatedly attacked it. They were defeated under Acrotatus, defeated the Megalopolitans under Agis but failed to capture the city when the North Wind shattered Agis’ battering-ram, and ultimately captured Megalopolis under Cleomenes.

CHAPTER XXXIX.

The Lacedæmonians attacked Arcadia and invaded Phigalia, defeating its inhabitants and besieging the town. They captured Phigalia and either forced the Phigalians to leave under terms of war or allowed them to evacuate. Their garrison was later driven out by the men from Oresthasium.

CHAPTER XLVIII.

The Lacedæmonians fought the people of Tegea in the first invasion led by Charillus. They were routed after the women appeared in the battle, and Charillus was captured.

CHAPTER L.

The light-armed Lacedæmonians defeated the light-armed Achæan troops at Mantinea, but Philopœmen routed their hoplites and killed Machanidas. Later, Philopœmen defeated and killed many Lacedæmonians in a night attack after setting fire to their camp at Gythium.

CHAPTER LI.

The Lacedæmonians were compelled by Philopœmen to join the Achæan League. They offered Philopœmen the house of Nabis, but he directed them to use their gifts to conciliate influential men in the Achæan League instead. During their impending civil war, about 300 leaders were exiled and about 3000 Helots were sold into slavery.

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I.

The Lacedæmonians besieged and captured Platæa during the Peloponnesian war, blockading it with a double wall. They later occupied Cadmea, an action that the Platæans said they had neither proposed nor helped carry out.

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians were initially friendly with the Thebans after the departure of the Medes. After the war between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, the Thebans joined the Corinthians against them; the Lacedæmonians defeated the Thebans at Corinth and Coronea but were defeated at Leuctra, after which their decemvirates and Harmosts were removed from their towns.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Lacedæmonians attacked the Thebans with their own forces and those of their allies. They were defeated at Leuctra, lost more than 1,000 men, and buried their dead only after their allies had done so.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Lacedæmonians were kept shut up at Leuctra by Epaminondas after the battle. When the Spartans came in full force to relieve them, Epaminondas allowed them to depart on conditions of war.
