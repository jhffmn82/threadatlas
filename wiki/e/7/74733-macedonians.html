Macedonians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Macedonians are a people repeatedly shown as the core soldiers and army of Macedonia, first restored by Philip after a disastrous defeat by the Illyrians. Philip rebuilds their confidence through speeches, training, and close formation, then leads them to victories over Athenians, Paeones, Illyrians, and allied Thracian powers, raising the kingdom’s wealth, majesty, and glory. Under Alexander they become an expansive campaigning force: they capture Thebes, cross into Asia in large numbers, defeat Persian forces at the Granicus and Issus, seize cities and treasures, build the broad mole and take Tyre, cross the Tigris, and defeat the Persians despite fear, wounds, and casualties. Their campaigns include plundering Persepolis, burning its palace, hard marches through snow and India, assaults on forts and cities, and difficult operations using sarissas, crampirons, cords, and signals. They are recognized for hardiness and valor, but also commit killings, enslavement, and plunder. Their relationship with Alexander is central but unstable. They at times follow him through extreme hazards, rejoice at successes, and receive rewards; at other times they want to return home, reject his plans, refuse to cross the Ganges, object to Persian appointments, and resist divine honors for him. After his death, they become a decisive military and political body: they support or abandon rival commanders and rulers, choose protectors, mutiny, condemn enemies, favor Polyperchon, shift between Eurydice, Olympias, Cassander, Antigonus, and Eumenes, and ultimately betray Eumenes after suffering the loss of their families and baggage. Their later history ends in defeat by Paulus Æmilius and the Romans, followed by liberation and reduced tribute from the Roman Senate; Macedonians in Rome are then ordered to leave Italy after war is declared against Perseus.

BOOK XVI.

The Macedonians suffered a major defeat against the Illyrians, losing more than four thousand men and becoming fearful of continuing the war. Philip restored their confidence through speeches and military training, organized them into a close formation, and led them to victories against the Athenians, Pæones, and Illyrians.

CHAP. 11.

The Macedonians benefited from Philip's victories, wealth, and military leadership. Philip raised the kingdom of Macedonia to greater majesty and glory than it had previously possessed.

CHAP. V.

The Macedonians were opposed by an alliance of the kings of Thrace, the Paeones, and the Illyrians. Under Philip's leadership, they defeated the allied peoples and brought them under the Macedonian kingdom.

The Macedonians besieged and captured Thebes after a prolonged and bloody battle. More than five hundred Macedonians were killed, and Alexander arranged for their burial.

CHAP. It.

The Macedonians crossed into Asia with Alexander and numbered about thirty thousand foot and four thousand five hundred horse according to the account. They defeated the Persian forces at the Granicus, took Miletus, and fought fiercely during the siege of Halicarnassus.

CHAP. III.

The Macedonians defeated the Persian army at Issus after fierce fighting. They pursued the Persians, took Darius's camp and treasures, and some treated the captive women brutally, while more moderate Macedonians pitied them.

CHAP. IV.

The Macedonians built and guarded the mole, attacked Tyre from land and sea, and repeatedly fought their way toward the walls despite severe losses. They eventually entered the city and captured it.

The Macedonians crossed the Tigris with great difficulty and prepared defensive measures against the hooked chariots. They were initially frightened and heavily pressed by Persian cavalry, but they disrupted most of the chariots, rallied after reverses, and defeated the Persians. About five hundred Macedonians were killed, though many were wounded.

CHAP. VU.

The Macedonians were entertained at Babylon and received payments from Alexander. At Persepolis they plundered the city, killed its men, fought among themselves over the spoils, enslaved women, and joined Alexander in burning the palace.

CHAP. VIII.

The Macedonians wanted to end the war with the death of Darius and return home. Alexander persuaded them to continue the remaining expeditions, and later separated those who complained about Parmenio's death or criticized him into a unit called the Unruly Company.

CHAP. IX.

The Macedonians followed Alexander through the snowy Paropamisadae, although some stragglers were left behind and others were blinded or killed by the reflected brightness of the snow. They fought the Massaga mercenaries and took part in the assault on Aornos.

CHAP. X.

The Macedonians endured the attacks of the Indian elephants and killed the soldiers guarding them with their sarissas. They suffered the loss of 280 horsemen and more than 700 foot soldiers, while Alexander arranged for the dead to be decently buried and rewarded survivors who had shown valor.

The Macedonians rejoiced when the fleet returned from its sea voyage, filling the theatre with acclamations. Later they refused to cross the Ganges, opposed Alexander in assembly, and mutinied when he appointed Persians to chief commands; they eventually sought to regain his favor with petitions and tears.

CHAP. XI.

Alexander believed that the Persians appointed as body squires could be trusted as safely as the Macedonians. Some Macedonians had sons by captives; Alexander found ten thousand such sons and appointed instructors to educate them.

They rejected Alexander's costly projects as impracticable and, after Python defeated the revolting Greeks and granted them safe conduct, suddenly attacked and killed them.

The Macedonians were defeated by Paulus Æmilius and the Romans. The Romans nevertheless proclaimed them free, reduced their tribute to half of what they had paid their kings, and treated them with clemency.

BOOK XXXVIL

After the Senate declared war against Perseus, the Macedonians in Rome were ordered to leave the city immediately and Italy within thirty days.

SUP. III.

The Macedonians in Scythia were surprised and killed by the rebelling barbarians. Alexander’s Macedonians captured the cities, entered Cyropolis, fought its defenders, and crossed the river to attack the Scythians.

SUP, V,

The Macedonians stormed forts and accepted the surrender of some opponents. Three hundred experienced rock climbers attempted the ascent, using crampons and cords; about thirty died, while the rest reached the summit and frightened the defenders into surrender.

SUP. VIIL

The Macedonians observed a day sacred to Bacchus, on which Alexander had customarily sacrificed each year.

SUR IX.

Many Macedonians condemn Anaxarchus's proposal but remain silent. Callisthenes argues that Alexander's Macedonian subjects should not be compelled to give him divine honors, and some Macedonians later approve Callisthenes's opposition.

SUP. XII.

The Macedonians helped repopulate Alexandria with men no longer fit for war and formed the main force in Alexander's advance. They captured the outward and inner walls of the first city Alexander attacked, pursued fleeing inhabitants, and killed the prisoners.

SUP. XIII.

The Macedonians were expected by Alexander to emulate the deeds of Bacchus and willingly endure further toils. They especially enjoyed seeing the ivy at Nysa because they had not seen any for a long time.

SUP. XIV.

The Macedonians fought the defenders of Massaga, initially drawing them away from the city before turning upon them. They endured several difficult assaults, including the collapse of a bridge from a wooden tower, and ultimately captured the city.

Macedonians in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Macedonians appear as a military and political group active across Greece. They believed Ptolemy was Philip’s son, turned hostile to Perdiccas after his failed Egyptian expedition, and considered sibling marriage unusual. After Chaeronea they controlled much of Greece, occupying Athens through garrisons at Munychia, the Piraeus, and the long walls; they also held Corinth, Salamis, Sunium, and the Museum until Olympiodorus expelled them from Athens. Their power was feared, though Aratus eventually allied with them, and Elis both supported them after Philip’s intervention and later joined opposition to Antipater and them after Alexander’s death. They supported Cassander and his sons, opposed Pyrrhus, and were defeated by him in Upper Macedonia. Under Demetrius, son of Philip, they attacked Messene but were driven out with heavy losses. Antigonus fortified towns during the Laconian war.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

The Macedonians believed that Ptolemy was really the son of Philip, although he was officially regarded as the son of Lagus. They also became hostile to Perdiccas after his failed expedition against Egypt and other grounds.

CHAPTER VII.

The Macedonians regarded marriage between siblings as unusual, unlike the Egyptians. They fought against the Athenians, supported Cassander and his sons, and later opposed Pyrrhus of Epirus.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Macedonians were defeated by Pyrrhus, whose victory brought him mastery of Upper Macedonia. Their arms were hung up to Zeus of the Macedonians at Dodona, with an inscription describing them as spoils taken from Macedonia.

CHAPTER XXV.

The Macedonians gained control over much of Greece after the battle of Chaeronea and occupied Athens with garrisons at Munychia, the Piraeus, and the long walls. They later held the Museum until Olympiodorus defeated them and captured it, after which Athens was delivered from their control.

CHAPTER VI.

The Macedonians and Antigonus were feared by the Greeks and held Corinth and the forts of the Piræus, Munychia, Salamis, and Sunium. Aratus opposed their power but eventually became their ally, and the Macedonian influence was involved in his death.

CHAPTER XXIX.

The Macedonians under Demetrius, son of Philip, secretly entered Messene before dawn but were driven out by the Messenians and the citadel's garrison. Exhausted by their march, they fled in disorder, and many perished after being pushed from the precipitous rocks.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

The Macedonians received the support of Elis after Philip’s intervention in Greece. After Alexander’s death, the people of Elis joined the Greeks in fighting against Antipater and the Macedonians.

Macedonians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Macedonians are a people associated with Pieria and Dium, where they offer Apollo a doe and, unlike others, do not customarily erect trophies after victories. They say Orpheus was slain in Pieria and removed his remains from destroyed Libethra to Dium. Hostility between Arcadians and Lacedæmonians increased their power under Philip, son of Amyntas. They gained Amphictyonic standing after the Sacred War, receiving the Phocians’ votes and later supplying six council members. They fought at Chæronea and later faced an Athenian war. Under Antigonus as regent and then Philip, son of Demetrius, they held garrisons at Corinth, Chalcis, Magnesia, and Elatea. Their supremacy was fatally damaged by decisive defeat to Flaminius and the Romans at Cynoscephalæ, forcing withdrawal from Greek towns. Under Perseus they were again defeated by Romans after his attack on Abrupolis and the Sapæans; they also held Athenians in slavery after Lamia.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

The Achæans fought against Philip and the Macedonians at Chæronea. The Athenians later fought a war with the Macedonians, and their goodwill throughout Greece did not guarantee them security in that conflict.

CHAPTER VII.

The Macedonians were ruled by Antigonus as regent for Philip, the son of Demetrius, until Philip came of age. Under Philip, they maintained garrison positions at Corinth, Chalcis, and Magnesia as bases against Greece.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Macedonians fought Flaminius and the Romans at Cynoscephalæ and were decisively defeated. Their supremacy received a fatal blow, and their garrisons had to be withdrawn from the Greek towns seized during the war.

CHAPTER X.

The Macedonians, under Perseus, were defeated in war after Perseus attacked Abrupolis and dispossessed the Sapæans, Roman allies. The Macedonians later held the Athenians in slavery after Lamia.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The hostility between the Arcadians and the Lacedæmonians increased the power of the Macedonians and Philip the son of Amyntas. The Arcadians did not fight on the side of the Greeks at Chæronea or in Thessaly.

CHAPTER XXX.

The Macedonians who inhabit the district of Pieria say that Orpheus was slain there by women. According to an account received at Larissa, after Libethra was destroyed they removed Orpheus’s remains to Dium.

CHAPTER III.

The Macedonians received the Phocians' votes in the Amphictyonic Council. The Phocians later fought against the Macedonians at Lamia and Crannon.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Macedonians were admitted to the Amphictyonic Council after the Council was changed following the Sacred War. In the narrator’s time they supplied six of the Council’s members.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

The Macedonians, under Cassander, were repulsed by the Elateans. Under Philip, the son of Demetrius, they retained Elatea until the town was reduced by the blockade of the Romans.
