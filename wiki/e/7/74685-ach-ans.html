  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Achaeans sided with the Tegeans at Mantinea, formed a Peloponnesian commonwealth, ruled Lacedaemon, heard the Roman Senate's displeasure, refused Eumenes' present, renewed their league with Seleucus, and accepted his present.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Achæans were a Greek people who settled in Ægialus, supplied troops for Agamemnon, formed the Achæan League, fought various Greek and foreign powers, became Roman allies, resisted Roman interventions, and were finally defeated by Mummius.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Achæan League was a political league formed at Ægium whose membership expanded across the Peloponnese, imposed authority on the Lacedæmonians, and increasingly came under Roman control.
  • In Description of Greece, the Achæans were an indigenous Peloponnese group driven from their homeland by the Dorians, who displaced the Ionians in Ægialus, colonized Elis, fought the Lacedæmonians and Messenians, and dedicated heroic statues at Olympia.
  • In Description of Greece, the Achæan league was led by Aratus, fought the Lacedæmonians and Cleomenes, defeated him at Sellasia with Antigonus, expanded through the accession of several cities, and alternately included and excluded the Messenians.
  • In Description of Greece, the Achaean confederacy included Corinth in its war against the Romans, while Critolaus led its revolt with the Achaeans and many Greeks from outside the Peloponnese.

Achæans in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Achæans are a Greek people, also called Argives as a general name for the populations of Argos and Lacedæmon. Driven from Lacedæmon and Argos by the Dorians, they proposed colonization to the Ionians, defeated and expelled them, and settled in Ægialus. They inhabited twelve towns, were ruled by the sons of Tisamenus and other powerful families, and provided Greece’s largest contingent for Agamemnon’s expedition against Ilium. Later they stayed out of the fighting at Thermopylæ, the naval battles, and Platæa, allied with Athenians, fought at Chaeronea, and avoided Lamia after reverses in Boeotia. Their towns were comparatively spared tyranny, war, and plague, apart from tyranny at Pellene, and they became powerful through the Achæan League. They fought the Lacedæmonians, including at Sellasia with Antigonus, and later imposed Achæan training on them. The League had an Achæan council and a general meeting at Ægium. Philopœmen became their leading authority and repeatedly their general; the Achæans reformed their infantry under him, fought at Larisus and Mantinea, and sent Lycortas against Messenia. Their relationship with Romans became central. Though initially resentful and fearful of Roman domination, they joined Flaminius, became Roman allies, and fought Philip, the Ætolians, and Antiochus. They nevertheless resisted Roman interventions over Lacedæmonian affairs, sentenced Areus and Alcibiades to death, and were later made entirely subject to Romans through Callicrates. More than 1,000 Achæans were sent to Rome and imprisoned in Etruria. Disputes over Oropians, private embassies, and Lacedæmonians escalated. Deceived by Diæus, they raised an army, attacked Lacedæmonians at Corinth, rejected Roman decisions, and prepared war under Critolaus and Diæus. They were defeated near Scarphea and finally by Mummius, after which survivors fled from Corinth. Other episodes include honoring Œbotas before Olympia, the poverty and dispersal of Patræ after war with the Galati, the killing of suppliants at Helice before its destruction, and the capture of Phana by fouling its water supply.

Description of Greece, Volume II / BOOK VII.--ACHAIA. / CHAPTER I.

The Achæans were descended through Achæus and became a general name for the populations of Argos and Lacedæmon, though the Argives were also called Danai. Expelled by the Dorians, they under Tisamenus fought the Ionians, defeated them, and settled in Ægialus.

CHAPTER VI.

The Achæans settled in twelve towns and were ruled by kings and powerful families, including the sons of Tisamenus and Damasias. They supplied the largest contingent from Greece in Agamemnon’s expedition against Ilium, but later remained absent from Thermopylæ, the sea-fights near Eubœa and Salamis, and Platæa. They allied with Patræ and the Athenians against the Lacedæmonians, fought at Chæronea against Philip and the Macedonians, and did not fight at Lamia because they had not recovered from their reverse in Bœotia.

CHAPTER VII.

The Achæans were especially powerful when the Greeks were not united, and their towns had largely escaped tyrannies, wars, and plague. They formed the Achæan League, fought the Lacedæmonians, and with Antigonus defeated them at Sellasia after Cleomenes violated his agreements.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Achæans initially resented the Romans’ harsh treatment of old Greek cities and feared replacing Macedonian rule with Roman domination. They eventually joined Flaminius in the siege of Corinth, became allies of the Romans, fought with them against Philip, the Ætolians, and Antiochus, and imposed severe measures on the Lacedæmonians.

CHAPTER IX.

The Achæans refused to hear Roman advice about the Lacedæmonians without a decree from the Roman Senate. They sentenced Areus and Alcibiades to death, resisted Roman decisions, and ultimately submitted to measures restoring exiles and changing the jurisdiction of capital cases.

CHAPTER X.

The Achæans were made entirely subject to the Romans through the actions of Callicrates. More than 1,000 Achæans accused of being friendly to Perseus were sent to Rome, imprisoned in Etruria, and only about 300 or fewer were later released.

CHAPTER XI.

The Achæans initially refused the Oropians’ request for help out of friendship and respect for the Athenians. After Menalcidas and Callicrates supported assistance, an Achaean army came too late to help, and it rejected their proposal to invade Attica.

CHAPTER XII.

The Achæans received no help from their side in the Oropian affair, but Menalcidas and Diæus acted in ways that angered them. They prosecuted Menalcidas, were vexed by Diæus's securing his acquittal, disputed the Lacedæmonians' right to consult Rome privately, and were later deceived by Diæus about the Roman Senate's instructions.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Achæans prepared an army to war against the Lacedæmonians, defeated them in battle, and established friendly relations and garrisons in towns around Sparta. They fined Damocritus 50 talents for failing to pursue his victory.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Achæans rejected the Roman decision, attacked suspected Lacedæmonians at Corinth, imprisoned those they arrested, and dismissed strangers detained on suspicion. Under Critolaus they prepared to fight Sparta and Rome, a course the text says led to their ruin.

CHAPTER XV.

The Achæans rejected Metellus’ proclamation, fought with Critolaus, and were defeated near Scarphea. Under Diæus they raised a new army, but their forces withdrew from Megara and their prospects for peace were frustrated by Diæus’ folly.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Achæans initially attacked the Roman advanced post and captured about 500 shields, but they were then defeated by Mummius. Their cavalry fled immediately, their infantry resisted until attacked from the flank, and many survivors fled to Corinth before escaping during the night.

CHAPTER XVII.

The Achæans fought a war against the Lacedæmonians. They learned through the oracle at Delphi that Œbotas’s imprecation was associated with their failure to win at Olympia, then honored him and adopted the practice of sacrificing to him before competing and crowning his statue if victorious.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Achæans drove the Ionians out and later settled in the region of Patræ. The people of Patræ were the only Achæans who joined the Ætolians against the Galati; after suffering reverses and poverty, most left Patræ, while Augustus incorporated the Achæans from Rhypæ into Patræ after destroying Rhypæ.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Achæans held their general meeting at Ægium. At Helice, they drove suppliants from Poseidon of Helice's temple and killed them, after which an earthquake and sea inundation destroyed the city.

CHAPTER XXVI.

The Achæans are associated with the boundary near Pellene, whose people are described as the last of the Achæans near Sicyon and Argolis. A man from Ægira was said to have died fighting most bravely against the Achæans.

CHAPTER XLIX.

The Achæans fought with the Arcadians and Antigonus against Cleomenes and the Lacedæmonians at Sellasia. They later chose Philopœmen as commander of their cavalry, and their forces fought at the river Larisus against Elis and an allied Ætolian force.

CHAPTER L.

The Achæans left everything to Philopœmen and made him their leading figure. Their infantry adopted new armor, Argolic shields, and long spears at his persuasion, but their light-armed troops were beaten by the light-armed Lacedæmonians at Mantinea.

CHAPTER LI.

The Achæans chose Philopœmen as their general for a second time and later for an eighth time. They sent Lycortas with an army to ravage Messenia during the dispute with the Messenians.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Achæans besieged Phana and, after consulting Delphi, captured it by fouling the town’s only water supply. They later offered a statue of Athene after reducing Phana.

Achæan League in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Achæan League was a Greek political league, formed by common consent at Ægium. The Sicyonians were its first non-Achæan members; other Peloponnesians joined, and increasing power drew Greeks from outside the Isthmus. Its membership came to include Corinth, Argos, the Lacedæmonians, Heraclea under Mount Œta, Orchomenus, Mantineans, Megalopolis, and other Greek cities. Some cities entered amid conflict: Mantineans joined after breaking with the Lacedæmonians, fought with an Achæan force under Aratus, and later helped weaken Lacedæmonian power; Megalopolis joined and honored Lydiades. Philopœmen compelled the Lacedæmonians to join, and the League treated them severely, including requiring their youths to train in the Achæan manner. It exercised jurisdiction over them except in capital cases and resisted member cities' attempts to send private embassies to Rome without common consent. Rome increasingly shaped its affairs: a Roman Senator addressed a general meeting; capital cases were assigned to foreign, meaning Roman, courts; Gallus could release towns; and the Senate ruled that several incorporated cities lacked ancestral connection with the Achæans and should not be compelled to remain. Metellus ordered those cities, including the Lacedæmonians, to leave. Diophanes was chosen general, and the League was described as the organization into which he first ranged all the Peloponnese; its member cities later received Roman permission for Polybius to establish constitutions and frame laws.

The Achæan League received Corinth after the city was freed from the Macedonian garrison. It later included the Lacedæmonians, dealt severely with them, and forced their youths to train in the Achæan manner.

CHAPTER IX.

The Romans did not remove the Lacedæmonians from the Achæan League. They ordered that foreign, understood as Roman, courts try capital cases, while other cases could be tried by the Lacedæmonians themselves or submitted to the Achæan League.

CHAPTER X.

A Roman Senator entered the Achæan League and addressed one of its general meetings. When the Senator demanded that alleged supporters of Perseus be condemned, Xeno challenged the accusation and offered to stand trial before either the Achæan League or the Romans.

CHAPTER XI.

Gallus was authorized to release towns from the Achæan League. The Ætolians who inhabited Pleuron were allowed to send a private embassy to Rome to seek release and received Roman consent.

CHAPTER XII.

The Achæan League was involved in the prosecution of Menalcidas and in the conflict with the Lacedæmonians. The Achæans argued that its cities could not send a private embassy to Rome without common consent.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Roman Senate held that the Lacedæmonians, Corinth, Argos, Heraclea under Mount Œta, and the Arcadians of Orchomenus should not be forced into the Achæan League because they lacked ancestral connection with the Achæans.

CHAPTER XV.

Metellus ordered the Lacedæmonians and other cities named by the Romans to leave the Achæan League. Critolaus and the Achæans had also blockaded Heraclea because it would not join the League.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Mantineans joined the Achaean League after becoming at variance with the Lacedæmonians. They defeated Agis in self-defence with an Achaean force under Aratus and later joined the Achæans against Cleomenes, helping to break down the power of the Lacedæmonians.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Megalopolis joined the Achæan League, after which Lydiades was held in high honor by the Achæans. His fame was considered equal to that of Aratus.

CHAPTER XXX.

The Achæan League is described as the organization into which Diophanes the son of Diæus first ranged all the Peloponnese. Greek cities that joined the Achæan League obtained permission from the Romans for Polybius to establish their constitutions and frame their laws.

CHAPTER LI.

The Achæan League received the Lacedæmonians after Philopœmen compelled them to join it. Philopœmen was chosen general of the Achæans for a second time and later for an eighth time.
