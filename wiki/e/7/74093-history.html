history in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

History records the causes, progress, and endings of events while making past actions useful to later readers. It teaches wisdom, prudence, justice, eloquence, and moral judgment: young and old learn from examples, private men are prepared for high places, and soldiers are encouraged to defend their country. It preserves the virtues and fame of worthy people, exposes cowardice, impiety, and wickedness to posterity, and restrains wrongdoing through fear of lasting judgment. Presented as an assertrix of truth and a metropolis of philosophy, it joins pleasant with profitable writing. It also preserves praise of gods, demi-gods, and worthy people, while cautioning readers not to judge ancient accounts solely by recent standards.

3 sections; each heading opens the text

front matter

History is presented as useful not merely for recounting past events, but for improving judgment by examining causes, developments, consequences, political changes, virtue, vice, and divine providence. The unit says that readers should use historical examples to avoid the failures of others and imitate honorable conduct.

THE PREFACE.

History is presented as a source of practical instruction, eloquence, justice, and moral improvement. It preserves the fame of virtuous people, brands the wicked with lasting disgrace, and carries their deeds through time more effectively than physical monuments.

DIODORUS SICULUS- / BOOK IV.

History preserves the praises of gods, demi-gods, and other worthy people for successive generations. Diodorus says that history suffers when ancient accounts are judged by standards suited only to recent events, and he urges readers not to examine old stories too critically.
