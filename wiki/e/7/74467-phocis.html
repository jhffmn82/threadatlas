Phocis in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Phocis is a region repeatedly attacked and plundered by the Boeotians, leaving its people weakened. Defeated at the end of the Sacred War, it faced razing or conversion of its cities into villages and owed Delphi sixty talents annually until restitution was complete.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. Vlll.

Phocis was repeatedly attacked and plundered by the Boeotians, who destroyed parts of the countryside and took several small towns. The conflict left the Phocians weakened after a series of defeats.

CHAP. X.

Phocis was defeated at the end of the Sacred War and subjected to extensive restrictions. Its cities were to be razed or converted into villages, while its inhabitants retained their lands but had to pay the oracle until the recorded debt for the sacrilege was repaid.

Phocis in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Phocis is a region opposite the Peloponnese, near Bœotia and the sea, with ports at Cirrha near Delphi and Anticyra. Its name is linked first to Corinthian Phocus near Tithorea and Delphi, and later to Phocus, son of Æacus, after the crossing of the Æginetans and Phocus. It includes Panopeus, Daulis, Elatea, Abæ, and Stiris, has a Phocian Council, and is approached from Chæronea by roads through Panopeus and Daulis or by rugged Stiris. Its people won against the Thessalians through stratagems and resistance, while it also endured disasters: Oedipus’ pollution at the Cross-roads, towns burned by the Mede, the Phocian War’s effect on Abæ’s temple, and the battle of Delphi, followed by the Galati’s catastrophic retreat and deaths.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK X.--PHOCIS. / CHAPTER I.

Phocis is described as lying opposite the Peloponnese, near Bœotia, and on the sea, with ports at Cirrha and Anticyra. Its inhabitants fought the Thessalians and achieved notable victories through stratagems and determined resistance.

CHAPTER IV.

Phocis contains Panopeus and Daulis, and Panopeus sends members to the Phocian Council. The Bœotians have their easiest approach to Phocis through the point near Panopeus.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Phocis was the scene of the battle and the barbarians’ disastrous retreat. Nearly 6,000 Galati were slain there in battle, more than 10,000 died during the wintry night and panic, and as many more died from starvation.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Phocis contains Elatea, which is described as one of its towns. Elatea shared in the general disasters of the Phocians and was one of the towns in Phocis burned by the Mede.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Phocis could be reached from Chæronea either by the road through Panopeus and Daulis or by the rugged mountain road to Stiris. Abæ and Stiris are described in connection with the region, and the Phocian War affected the temple at Abæ.
