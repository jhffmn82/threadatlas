  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian: Eumenes is presented as a Cardian commander, skilful and prudent general, and constant, faithful friend whose career centers on defending the kings and opposing Antigonus’s bid for the kingdom. He begins by receiving Cappadocia from Perdiccas, raising cavalry there, and marching with a strengthened army. He defeats Neoptolemus and Craterus’s army, though he is wounded and later condemned to death by Macedonians despite the victory. After further conflict, including the capture of Perdiccas and his army, Antigonus defeats him in Cappadocia; Eumenes retreats to the impregnable Nora with loyal followers, endures confinement or siege there, and is eventually released under an agreement. Restored to action, he is urged by Polyperchon to remain loyal to the kings and is made their general throughout Asia, with authority, money, and command of the Argyraspides. He gathers troops, escapes attempts on his life, seeks to recover Phoenicia, then marches through difficult territory to Persia, Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis. He secures royal funds, pays his soldiers, organizes consultations, strengthens his authority through political measures including a forged letter, and resists Antigonus through defenses, surprise attacks, false reports, and a fire-signal stratagem. Illness forces him to travel in a litter, but he continues to command allied forces supported by cavalry and elephants. His struggle ends disastrously: after fighting Antigonus and losing baggage, he is seized by Macedonians, delivered to Antigonus, and put to death. The records also credit him with gaining Abdera by bribery through Python, rewarding Gauls and soldiers generously, receiving regions on this side of Taurus from Roman commissioners, and later falling under Roman suspicion because of alleged alliance with Perseus. Across the account, his military ability, loyalty to the kings, persistence after defeat, and ultimate betrayal are the defining features.

Eumenes in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Eumenes is presented as a Cardian commander, skilful and prudent general, and constant, faithful friend whose career centers on defending the kings and opposing Antigonus’s bid for the kingdom. He begins by receiving Cappadocia from Perdiccas, raising cavalry there, and marching with a strengthened army. He defeats Neoptolemus and Craterus’s army, though he is wounded and later condemned to death by Macedonians despite the victory. After further conflict, including the capture of Perdiccas and his army, Antigonus defeats him in Cappadocia; Eumenes retreats to the impregnable Nora with loyal followers, endures confinement or siege there, and is eventually released under an agreement. Restored to action, he is urged by Polyperchon to remain loyal to the kings and is made their general throughout Asia, with authority, money, and command of the Argyraspides. He gathers troops, escapes attempts on his life, seeks to recover Phoenicia, then marches through difficult territory to Persia, Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis. He secures royal funds, pays his soldiers, organizes consultations, strengthens his authority through political measures including a forged letter, and resists Antigonus through defenses, surprise attacks, false reports, and a fire-signal stratagem. Illness forces him to travel in a litter, but he continues to command allied forces supported by cavalry and elephants. His struggle ends disastrously: after fighting Antigonus and losing baggage, he is seized by Macedonians, delivered to Antigonus, and put to death. The records also credit him with gaining Abdera by bribery through Python, rewarding Gauls and soldiers generously, receiving regions on this side of Taurus from Roman commissioners, and later falling under Roman suspicion because of alleged alliance with Perseus. Across the account, his military ability, loyalty to the kings, persistence after defeat, and ultimate betrayal are the defining features.

Eumenes of Cardia received Cappadocia from Perdiccas to govern as his allotted share.

CHAP. IIL

Eumenes received a considerable force from Perdiccas and marched to the Hellespont. He completed his army with cavalry raised from his own province, since his troops had previously lacked sufficient horse.

Eumenes defeated Neoptolemus, incorporated the surviving Macedonians into his army, and collected many horsemen because he lacked infantry able to confront the Macedonian phalanx. In the battle against Craterus's army, he defeated both wings, killed Neoptolemus, retained the field and the bodies of both commanders, and sent terms to the phalanx. He was wounded and could not effectively pursue the Macedonians when they broke their oath and departed, but news of his victory later led the Macedonians to condemn him and his adherents to death.

Eumenes captured Perdiccas and his army after a night attack and spared most of the ordinary soldiers, incorporating them into his own forces. Antigonus later defeated him in Cappadocia, after which Eumenes withdrew to Nora with about six hundred loyal followers and continued negotiating and preparing for possible changes in fortune.

CHAP. IV.

Eumenes had earlier been defeated by Antigonus and confined for a year in Nora. Antigonus later made an agreement with him and released him, after which Eumenes gathered former friends and soldiers and began rebuilding a force in support of the kings.

Polyperchon urged Eumenes to remain loyal to the kings and not ally with Antigonus. He invited Eumenes either to come to Macedonia and share in protecting the kings or to remain in Asia while receiving men and money to make war against Antigonus.

CHAP. V.

Eumenes received authority from the kings to command throughout Asia and was given five hundred talents, though he later refused the money because he claimed no principality and considered his command compelled by the kings. He left Cappadocia with about five hundred horse and more than two thousand foot, joined the Argyraspides in Cilicia, defeated Antigonus's attempt to have him killed, and marched toward Phoenicia to assemble a fleet.

Eumenes withdrew from Phoenicia when he could not accomplish his intended recovery of the region for the kings. He marched through Coelosyria toward the higher provinces, suffered attacks near the Tigris and Euphrates, escaped Seleucus, and reached Persia with fifteen thousand foot soldiers and thirteen hundred horse.

Eumenes wintered in Babylonian territory with the Argyraspides, rejected attempts to separate him from them, and crossed the Tigris toward Susa. In Susiana he divided his army because of scarce provisions, summoned the eastern governors, obtained money from the royal treasury, paid the Macedonians six months in advance, and organized daily consultations in the king's pavilion.

CHAP. n.

Eumenes defended the Tigris with forts and requested additional Persian archers from Peucestes. After learning of Antigonus's attempt to cross the Coprates, he attacked the troops that had crossed and routed them. He later marched from Pasitigris to Persepolis, where he used a forged letter, proceedings against Siburtius, and requests for contributions to increase his authority over the army.

Eumenes moved forward to meet Antigonus after learning that Antigonus was marching into Persia. He fell ill after feasting with the army, recovered sufficiently to continue in a litter, and later outwitted Antigonus by sending false deserters to report that he would attack that night. He thanked the Macedonians for rejecting Antigonus's offers and warned them that Antigonus intended to destroy their commanders once he gained control of the army.

Eumenes commanded the opposing army and reinforced his distressed right wing with swift cavalry from Eudamus. Supported by elephants, he drove Python's forces toward the mountains, but later had to recall troops after Antigonus attacked Eudamus's regiments. He wished to return to bury his dead but was compelled by his army to withdraw to its carriages.

Eumenes used a fire-signal stratagem to make Antigonus believe that his dispersed army had assembled, gaining time to gather his forces and fortify his camp. He fought Antigonus, saw his horse defeated and his baggage captured, and was later seized by the Macedonians and delivered to Antigonus, who put him to death.

Eumenes used bribery to gain Abdera through Python, who opened the gates but later repented when he saw the destruction of his country. Unlike Perseus, Eumenes paid his soldiers what they were owed, gave them additional gifts, and won their loyalty through generosity.

BOOK XXXVIL

Eumenes received the regions on this side of the Taurus and was given favorable treatment by the Roman Senate. Later, intercepted letters and accusations caused the Romans to suspect that he had allied with Perseus, so Caius was sent to observe his affairs and movements.

BOOK XVII.

He defeated Neoptolemus and Craterus, opposed Antigonus, and took refuge at Nora after being routed. He later continued the struggle in Asia but was eventually betrayed and killed.
