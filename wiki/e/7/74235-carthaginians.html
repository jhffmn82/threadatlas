  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Carthaginians are described as the people and principal inhabitants of Carthage, whose history centers on wars, alliances, territorial expansion, and eventual conflict with Rome.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Carthaginians are described as an African power conducting overseas conquests and extensive campaigns, especially in Sicily, through armies, fleets, confederates, mercenaries, and colonies.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Carthaginians are described as builders of Caralis and Sulci, a great naval power, and conquerors of nearly all Sardinians.
  • In Description of Greece, the Carthaginians are described as Tyrian and Phoenician in origin, the greatest barbarian maritime nation, and recurring captors of Greek cities in Sicily until challenged by Pyrrhus.

Carthaginians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Carthaginians are the people and principal inhabitants of Carthage, possessing Carthage, a navy, dominions, and at times control of the sea. Their record is chiefly one of repeated wars, alliances, reversals, and recoveries in Sicily, Africa, Spain, and against the Romans. Under Mago they receive Selinus; they oppose Dionysius, win at Cronium, retain Selinus and part of Agrigentum in peace, yet endure plague, African resistance, Sardinian revolt, and internal disorder before subduing Africans and recovering Sardinia. They make alliances with Sicilian princes, send forces under Hanno, besiege Entella and Syracuse, but lose influence and suffer a severe defeat by Timoleon, after which they agree that Greek cities shall be free. Against Agathocles, they support Syracuse's exiles, occupy Sicilian positions, raise large fleets and armies, defeat him at Himera, and nearly control Sicily. Yet his move into Africa brings alarm, battlefield defeats, a siege of Tunis, civil danger from Bomilcar, and losses at sea and on land. They use fortified camps, provisions, Numidians, and redistribution of troops to recover, and after four years make peace with the Greeks. The records also describe gifts to Tyre and the sacrifice of hundreds of children during crisis, as well as child sacrifice as a custom. They later secure their country from invasion, ally with the Romans against Pyrrhus, resist him at Lilybaeum, and join Hiero against the Romans. Their long war with the Romans over Sicily includes struggles for Messana, Agrigentum, Panormus, and Lilybaeum; a victory in Africa under Xanthippus restores them from near ruin, while later defeats continue. They expand in Spain under Amilcar, Asdrubal, and Hannibal, whose leadership restrains defections among his own nation. After defeat by P. Scipio and further conflict, they surrender their country, hostages, weapons, and engines to the Romans, but renew war when ordered to abandon Carthage and rebuild far from the sea.

CHAP. n.

The Carthaginians opposed Dionysius after he protected cities subject to them and refused to restore them. They defeated him at Cronium, pursued his army, and later made peace while retaining Selinus and part of Agrigentum and receiving a payment of a thousand talents.

CHAR III.

The Carthaginians entered Italy with an army, restored the Hipponiats to their city, and received exiles from many places. After a plague, African resistance, Sardinian revolt, and disorder within Carthage, they subdued the Africans and recovered Sardinia.

CHAP. VIIL

The Carthaginians were weakened by plague and the defection of many Africans when Dionysius attacked their territory in Sicily. They later manned two hundred ships, unexpectedly entered the harbor at Eryx, and carried away most of Dionysius’s ships.

CHAP. XI.

The Carthaginians made alliances with Sicilian princes, especially Hicetas, and sent a large force under Hanno into Sicily. Their forces besieged Entella and later occupied Syracuse's great harbour, but they withdrew their fleet and army when Timoleon received reinforcements.

CHAP. XII.

The Carthaginians lost influence as Timoleon's reputation grew and many communities under their rule sought alliance with him. Timoleon sent a thousand armed men into their territories, who gathered rich plunder that he sold to pay his soldiers.

CHAP, XIII.

The Carthaginians transported a large army and fleet into Sicily, but Timoleon defeated them. They lost at least ten thousand soldiers, more than fifteen thousand prisoners, many arms, two hundred chariots, and their baggage; afterward they sought peace and agreed that the Greek cities should be free.

Carthaginians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Carthaginians are a people and African power, centered in Africa and operating through armies, fleets, confederates, mercenaries, colonies, mines, and overseas possessions. They seek wealthy and strategic islands, coveting Sardinia, settling Pityusa, conquering Sardinia, opposing an Etrurian colonization plan, and controlling Spain. By opening and exploiting Iberian silver mines, they gain wealth sufficient to hire many soldiers and wage extensive wars. Their principal unfolding conflict is in Sicily. An early invasion under Amilcar ends in rout at Himera, followed by peace payments and promises. Later, desiring to protect Egesta, they assemble forces from Africa and Campania, invade again, take Selinus, defeat and destroy Himera, and return with prisoners and plunder. They build Therma, recruit widely, and undertake larger campaigns: besieging, starving, plundering, and occupying Agrigentum; preparing weapons and engines; attacking Gela; and devastating territory around Gela and Camarina. Plague repeatedly weakens their armies and helps bring peace or military reverses. They defend Motya but lose it, then raise another large army and fleet from Africa, Spain, confederates, and mercenaries, capture Motya and Messana, defeat a Sicilian fleet, and besiege Syracuse. Disease and Syracusan attacks destroy their camp, forts, fleet, and transports. Routed by sea and land, they lose their Sicilian possessions and face revolt among African confederates. Confined to Carthage, they seek religious appeasement through sacrifices to Proserpina and Ceres, then defeat the rebels with officers, Sardinian supplies, and rebel divisions. Under Mago they attempt recovery, suffer defeat near Abacene, renew war with a large landing force, and finally make peace after shortages and attacks on provisions.

The Carthaginians repeatedly crossed into Sicily and captured Greek cities, eventually besieging Syracuse. Pyrrhus forced them to lift the siege and then, with excessive confidence, challenged their naval power.

CHAPTER XVII.

When they became a great naval power, the Carthaginians subdued nearly all the Sardinians. They built the towns Caralis and Sulci and were allied with the Libyans or Iberes. Their conquest did not reduce the Ilians and Corsi to slavery because those peoples were protected by the mountains.
