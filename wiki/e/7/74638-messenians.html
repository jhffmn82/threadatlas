Messenians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Messenians were a people repeatedly oppressed and expelled by the Lacedaemonians. Survivors settled at Naupactus, Cephalenia, and Sicily, where they founded Messana; others were invited by Epaminondas to repopulate Messene. Included in the general Greek league after Mantinea, they later received the Mamertines as allies, who massacred them and seized their city.

Messenians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Messenians, exiles hostile to the Spartans, joined the Helots in attacking Sparta after an earthquake and continued war from a fort in Messenia. Dismissed from Ithome, they inhabited Naupactus; expelled by the Lacedaemonians, some went to Sicily, while most who joined Cyrene’s exiles were killed.

CHAP. XIII.

The Messenians joined the Helots in attacking Sparta after the earthquake. They continued the war from a fort in Messenia and later joined the revolt of the Helots against the Lacedaemonians.

CHAP. VI.

The Messenians were expelled by the Lacedaemonians because of the ancient hatred between them. Some went to Sicily and served under Dionysius, while about three thousand went to Cyrene and joined its exiles, where nearly all were killed in battle.

Messenians in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Messenians are a people of Messenia whose history is marked by repeated war, defeat, exile, return, and continuing resistance to the Lacedæmonians. They revolted during Anaxander’s reign and killed Teleclus in the temple of Artemis Limnas. After Amphea fell, they gathered at Stenyclerus under Euphaes, fought with Arcadian help, suffered losses and pestilence, and withdrew to Ithome. Although they defeated the Lacedæmonians with their allies, Aristodemus’s death preceded the evacuation of Ithome. They were compelled to pay Sparta half their land’s produce and observe mourning requirements. They later rose again under Aristomenes. Early successes ended in disaster at the Great Trench when Aristocrates withdrew the Arcadians. The Messenians retreated to Eira, raided Laconia and Messenia, and resisted siege for eleven years; Aristomenes hid sacred records on Mount Ithome in hope of recovery. Eira’s final three-day defense involved even the women, amid hunger, thirst, cold, rain, and exhaustion. Its capture led to some Messenians becoming Helots, while others escaped to Arcadia and later founded a settlement in Sicily. After a second defeat they evacuated the Peloponnese; many remaining there became slaves. In another revolt they held Ithome, received Naupactus from the Athenians, conquered Œniadæ, broke through its Acarnanian blockade, and used Naupactus against the Peloponnese, including by assisting the Athenians at Sphacteria. Most exiles later settled in Euesperitæ. After nearly 300 years, or 287 years after Eira’s capture, they returned, rebuilt their country, retained their customs and Doric dialect, and again resisted Sparta. They received Macedonian aid under Philip, captured and controlled Elis after expelling its Lacedæmonian party, and eventually joined the Achæan League. They sheltered Arcadians after Megalopolis, joined Aratus at Sellasia, captured Philopœmen, punished those responsible for his death, and rejoined the League. Their Victory monument at Olympia commemorated their share at Sphacteria but omitted the enemy’s name from fear. Olympic success declined in exile and resumed after restoration, with Damiscus’s victory.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

The Messenians and Lacedæmonians gave different accounts of the war between them. After revolting and being defeated, the Messenians evacuated the Peloponnese under terms of war, while many who remained became slaves on Lacedæmonian soil.

CHAPTER VI.

The Messenians killed Teleclus, king of the other Spartan royal family, in the temple of Artemis Limnas. They later evacuated the Peloponnese after being conquered for a second time by the Spartans.

CHAPTER VI.

The Messenians gather at Stenyclerus after the capture of Amphea and prepare for war under Euphaes. They fight the Lacedæmonians, retreat to Ithome after suffering losses and pestilence, and continue the war with support from Arcadian allies.

CHAPTER XI.

The Messenians and their allies defeated the Lacedæmonians after using light-armed troops to harass their flanks and disrupt their formation. After Aristodemus’s death they evacuated Ithome, were subjected to Spartan tribute and mourning requirements, and later rose in insurrection under Aristomenes with support from several allies.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Messenians fight the Lacedæmonians under Aristomenes and initially achieve victories, but suffer a major defeat at the Great Trench after Aristocrates withdraws the Arcadians. They retreat to Eira, raid Laconia and Messenia, and endure an eleven-year blockade. Their sacred records are hidden by Aristomenes on Mount Ithome so that they may retain hope of recovering their country.

CHAPTER XXI.

The Messenians defended Eira for three days and nights despite rain, cold, hunger, thirst, and exhaustion, with their women also taking up arms. After Eira fell, some were made Helots, while others escaped to Arcadia and later founded a settlement in Sicily.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Messenians revolted from the Lacedæmonians, held out at Ithome, received Naupactus from the Athenians, and defeated the Acarnanians at Œniadæ before escaping the siege. After nearly 300 years away from the Peloponnese, they returned, rebuilt their country, preserved their customs and Doric dialect, resisted Sparta, received aid from Philip and the Macedonians, and captured Elis.

CHAPTER XXIX.

The Messenians defeated Demetrius, son of Philip, after first mistaking his troops for Lacedæmonians. They eventually joined the Achæan League, sheltered the Arcadians who escaped from Megalopolis, helped take Sparta, resisted Nabis, and later captured Philopœmen before punishing those responsible for his death and rejoining the League.

CHAPTER XXVI.

They received Naupactus from the Athenians and erected a Victory at Olympia on a pillar. They said the monument commemorated their participation with the Athenians in the action at Sphacteria, though they omitted the enemy’s name because they feared the Lacedæmonians.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

When the Messenians were exiles from the Peloponnese, their success at Olympia also failed, apart from Leontiscus and Symmachus, whom the Sicilians disputed as Messenians. After their return to the Peloponnese, their Olympic success returned, beginning with Damiscus's victory in the boys' course.

Messenians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Messenians, bordering Lacedæmon, were restored by Epaminondas, the Thebans, and Argives, and helped the Thebans during Thebes’s restoration. Later, divided between wealthy and popular parties, they captured the wounded Philopœmen; Dinocrates and the wealthy urged execution while the popular party sought to save him.

CHAPTER LI.

The Messenians captured Philopœmen after he was wounded and had fallen from his horse. In their meeting, Dinocrates and the wealthy Messenians urged his execution, while the popular party sought to save him.

CHAPTER X.

The Messenians were restored with the assistance of the Argives, who supported Epaminondas and the Thebans.
