Piraeus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Piraeus is a port at and of Athens. It is repeatedly contested in warfare: Sphodrias’s planned nighttime attack fails when Athenians detect it; Nicanor secretly seizes its walls and harbor mouth, and Alexander retains it rather than restoring it to Athens. Cassander later seizes it with ships and men, uses it as a return point, and holds it through his forces. Finally Demetrius assaults it, gains entry with help from insiders, and captures it from Cassander’s garrison.

The Piraeus was the intended target of Sphodrias's nighttime attack with ten thousand heavy-armed men. The Athenians detected the attempt, and it failed.

Nicanor secretly seized the walls of the Piraeus and the mouth of its harbor after deceiving the Athenians over Munychia. Alexander, the son of Polyperchon, later encamped there and retained the port rather than restoring it to the Athenians.

After this, Cassander having got five*and-thirty long ships and

The Piraeus was seized by Cassander after he sailed there with thirty-five long ships and four thousand men. It later served as the place to which Cassander returned after raising the siege of Salamis, and Nicanor eventually brought his victorious fleet there.

CHAP. ni.

The Piraeus was held by Cassander's forces when Demetrius arrived. Demetrius assaulted it from every side, and some of his men scaled the walls with assistance from people inside, resulting in its capture.

Piraeus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Piraeus began as a place lacking a harbor fit for Athenian needs, until Themistocles planned a spacious, beautiful harbor to strengthen power at sea. It was blockaded by Lysander, and its fortifications and long walls were demolished under Athens's surrender terms after the Peloponnesian War. Thrasybulus seized Munychia and then Piraeus, gathering opponents and exiles. Later Conon arrived with eighty ships and helped restore its walls.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

I.

Piraeus was described as lacking a harbor suitable for the Athenians' needs. Themistocles planned to turn it into a spacious and beautiful harbor that would strengthen Athenian power at sea.

CHAP. XV.

The long fortifications of the Piraeus were required to be demolished as one of the terms imposed upon Athens at its surrender.

CHAP. VI.

Thrasybulus marched against the Piraeus after defeating the forces gathered at Acarnas and seized Munychia there. His forces later gained possession of the Piraeus, where many opponents of the tyranny and Athenian exiles joined him.

CHAP. IX,

The walls and long walls of the Piraeus had been demolished by the Lacedaemonians after the Peloponnesian War. Conon arrived there with eighty ships and helped arrange their reconstruction.

Piraeus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Piraeus, once a township, became a port under Themistocles, arranged with three harbours, ship stations, his tomb, temples, paintings, and porticoes; Conon erected a temple of Aphrodite. Later Macedonian forces held it, Demetrius did not promptly return it after Lachares fled, and Olympiodorus recovered it with Munychia.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Description of Greece / BOOK I.--ATTICA.

Piraeus was a township in ancient times and became a port under Themistocles. It had three harbours, ship stations, the tomb of Themistocles, temples of Athene and Zeus, paintings, porticoes, and a temple of Aphrodite erected by Conon.

CHAPTER XXV.

The Piraeus was among the Athenian possessions taken or controlled by Macedonian forces. Demetrius son of Antigonus did not immediately return it after Lachares fled, but Olympiodorus later recovered it along with Munychia.
