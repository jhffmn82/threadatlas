Persia in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Persia is an empire ruled by Darius, with Persepolis as its royal seat, and also a country of narrow valleys, high signaling watchtowers, archers and slingers, and land ranging from hot, barren ground to fertile, well-watered highlands. It fought Cyrus, the Lacedaemonians, Evagoras, Egypt, and the Phocians' allies; despite defeats at Sipylos and elsewhere, it later imposed or mediated peace among the Greeks. Threatened by revolts in Asia and Tachos's Egyptian attack, Artaxerxes raised forces, accepted the fleeing Tachos, and used him against Egypt. Violent changes under Ochus, Arses, Bagoas, and Darius preceded preparations against the Macedonian threat. Philip planned war against the Persian monarchy but died, leaving Alexander forces to overturn it. After Issus put the empire at great risk, Alexander invaded: cities and treasures were taken, Persepolis was plundered and burned, and Darius was murdered while fleeing. Later Antigonus entered Persia, was honored as king, removed the beloved governor Peucestes, and appointed Asclepiodorus with an army.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAR XL

Persia was threatened by revolts in Asia and by Tachos's attack from Egypt. Artaxerxes responded by raising forces, accepting Tachos after his flight, and using him in the campaign against Egypt.

BOOK XVI.

Philip devoted his thoughts to destroying the Persian monarchy after defeating the Illyrians, Pæones, Thracians, Scythians, and neighboring peoples. He died while preparing the expedition, but left Alexander enough forces to overturn the Persian empire.

CHAP, XV.

Persia was the target of Philip's war and intended expedition. Philip believed the Delphic oracle and Neoptolemus's verses foretold his victory over the king of Persia.

BOOK XVII.

Persia experienced violent changes of rulers under Ochus, Arses, Bagoas, and Darius. Under Darius, it began preparing a large army and fleet against the Macedonian threat.

CHAP. III.

The Persian Empire was placed at great risk by the battle at Issus. Alexander believed that one victory could ruin the whole empire, and Darius's defeat greatly advanced that danger.

CHAP. VU.

Persia was invaded by Alexander after the defeat of Darius. Its cities and royal treasures were taken, Persepolis was plundered and burned, and Darius was later murdered while fleeing through the Persian territories.

CHAP. n.

Persia was described as containing long, narrow valleys and high watchtowers that allowed commands to be relayed over great distances by men with loud voices. Its inhabitants supplied Peucestes with booty and provisions, and its most warlike people were archers and slingers. The country around Persepolis was described as partly hot and barren and partly elevated, fertile, and well watered.

CHAP, m.

When Antigonus entered Persia, its people honored him as a king and he became absolute lord of Asia. He reassigned several provincial governments, removed Peucestes from Persia, and appointed Asclepiodorus governor with a considerable army.

BOOK XXXI.

The Cappadocian kings also claimed descent from one of the seven Persians who killed Magus. Anaphas is described as a member of that group and as a man who abdicated the government of Cappadocia rather than pay tribute to the Persians.

BOOK XIV.

Persia fought Cyrus, the Lacedaemonians, Evagoras, Egypt, and the Phocians' allies. Persian forces were defeated at Sipylos and in other engagements, though Persia later imposed or mediated peace among the Greeks.

Persia in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Persia is an empire that came to hold Babylon after Cyrus conquered Astyages and transferred the Medes’ empire to the Persians. It expanded power over Greek cities in Asia and threatened Greece through Xerxes’ invasion, demanding earth and water and mounting a vast land and naval expedition. After Persian defeats, it agreed to free the Greek cities in Asia. Succession trouble followed Xerxes’ death, but Artaxerxes restored order, answered revolt in Egypt, sought coastal control, opposed the Lacedaemonians, and intervened against Evagoras in Cyprus.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. III.

The Medes' empire is described through conflicting accounts. According to Ctesias, it began under Arbaces after the overthrow of Sardanapalus, passed through a succession of kings, and ended when Cyrus conquered Astyages and transferred the empire to the Persians.

LAST TEN BOOKS / BOOK XI.

Persia prepared a massive land and naval expedition against Greece. The Persian king demanded earth and water from the Greek cities and accepted support from several Greek communities.

CHAP. XIX.

Persia experienced succession troubles after the death of Xerxes, but Artaxerxes restored order and prepared a large military response to the Egyptian revolt.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK XIL

Persia threatened Greece through Xerxes’ invasion and had previously brought the Greek cities in Asia under its power. Persian forces were later defeated by the Athenians, and Persia agreed to terms freeing the Greek cities in Asia.

CHAP. VI.

The Persian Empire, represented by Artaxerxes, sought to reassert control over the governments and Greek cities on the sea-coasts after Cyrus's defeat. Persian forces under Tissaphernes and Pharnabazus opposed the Lacedaemonians.

CHAP. XL

Persia intervened in Cyprus against Evagoras. Persian authorities ordered ships to be built and equipped, directed Hecatomnus to attack Evagoras, and sent a large army toward Cyprus.

the Persian Empire in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · -0471

The Persian Empire is an eastern Persian realm of great wealth centered at Susa. Under Xerxes, it sends wealth, forces, and ships against Hellas, but suffers a catastrophic defeat at Salamis. Much of its army, fleet, and leading men are lost; Persia mourns, its power to exact tribute and rule is weakened, and its former wealth, authority, and glory give way to bloodshed and ruin.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

THE PERSIANS

Persia sends its wealth, forces, and ships against Hellas under Xerxes. Atossa and the Chorus fear that defeat will leave Persia desolate, with Susa mourning its dead and its imperial power weakened.

Enter a MESSENGER.

The Persian Empire suffers a catastrophic defeat, losing much of its army and fleet and many of its leading men. Its Asian land is left mourning, and its power is described as unable to exact tribute or sustain rule as before.

The GHOST OF DARIUS rises.

The Persian kingdom is described as fallen into bloodshed, mourning, and ruin. Its former wealth, authority, and imperial glory are contrasted with the destruction caused by Xerxes’ expedition.
