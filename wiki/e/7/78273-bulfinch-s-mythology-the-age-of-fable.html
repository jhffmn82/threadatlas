Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

work · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections
Also called: Bulfinch's Greek and Roman Mythology

Abstract written by a model

This document is a wide-ranging survey of mythology, religion, literature, legendary beings, and Greek sculpture. It begins by introducing Greek, Roman, and Scandinavian traditions, the classical cosmos, Olympus, the succession of Uranus, Kronos, and Jupiter, and the principal gods, lesser divinities, Roman household spirits, and Saturn’s Golden Age. Its central and longest course retells Greek and Roman myths in roughly thematic and heroic sequence. Creation emerges from Chaos; Prometheus aids humanity, Pandora releases evils, and Deucalion and Pyrrha survive Jupiter’s flood. Many tales explain natural features, plants, animals, constellations, and seasons through divine love, punishment, transformation, and death: Apollo and Daphne, Phaeton, Proserpine and Ceres, Pygmalion, Cupid and Psyche, Narcissus, Arachne, Niobe, and others. Major heroes include Perseus, OEdipus, Bellerophon, Jason, Medea, Hercules, Theseus, Daedalus, Orpheus, and Bacchus. The narratives repeatedly contrast hospitality, fidelity, humility, and piety with pride, betrayal, rejection, and impiety. The account then turns to Troy and its aftermath. Paris’s judgment and seizure of Helen lead Agamemnon, Achilles, Hector, Priam, Ulysses, and others into the Trojan War. The Iliad culminates in Patroclus’s death, Achilles’s killing of Hector, and Priam’s recovery of Hector’s body; Troy subsequently falls through the Wooden Horse. The Odyssey follows Ulysses through hazards and homecoming to Ithaca, where he destroys the suitors. The Aeneid follows Aeneas from Troy through Dido, the underworld, and war in Italy to his marriage with Lavinia, Alba Longa, and the later founding of Rome by Romulus and Remus. Later sections broaden the scope to Pythagoras, Egyptian religion and Osiris and Isis, theories of pagan myth, classical authors Homer, Virgil, and Ovid, and legendary creatures. They also survey Zoroastrianism, Hindu traditions, Buddhism, and Northern mythology centered on Odin, Thor, Loki, Baldur, Ragnarok, and the Eddas. Celtic Druids, Iona, Columba, and the Culdees provide a transition into Christian influence. The document ends with a history of Greek sculpture, from early work through Myron, Polycleitos, and Pheidias, especially the Parthenon and Pheidias’s Athene and Zeus, then later artists and works. It presents Greek myth as a lasting source for literature, religion, and art, even as later Greek sculpture shifts from ideal divinity toward emotion and technical display.

Chapter I

The Introduction explains why Greek, Roman, and Scandinavian mythology is studied and describes the peoples, languages, and imaginative traditions associated with them. It outlines the Greek conception of the earth, the River Ocean, the realms of the blessed, and Mount Olympus, the home of the gods. It then relates the succession of Uranus, Kronos, and Jupiter and describes the principal Greek and Roman deities, their powers, families, symbols, and stories. The unit also introduces various lesser deities, including the Muses, Fates, Furies, Nemesis, Pan, Satyrs, Momus, and Plutus.

ROMAN DIVINITIES

This unit describes Roman divinities and identifies several as peculiar to Roman mythology, while noting that some Grecian divinities were also received by the Romans. It explains Saturn's reign during the Golden Age, the Saturnalia held in his memory, and the roles of deities including Vesta, Janus, and others. It also distinguishes the household gods Penates and Lares and describes the protective spirits called Genius and Juno. The unit concludes with a passage by Macaulay and notes on the pronunciation of proper names.

CHAPTER II

Ovid's creation narrative describes how God and Nature separated Chaos into earth, sea, air, and heaven, and how man was made in the image of the gods. Prometheus gave man fire, while Pandora's opening of a jar released evils but left hope behind. After humanity declined from the Golden Age into violence and corruption, Jupiter destroyed the world with a flood, sparing only Deucalion and Pyrrha on Mount Parnassus. They followed an oracle's command to cast stones behind them, from which a new human race arose, while Prometheus was later imprisoned and tortured for aiding mankind.

PYTHON

The unit recounts Apollo's slaying of Python, the founding of the Pythian games and the Delphic oracle, and Apollo's unsuccessful pursuit of Daphne, who becomes a laurel tree. It then tells how Pyramus and Thisbe's forbidden love ends in their deaths beneath a white mulberry tree, whose berries become purple. Finally, it relates Cephalus's mistaken killing of Procris with her own javelin after she suspects him of loving the breeze.

Chapter III

Jupiter changes Io into a heifer to conceal his affair, but Juno has Argus guard her until Mercury kills him and Jupiter persuades Juno to restore Io. Juno changes Callisto into a bear, but Jupiter places Callisto and her son Arcas in the heavens as the Great and Little Bear. Diana changes Actaeon into a stag after he sees her bathing, and his own hunting dogs pursue and kill him. The unit also explains the origins of the Syrinx pipes and describes the constellations, including the Little Bear and the Pole star.

LATONA AND THE RUSTICS

Latona, carrying her infant twins, was refused water by the rustics of Lycia, who polluted the pond and were transformed into frogs. Phaeton, after Epaphus challenged his claim to be the Sun's son, obtained permission to drive the Sun's chariot despite the Sun's warnings. Phaeton lost control of the horses, causing a vast conflagration that scorched Earth, dried rivers and seas, and destroyed cities and nations. Jupiter struck Phaeton with a thunderbolt, and the Heliades were transformed into poplar trees whose tears became amber.

Chapter IV

Midas receives the power to turn everything he touches into gold, suffers from the gift, and is later given ass ears by Apollo after judging a music contest in favor of Pan. His hairdresser reveals the secret by whispering it into the ground, from which reeds grow and repeat it. The unit also tells of Gordius, the Gordian Knot, and Alexander the Great, and of Jupiter and Mercury rewarding the hospitable Baucis and Philemon while destroying their inhospitable village. Baucis and Philemon become trees after serving as guardians of the transformed temple.

PROSERPINE

Proserpine is abducted by Pluto from the vale of Enna, prompting Ceres to search for her and punish the earth with famine. Jupiter arranges a compromise: because Proserpine has eaten pomegranate seeds in the underworld, she spends half the year with Ceres and half with Pluto, explaining the seasons. The story also tells how Glaucus becomes a sea-god and how Circe transforms Scylla into a monstrous terror to sailors. The unit connects these myths with Sicily, Enna, the River Alpheus, and the fountain Arethusa.

Chapter V

Pygmalion, a sculptor who despises women, creates an ivory maiden and falls in love with her; Venus brings the statue to life, and their union produces Paphos. Dryope is transformed into a lotus tree after picking flowers from the nymph Lotis, leaving her family to mourn and care for her child. Venus falls in love with Adonis, but he is killed by a wild boar, and she changes his blood into the short-lived anemone. Apollo accidentally kills Hyacinthus during a game of quoits and changes his blood into a flower marked with signs of grief.

CEYX AND HALCYONE: OR, THE HALCYON BIRDS

Ceyx, King of Thessaly, sails to Claros despite Halcyone's fears and dies when a violent storm destroys his ship in the Aegean Sea. Juno sends Iris to Somnus, who commissions Morpheus to appear to Halcyone in a dream and reveal Ceyx's death. Halcyone finds Ceyx's body at the shore, is transformed into a bird while reaching for him, and the gods transform Ceyx as well. They become halcyon birds, whose floating nest is protected by seven calm winter days at sea.

Chapter VI

Pomona is a Hamadryad devoted to cultivated gardens, fruit trees, and especially apples, while avoiding love and keeping men out of her orchard. Vertumnus loves Pomona and repeatedly visits her in disguises before finally appearing as himself and winning her love. Disguised as an old woman, Vertumnus tells Pomona the story of Iphis, who kills himself after Anaxarete cruelly rejects him, and Anaxarete is transformed into stone when she sees his funeral. Pomona then accepts Vertumnus, and the unit also describes her later role as a patroness of orchards and other fruits.

CUPID AND PSYCHE

Psyche's extraordinary beauty angers Venus, who orders Cupid to make her fall in love with an unworthy person, but Cupid wounds himself and becomes her husband. Psyche lives with her invisible husband in a splendid palace until her jealous elder sisters persuade her to view him, causing him to abandon her. Venus then imposes several tasks on Psyche, which she completes with help from ants, a river-god, a tower's voice, and Cupid. Jupiter finally grants Psyche immortality, and she and Cupid remain married and have a daughter named Pleasure.

The fable of Cupid and Psyche is usually considered allegorical.

The fable of Cupid and Psyche is presented as an allegory of the human soul’s purification through suffering and preparation for true happiness. Psyche is associated with the Greek word for butterfly and soul, and is represented in art as a winged maiden, alone or with Cupid. The story first appears in the works of Apuleius and is later referenced by Milton, T. K. Hervey, Keats, and Moore. In the allegory, Psyche ultimately becomes Love’s angel bride in heaven.

Chapter VII

Cadmus searches for his sister Europa, follows Apollo's oracle to the site of Thebes, and kills a serpent sacred to Mars. He sows the serpent's teeth, from which armed men arise; five survivors help him build Thebes, and he later becomes king of the Enchelians before turning into a serpent with Harmonia. The Myrmidons originate when Jupiter transforms ants into people at Aeacus's request after a plague destroys the population of Aegina. Cephalus visits Aeacus to seek soldiers, and Aeacus offers him the newly created, industrious Myrmidons.

Chapter VIII

The unit recounts the stories of Scylla and Minos during the siege of Megara, Echo and Narcissus, Clytie and Apollo, and Hero and Leander. Scylla betrays her father Nisus for love of Minos but is rejected and transformed into a bird, while Narcissus wastes away after falling in love with his reflection and Echo becomes only a voice. Clytie is transformed into a sunflower because of her unreturned love for Apollo. Hero and Leander's love ends when Leander drowns while swimming the Hellespont, after which Hero kills herself.

Chapter IX

Minerva, goddess of wisdom and the arts, defeats Arachne in a weaving contest after Arachne challenges her and insults the gods; Arachne is transformed into a spider. Niobe, queen of Thebes, boasts of her fourteen children and causes the people to abandon the worship of Latona, so Apollo and Diana kill all of Niobe's children, after which Niobe becomes stone. The unit also introduces the Graeae and the Gorgons, including Medusa, and explains a theory that they personify different terrors of the sea.

PERSEUS AND MEDUSA

Perseus, son of Danae and Jupiter, survives Acrisius's attempt to prevent the prophecy about his death and grows up on Seriphus. With help from Minerva and Mercury, Perseus slaughters Medusa, uses her head to turn Atlas and his enemies to stone, and rescues Andromeda from a sea-monster. At the wedding feast, Perseus defeats Phineus and his followers by displaying Medusa's head. The prophecy concerning Acrisius is fulfilled when Perseus accidentally kills him with a discus, and Perseus later gives Medusa's head to Minerva.

Chapter X

The unit describes mythological monsters, including giants, the Sphinx, Pegasus, the Chimaera, Centaurs, Pygmies, and the Griffin. OEdipus becomes king of Thebes after solving the Sphinx's riddle, but later learns that he killed Laius, his father, and married Jocasta, his mother. Bellerophon captures Pegasus with Minerva's help and uses the winged horse to defeat the Chimaera, though his pride later brings ruin. The unit also recounts the wisdom of Chiron, the battles of the Pygmies, and the Griffin's guardianship of gold.

Chapter XI

The unit tells how Jason and the Argonauts sailed in the Argo to Colchis to obtain the Golden Fleece for Jason's uncle Pelias. With help from Medea, Jason survived the fire-breathing bulls, the armed men sprung from the dragon's teeth, and the sleepless dragon guarding the fleece. Jason and Medea escaped with the fleece and returned safely to Thessaly, while the unit also describes the voyage's legendary heroes, including Hercules, and suggests that the story may preserve memories of an early maritime expedition.

MEDEA AND AESON

Medea rejuvenates Aeson through incantations, a magic caldron, and enchanted substances, but later uses deception and sorcery to help bring about Pelias's death and takes revenge on Jason. The Calydonian Hunt gathers heroes from Greece to destroy the Calydonian boar, and Meleager kills the boar before dying through the power of the fatal brand preserved by Althea. Atalanta competes in a deadly race against her suitors, and Hippomenes wins her with three golden apples given by Venus. Venus and Cybele then transform Atalanta and Hippomenes into lions after they fail to honor the gods.

Chapter XII

This unit recounts the birth, labors, adventures, death, and apotheosis of Hercules, the son of Jupiter and Alcmena who was opposed by Juno and subjected to Eurystheus. It describes his victories over monsters and giants, his quests for the Amazons’ girdle, Geryon’s oxen, the golden apples, and Cerberus, and his service under Omphale. Hercules dies after being poisoned by the robe treated with the blood of Nessus, but Jupiter makes his divine nature immortal and takes him to heaven, where he marries Hebe. The unit also explains Hebe’s role as cupbearer of the gods and Ganymede’s appointment as her successor.

Chapter XIII

Theseus, son of Aegeus, becomes a hero by defeating robbers and monsters, rescuing Athens from the Minotaur, and becoming king of Athens. He later fights the Amazons, befriends Pirithous, descends with him to the underworld, and suffers the deaths of his father and son before dying at Scyros. Daedalus builds the labyrinth and escapes Crete by flying with his son Icarus, who falls into the sea. Castor and Pollux, brothers of Helen, become renowned heroes, patrons of sailors, and the constellation Gemini.

Chapter XIV

Bacchus, son of Jupiter and Semele, survives Semele’s destruction, becomes associated with vine cultivation, and establishes his worship after wandering through Asia and India. In Thebes, Pentheus opposes Bacchus, rejects Acetes’s account of the god’s power, and is torn apart by his own mother and other worshippers. Acetes recounts how Bacchus punished Tyrrhenian mariners by transforming them into dolphins after they tried to sell him as a slave. Ariadne, abandoned by Theseus on Naxos, is consoled and married by Bacchus, whose wedding gift becomes the Ariadne’s crown constellation after her death.

Chapter XV

The unit describes Pan and other rural deities, including the nymphs associated with woods, mountains, springs, and the sea. It recounts how Erisichthon was punished by Ceres and Famine after destroying a sacred oak, while Rhoecus was rewarded and then punished after neglecting a Hamadryad’s messenger. It surveys the water deities, including Neptune, Amphitrite, Nereus, Doris, Triton, Proteus, Thetis, Leucothea, and Palaemon. It also introduces the Camenae, Egeria, and the four winds, especially Boreas and Zephyrus.

Chapter XVI

Achelous recounts how Hercules defeated him in wrestling and in his serpent and bull forms, tore off his horn, and thereby became associated with the Cornucopia. Admetus is spared death only after Alcestis offers herself in his place, but Hercules later forces Death to surrender her. Antigone remains loyal to OEdipus and defies Creon's order by burying Polynices, while Haemon dies after failing to save her. Penelope stays faithful to Ulysses during the Trojan War by delaying her suitors with the unfinished robe known as Penelope's web.

Chapter XVII

The unit recounts Orpheus's marriage to Eurydice, her death, and Orpheus's failed attempt to rescue her from the underworld through the power of music. It describes Orpheus's later death at the hands of the Thracian maidens and his reunion with Eurydice after death. It also explains Aristaeus's role as a beekeeper and his recovery of a swarm through instructions from Proteus and Cyrene. The unit concludes with brief accounts of Amphion and other mythical musicians and prophets.

Chapter XVIII

Chapter XVIII recounts legendary adventures associated with the poets Arion, Ibycus, Simonides, and Sappho. Arion survives a plot by sailors when a dolphin carries him safely to shore, while Ibycus's murderers are exposed after the cranes he invoked appear during a theatrical performance of the Furies. Simonides is saved from the collapse of Scopas's banquet hall by a message attributed to Castor and Pollux. Sappho is remembered for her love of Phaon and her leap from Leucadia, and the chapter also discusses the lasting influence of early Greek poets.

Chapter XIX

The unit recounts the myths of Endymion, Orion, Aurora and Tithonus, and Acis and Galatea. Endymion receives perpetual youth and sleep, while Diana watches over him; Orion is blinded, restored, and later placed among the stars after Diana accidentally kills him. Aurora’s beloved Tithonus grows old despite receiving immortality, and their son Memnon dies fighting Achilles at Troy. Galatea tells Scylla how Polyphemus’s jealousy led to Acis’s death, after which she transforms Acis into a river.

Chapter XX

The Judgment of Paris begins when Paris awards a golden apple to Venus, who promises him the fairest woman, Helen, and helps him carry her from Sparta to Troy. Menelaus calls upon the Greek chiefs to recover Helen, and Ulysses helps recruit reluctant leaders, including Achilles. Agamemnon commands the Greek expedition against Priam’s Troy, while Hector leads the Trojan resistance. Before the fleet can sail, Diana demands the sacrifice of Iphigenia, whom she ultimately rescues and carries to Tauris.

THE ILIAD

The Iliad begins with the quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon, after Agamemnon refuses to return Chryseis and then takes Briseis from Achilles. Achilles withdraws from the war, and Jupiter grants the Trojans success until Patroclus leads the Myrmidons in Achilles' armor to rescue the Greeks and their ships. Patroclus drives back the Trojans and kills Sarpedon, but Apollo and Hector help bring about his mortal wounding and death. The Greeks recover Patroclus's body and send Antilochus to tell Achilles, while Hector takes Achilles' armor.

After Patroclus's death, Achilles reconciles with Agamemnon, receives new armor from Vulcan through Thetis, and returns to battle against the Trojans. He defeats Hector in single combat and drags Hector's body before Troy, while Priam, Hecuba, and Andromache mourn. Jupiter sends Priam to ransom Hector's body, and Achilles, moved by Priam's appeal and the memory of his own father, returns it and grants a twelve-day truce. Troy then performs Hector's funeral rites, while the Greeks honor Patroclus with a funeral, games, and a banquet.

Chapter XXI

The unit recounts the final fall of Troy, including Achilles's death, the capture of the Palladium, and the Greeks' use of the Wooden Horse to enter and destroy the city. It describes the fates of Priam, Hecuba, Cassandra, Polyxena, Helen, and Menelaus after Troy's defeat. It also tells how Agamemnon was murdered by Clytemnestra and AEgisthus, and how Orestes and Electra responded to his death. Finally, it follows Orestes and Pylades through their escape from Tauris with Iphigenia and Diana's statue, ending with Orestes's acquittal at Athens.

Chapter XXII

The Odyssey narrates Ulysses's wanderings as he returns from Troy to his kingdom of Ithaca. He encounters the Lotus-eaters, Cyclopes, Aeolus, Laestrygonians, Circe, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the sacred cattle of the Sun. After his crew is destroyed for killing the cattle, Ulysses reaches Calypso's island, where Calypso keeps him until Jupiter orders her to release him.

Chapter XXIII

Ulysses is shipwrecked on Scheria, where Nausicaa and the Phaeacians provide him with clothing, hospitality, gifts, and a ship to return to Ithaca. After reaching Ithaca, Minerva disguises Ulysses as a beggar so that he can secretly reunite with Telemachus and plan vengeance against the suitors occupying his palace. Ulysses wins the contest of the bow, reveals himself, and, with Telemachus and faithful servants, kills the suitors. The Phaeacian ship is later transformed into a rock by Neptune.

Chapter XXIV

Aeneas escapes the destruction of Troy with his father Anchises and son, then leads Trojan fugitives through many lands toward Italy. Along the way, the Trojans encounter the Harpies, Polyphemus, and storms stirred up by Juno before Neptune calms the sea. In Carthage, Queen Dido welcomes Aeneas and falls in love with him, but he leaves at Jupiter’s command, and Dido kills herself. During the final voyage toward Italy, Palinurus, Aeneas’s pilot, is overcome by sleep and falls overboard as the ships continue on course.

Chapter XXV

The unit describes Virgil's account of Aeneas's journey with the Sibyl through the infernal regions, from Lake Avernus and the river Cocytus to Tartarus and Elysium. Aeneas encounters Charon, Palinurus, Dido, the condemned dead, and the blessed, eventually meeting his father Anchises. Anchises explains the purification, rebirth, and transmigration of souls and foretells the wars and triumphs that will lead to a Trojan state and the rise of Rome in Italy. The unit also compares different classical ideas of Elysium and recounts legends concerning the Sibyl and her prophetic books.

Chapter XXVI

Aeneas reaches Italy and is favorably received by Latinus, who recognizes him as the foreign husband foretold for Lavinia, but Juno sends Alecto to provoke opposition and war. Turnus, the Rutulians, Mezentius, and other allies prepare to fight the Trojans, while Camilla joins Turnus with her mounted warriors. Guided by Tiber, Aeneas reaches Evander, gains an alliance with him and Pallas, and is directed to lead the Etruscans against Mezentius and Turnus. The people open the temple of Janus, and the country is consumed by war against the Trojans.

NISUS AND EURYALUS

Turnus attacks the Trojan camp while Aeneas is absent, but Nisus and Euryalus attempt a night raid to reach Aeneas and are killed after being discovered. Aeneas returns with Etrurian allies, and the war between the Trojans and Rutulians continues with the deaths of Mezentius, Lausus, Pallas, and Camilla. Aeneas defeats Turnus in single combat after seeing Pallas's belt and kills him. The unit closes the Aeneid's story by describing Aeneas's marriage to Lavinia, the founding of Alba Longa, and the later founding of Rome by Romulus and Remus.

Chapter XXVII

The unit describes Pythagoras, his teachings about numbers, the soul, music, and the universe, and the practices of the Pythagoreans at Crotona. It explains metempsychosis, the harmony associated with the musical proportions of the heavens, and the conflict between Crotona and luxurious Sybaris. The unit then surveys Egyptian religion, including its changing interpretations, solar worship, local triads, and principal deities such as Osiris, Set, Horus, Amen Ra, Ra, and Isis. It also discusses Champollion the younger's role in making Egyptian hieroglyphics accessible to modern study.

MYTH OF OSIRIS AND ISIS

The unit recounts the myth of Osiris and Isis, including Osiris's gifts to Egypt, his murder by Typhon, and Isis's search for and burial of his body. It explains the worship of Apis at Memphis and the Egyptian belief that Osiris's soul inhabited the sacred bull. It describes the major ancient oracles, including those of Jupiter at Dodona, Apollo at Delphi, Trophonius at Lebadea, Aesculapius at Epidaurus, and Apis at Memphis. It also discusses the belief that pagan oracles became silent at the birth of Christ and cites literary allusions to these myths and oracles.

Chapter XXVIII

The unit presents five theories about the origins of Pagan mythology and concludes that myths arose from a combination of sources, including attempts to explain nature and names. It describes celebrated statues of the gods, especially works associated with Phidias and the Apollo Belvedere, Diana à la Biche, and Venus of Melos. It then discusses Homer, Virgil, and Ovid, whose Iliad, Odyssey, Aeneid, Metamorphoses, and Fasti supplied much of the mythological material treated in the book. The unit also notes uncertainty about Homer’s authorship and praises the enduring literary achievements of Virgil and Ovid.

Chapter XXIX

This unit examines the modern monsters Phoenix, cockatrice or basilisk, unicorn, and salamander, whose stories survived in popular belief after Paganism was superseded by Christianity. The Phoenix is described through accounts by Ovid, Tacitus, Herodotus, and others as a long-lived bird that dies, renews itself, and carries its predecessor’s remains to the Sun’s temple. The cockatrice or basilisk is portrayed as a crowned serpent whose breath, gaze, or poison kills, though it can be defeated by a weasel, rue, or a cock’s crow. The unicorn and salamander are likewise treated as legendary animals, with the unicorn captured through a virgin’s innocence and the salamander’s supposed resistance to fire explained by natural observations and asbestos.

Chapter XXX

The unit surveys Eastern religions, especially Zoroastrianism in Persia and Hindu traditions, Brahmanism, and Buddhism in India and other parts of Asia. It describes Zoroaster's teachings about Ormuzd, Ahriman, and the eventual triumph of good over evil, along with Persian rites and the survival of the Parsees. Hindu mythology is presented through the Vedas, Brahma, Vishnu, Siva, the Trimurti, and the Avatars of Vishnu, including Krishna. The unit also explains Hindu castes, the status of Brahmans, Shatriyas, Vaissyas, Sudras, and the severely excluded Pariahs.

BUDDHA

The unit describes Buddha, identified as Gautama, his rejection of Brahmanic austerities, attainment of liberating knowledge, and preaching of Buddhism from Benares across India. It explains Buddhist rejection of the Vedas, caste distinctions, and bloody sacrifices, as well as Buddhism's spread to Ceylon, China, and other regions after persecution in India. It also describes the Grand Lama and Dalai Lama as Buddhist spiritual leaders in Thibet and recounts how reports of an Asian religious chief contributed to the European legend of Prester John. The text further compares Lamaism with Roman Catholic institutions and suggests possible influence from Nestorian Christians.

Chapter XXXI

The unit introduces Northern mythology as preserved in the Eddas, describing the creation of the world and humanity, the cosmic tree Ygdrasil, and the realms of the gods, giants, and the dead. It focuses on Odin, Valhalla, the Valkyrior, and the daily battles and feasts of heroes chosen from earthly battlefields. It also describes major gods and beings including Thor, Frey, Freya, Loki, Fenris, the Midgard serpent, and Hela. Several stories recount the building of the gods' fortress, the recovery of Thor's hammer from Thrym, and Frey's marriage to Gerda after surrendering his sword.

Chapter XXXII

Thor, accompanied by Thialfi and Loki, travels toward the giants' country and reaches Utgard, where Utgard-Loki challenges them to contests. Loki loses an eating contest to Logi, Thialfi loses races to Hugi, and Thor fails to empty a drinking horn, lift a cat, or defeat the old woman Elli. Utgard-Loki later reveals that these contests and the travelers' meeting with Skrymir were shaped by illusions involving Fire, Thought, the sea, the Midgard serpent, and Old Age. The unit also recounts Thor's later battle with the giant Hrungnir, whom Thor kills with his mallet.

Chapter XXXIII

Baldur is killed when Loki guides the blind Hodur to throw mistletoe at him, and an attempt to secure his release from Hel fails because Thaukt refuses to weep. The gods burn Baldur and Nanna on his ship, while Loki is captured and punished. The unit describes the Elves, Ragnarok, Runic letters, the Skalds, and Iceland as the source of the Eddas and Sagas.

Chapter XXXIV

The unit describes the Druids, the priests, magistrates, scholars, physicians, and teachers of the ancient Celtic nations. It explains their beliefs, sacred places, sacrifices, festivals, learning, moral instruction, and oral traditions, including the roles of the Bards. The Druids were persecuted during the Roman invasion and retreated to Anglesey and Iona. Their influence in Iona and the surrounding regions ended when St. Columba arrived and led the inhabitants to profess Christianity.

IONA

Iona, a small island near Mull, became a center of civilization and religion after Columba landed there with twelve followers in 563 and founded a monastery. Columba and the Culdees preached the gospel, taught youth, and extended Christian influence through the Scottish Highlands and Islands, despite resistance from Druids and heathen peoples. The Culdees allowed marriage and did not require poverty, but their communities were eventually suppressed as the Romish Church gained power. Iona later suffered repeated attacks by Norwegian and Danish rovers and became known for its ecclesiastical, sepulchral, and earlier Druidical remains.

SKETCH OF THE HISTORY OF GREEK SCULPTURE

The unit traces Greek sculpture from primitive, decorative forms to the classical achievements of Myron, Polycleitos, and Pheidias. It discusses innovations including bronze casting, marble sculpture, and chryselephantine work, as well as the development of artistic schools at Aegina, Athens, and Argos. Myron is associated with realistic movement, Polycleitos with ideal human form, and Pheidias with divine beauty and the monumental sculptures of the Parthenon. The Parthenon’s pediments, frieze, and metopes are described, including the representation of the Pan-Athenaic procession.

The fame of Pheidias himself, however, rested very largely on

Pheidias’s fame rested largely on the Athene Promachos, the Athene Parthenos, and the Olympian Zeus. The Athene Promachos was an early, dignified statue that retained conventions of the earlier school, and no copy survives. The Athene Parthenos symbolized the protecting divinity of Athens, while the Olympian Zeus represented the greater divinity honored throughout Hellas. Both the Athene Parthenos and the Olympian Zeus were chryselephantine works praised for their technical mastery, perfect form, and intellectual beauty.

The unit traces Greek sculpture from the post-Pheidian works in Athens through the later schools of Scopas, Praxiteles, and Lysippos to the decline of Greek art under Roman influence. It contrasts the restrained, ideal, and reposeful art of Pheidias with later interests in sensual beauty, individual character, dramatic action, and emotional suffering. It discusses major works including the Niobe group, Hermes of Olympia, Victory of Samothrace, Venus of Milo, Dying Gladiator, Laocoon, and Farnese Bull. The unit concludes that Greek art lost imaginative power while retaining technical correctness and that the Renaissance later restored Greek myths as artistic subject matter.
