Antiochus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Antiochus was a king of Syria, described as generous and mild yet repeatedly portrayed in conflicting ways. He besieged Jerusalem, accepted hostages, pardoned the Jews, destroyed the walls, and collected tribute; another account says Antiochus surnamed Epiphanes defiled the Jewish temple and compelled Jews to eat swine’s flesh. He defeated and spared Egyptians, but was also criticized for luxury, sacrilege at Jupiter Belus’s temple, and neglect of military affairs. Though secretly hostile to the Romans, he sought friendship with them on equal terms and claimed sovereignty in Europe. After defeat he abandoned Europe, sought peace on increasingly harsher terms, and accepted a settlement surrendering territory, elephants, ships, Hannibal, Thoas, and others, while paying fifteen thousand Euboean talents.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK XXXIV.

Antiochus besieged Jerusalem, but after receiving hostages he pardoned the Jews, demolished the city's walls, and collected the tribute due. His friends urged him to destroy the Jews or abolish their laws, but he was described as generous and mild. Antiochus surnamed Epiphanes was also said to have entered the Jewish temple, sacrificed a swine there, defiled their books, extinguished their temple lamp, and compelled Jews to eat swine's flesh.

BOOK XXVI.

Antiochus was condemned for attacking Roman soldiers in peacetime, neglecting military affairs for luxury, and abandoning Lysimachia, which later aided Scipio's army. He also robbed the temple of Jupiter Belus in Elymais after pretending that its inhabitants had made war against him, and the text says the gods soon took vengeance for this sacrilege. After a defeat, he became disheartened and resolved to abandon Europe.

37. P. Scipio, when he was but very young, performed great and

Antiochus III displayed both admirable and contemptible conduct. He defeated the Egyptians but spared them and gained advantage through his humanity; he also behaved in an undignified and ridiculous manner at public games, while secretly remaining an enemy of the Romans.

BOOK XXXVIL

Antiochus sought peace with Rome, first offering to bear half the war's expenses and surrendering several cities, and later asking pardon on any terms. He accepted a settlement requiring him to abandon Europe and territories on the Roman side of the Taurus, surrender his elephants and long ships, pay fifteen thousand Euboean talents, and deliver Hannibal, Thoas, and others.
