Philip in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Philip is a Macedonian king, ruler, general, and later supreme governor of Greece whose career moves from hostage to expansive monarch and then ends in assassination, though later records also attach a harsher, troubled reign to a Philip of Macedon. Held hostage by the Illyrians and sent to Thebes, he escaped and became king after Perdiccas was killed. He secured Macedonia by defeating Pæones, Illyrians, Thracians, and other opponents; captured places including Amphipolis, Pydna, Methone, Micaberna, Torone, Olynthus, and Elatea; enlarged his kingdom; and drew wealth from gold mines and tribute. His methods combine military force, mercenaries, bribery, and diplomacy: he said gold enlarged his dominion more than his sword, yet he also rewarded bravery and released Athenian prisoners without ransom. In Thessaly he removed tyrants, restored cities to liberty, and won enduring Thessalian alliance. He defended the oracle, participated in Amphictyonic decisions, and ended the Sacred War, actions linked to his growing authority. His ambition then widened to sovereignty over Greece and war against Persia. After making peace when resistance checked his sieges of Perinthus and Byzantium, he defeated Athenians and Boeotians at Chaeronea, renewed peace with Athens, garrisoned Thebes, and was chosen generalissimo of Greece with absolute power. He began the Persian war by sending Attalus and Parmenio into Asia, consulted the Delphic oracle, and arranged Cleopatra's marriage to Alexander, king of Epirus. He was stabbed and killed by Pausanias of Orestis at Aegae after about twenty-four years of rule; Alexander succeeded him. Other records praise him as Europe’s greatest and most victorious prince, crediting mild treatment of conquered people and a governmental order for Greek cities, while also describing a Philip influenced toward cruelty and tyranny, piracy, plunder, executions, unnecessary wars, defeat, and preservation through Roman clemency.

Narrative, section by section

BOOK XVI.

Philip was held hostage by the Illyrians and delivered to the Thebans, who placed him under the care of the father of Epaminondas. He later escaped, became king of Macedon after Perdiccas was killed, defeated the Athenians, Pæones, and Illyrians, and prepared to attack Persia.

CHAP. 11.

Philip, king of Macedonia, defeated the Illyrians, captured Amphipolis and Pydna, and made a league with the Olynthians. He enlarged Cremides and renamed it Philippi, developed its gold mines, accumulated wealth, raised mercenaries, and bribed some Grecians.

CHAP. III.

Philip, king of Macedon, assisted the Aleuadae against the tyrants in Thessaly. He subdued the tyrants, restored the cities to liberty, and gained the lasting friendship and alliance of the Thessalians.

CHAP. V.

Philip was opposed by the kings of Thrace, the Paeones, and the Illyrians, who had previously been defeated by him individually. He attacked them suddenly while they were raising their forces, broke them apart, and forced them to submit to the Macedonian kingdom.

CHAP. VII.

Philip of Macedon took Methone, destroyed it, and forced surrounding communities to submit. He later entered Thessaly, was twice defeated by Onomarchus, but returned with Thessalian support and defeated the Phocians. He captured Onomarchus and caused the other Phocian prisoners to be thrown into the sea.

CHAP. Vlll.

Philip defeated Onomarchus, freed the Pheraeans from tyranny, restored their city to liberty, and settled other matters in Thessaly. He marched toward Pylos to fight the Phocians but returned to Macedonia after the Athenians denied him passage, enlarging his kingdom by force and by his piety toward the gods.

CHAP. X.

Philip captured Micaberna and Torone and then besieged and captured Olynthus by bribing Euthycrates and Lasthenes. He rewarded bravery, used money to corrupt citizens in other cities, and said that gold had enlarged his dominion more than his sword. He later aided the Boeotians, ended the Sacred War without a battle, approved the Amphictyonic decrees, and returned to Macedonia while looking toward command of Greece and war against Persia.

Philip appeared in defense of the oracle and prospered thereafter because of his piety. He was eventually declared supreme governor of all Greece and gained the largest kingdom in Europe.

CHAP. XI.

Philip invaded the Illyrians, ravaged their lands, captured many towns, and returned to Macedonia with booty. He later expelled the tyrants from the cities of Thessaly and invaded Thrace, forcing the Thracians to pay tribute and encouraging Greek cities to ally with him.

CHAP. XII.

Philip helped Alexander, the brother of Olympias, succeed Arybas as king of the Molossians. He besieged Perinthus with a large army, using engines, battering rams, towers, and mines, and later marched with part of his army to besiege Byzantium.

CHAP, XIII.

Philip was besieging Byzantium when Athens and other Greeks sent forces against him. He raised both his sieges and made peace with Athens and the other Greeks who were fighting him.

CHAP. XIV.

Philip captured Elatea, assembled his forces, and defeated the Athenians and Boeotians at Chaeronea. Afterward, he released the captives without ransom, renewed peace with Athens, placed a garrison in Thebes, and was appointed general of all Greece with absolute power to prepare a war against Persia.

CHAP, XV.

Philip began the war against the Persians, sent Attalus and Parmenio into Asia, consulted the Delphic oracle, and arranged the marriage of his daughter Cleopatra to Alexander, king of Epirus. He interpreted the oracle and Neoptolemus's verses as favorable signs for his Persian expedition. He was later stabbed and killed by Pausanias of Orestis at the theatre at Aegae after reigning about twenty-four years.

BOOK XVII.

His death begins the events described in this unit. Alexander succeeded him and carried on his government, while Attalus's position and Philip's arrangements in Asia became matters of immediate concern.

BOOK XXVI.

Philip of Macedon encouraged piracy, supported attacks on merchants and islands, and was influenced by Heraclides to execute senators and undertake unnecessary wars. He plundered temples, sepulchres, and places in Attica and around Pergamas, becoming widely hated. After suffering defeat and nearly losing his kingdom, he was preserved by Roman clemency.

Philip son of Amyntas liberated a kingdom subject to the Illyrians and enlarged his kingdom through military skill and mild treatment of those he conquered. After defeating the Athenians, he buried their dead and returned more than two thousand prisoners without ransom, leading his former enemies to yield him the sovereignty of Greece. He later maintained his gains by destroying Olynthus and striking fear into his enemies.

Philip the son of Amyntas in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Philip, son of Amyntas, is a Macedonian king regarded as the most valiant among Macedonian kings, yet judged not good because he breaks oaths to the gods, violates truces, and dishonours good faith. He joins the Thebans in the war against the Phocians, defeats them, and his victory brings Onomarchus’s flight and death. He later encamps at Nestane and seeks, through Arcadia, to detach the Arcadians from the other Greeks. During his reign many Greek cities are betrayed. After Chaeronea he removes Linus’s remains in obedience to a dream, but erects no trophies for any victory.

CHAPTER X.

During the reign of Philip, the son of Amyntas, many Greek cities were betrayed, and Lacedæmon was not the only city affected. The text also contrasts his treatment of Greek opponents with the later Roman practice of sending accused Achæans to Rome.

CHAPTER VII.

Philip, son of Amyntas, encamped at Nestane and used Arcadia in an attempt to win over the Arcadians and separate them from the other Greeks. He is regarded as the most valiant of the Macedonian kings, but not as a good man because he broke oaths, violated truces, and dishonoured good faith.

CHAPTER XL.

Philip son of Amyntas erected no trophy at Chæronea or elsewhere for victories over Greeks or barbarians. The text contrasts this with the trophies of Sulla and the Romans.

CHAPTER II.

Philip the son of Amyntas joined the Thebans and defeated the Phocians in battle. His victory led to Onomarchus's flight and death.
