Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Carthage is a strongly fortified city in Africa that wages wars, makes peace, supplies armies and fleets, and is ultimately captured and destroyed by Rome. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Carthage is a political and military center that conducts wars and diplomacy, suffers defeats and disasters, and repeatedly prepares, celebrates, recovers, and resists rebellion. In The Aeneid, Carthage is Dido’s newly rising Tyrian colony in Libya, a prosperous but unfinished city whose building is neglected during her love for Aeneas and whose destruction she fears after his departure. In Heroides and Amores, Carthage is Dido’s newly founded and expanding city, whose walls, kingdom, peoples, wealth, and power she offers to Aeneas instead of Italy. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Carthage is a Tyrian colony founded by Dido on Africa’s coast that grows around Byrsa, receives Aeneas and the Trojans, and is destined to rival Rome.

Carthage in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Carthage first appears

Abstract written by a model

Carthage is a city in Africa, situated by the sea and described as a Carthaginian stronghold. It was strongly walled, with fortifications forty cubits high and twenty-two broad, making it difficult to take by force. In its earlier recorded conflicts, it prepared for war against Timoleon by raising troops, money, mercenaries from Spain, Gaul, and Liguria, and a large fleet. After defeat in Sicily it recalled Cisco, made him general, sought foreign soldiers, and requested peace. Against Agathocles it raised a fleet and army under Amilcar, sent reinforcements, protested breaches of agreements, and through Amilcar made peace with the Agrigentines, Geloans, and Messenians, obtaining Heraclea, Selinus, and Himera. Agathocles's arrival in Africa brought terror, defeat, blockade, prayers, offerings, and sacrifices within the city. Carthage also faced the loss of Amilcar, threats from Ophelas, and Bomilcar's revolt; its citizens defeated Bomilcar's forces, restored the ancient government, and executed him. During a siege and scarcity of provisions, its senate sent thirty thousand soldiers into the countryside, relieving the shortage, restoring allies, and dividing the enemy. After a camp disaster, returning troops entered through opened gates, and peace with the Greeks restored towns they had held. Carthage later supplied troops, corn, provisions, and fleets to relieve Lilybaeum, acted against the Numidians, and chose Hannibal as general. Its end is defined by Rome: after P. Scipio defeated Hannibal, Carthage came under the Roman yoke. Though Roman assurances initially allowed its country, laws, temples, sepulchres, liberties, and estates, Rome later ordered the city abandoned and destroyed, with a replacement settlement eighty furlongs from the sea. The Romans captured and razed Carthage; its spoils were searched by Sicilian ambassadors, and the text links its destruction to later Roman faction, revolt, civil war, and hostility from allies.

Carthage in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Carthage first appears

Abstract written by a model

Carthage is a city in Africa, a political and military center repeatedly shaped by war, diplomacy, celebration, disaster, and recovery. It agreed with Xerxes to attack Greeks in Italy and Sicily while he invaded Greece, then spent three years raising a vast army and fleet. Its Sicilian expedition was defeated at Himera, bringing fear and mourning, but its ambassadors obtained peace from Gelon under financial and religious conditions. Carthage later welcomed Hannibal with public acclamation after his successes in Sicily, answered Syracusan peace envoys doubtfully, and renewed preparations for operations there with armies, warships, and transports. It received Agrigentum's choicest spoils, including the Phalarian bull, though the bull was later found there when the city was destroyed and restored to the Agrigentines. Alarmed by Dionysius's demands amid plague and poor preparation, it also served as Himiico's departure point and as refuge after defeat. Finally threatened by African rebels, it used ceremonies, sacrifices, naval preparation, and Sardinian supplies until the rebel army dispersed.

Carthage in The Aeneid

Virgil · 28 to 18 BC · the work’s page · read it where Carthage first appears

Abstract written by a model

Carthage is a newly rising Tyrian colony and city in Libya, on the African coast, founded by Dido after her flight from Tyre and ruled by her. Prosperous in war and trade, it is especially beloved by Juno, who fears an ancient prophecy that the Trojan race will destroy its towers. Its visible turrets, walls, mounds, harbor works, temples, and homes mark a city still incomplete and surrounded by enemies. When Aeneas arrives, Jupiter orders its ports opened to Trojan guests. Later, Dido and Aeneas neglect the city’s building works for their love, until Jupiter orders Aeneas to leave; Trojan departure preparations fill its shore and harbor, while Dido recalls raising the lofty city herself and fears fire might consume it.

Chapter XXIV

Carthage is a Tyrian colony on the African coast opposite Sicily, founded by Dido after she escaped from Tyre. It grows around the citadel called Byrsa and is destined to become a powerful rival of Rome. Aeneas and the Trojans arrive there after a storm, and Dido receives them with hospitality.
