Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Ptolemy is presented as a Macedonian commander who becomes king of Egypt, expands and defends his power through warfare, alliances, wealth, fleets, garrisons, calculated generosity, and religious honors. In Description of Greece, Ptolemy, son of Lagus, becomes king of Egypt after Alexander’s death, seizes and expands his kingdom, kills rivals, brings Alexander’s corpse from Memphis, resists invasions, aids allies, opposes enemies, and marries his sister Arsinoe.

Ptolemy in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Ptolemy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Ptolemy is presented chiefly as a Macedonian commander who becomes king and ruler of Egypt. Early on, he is loved for valor and obliging behavior after Alexander heals a poisoned wound. After the division of provinces, he takes Egypt, governs it mildly, uses a treasury of eight thousand talents to hire mercenaries, allies with Antipater against Perdiccas, and gains Cyrene through Ophelas. He honors Alexander by receiving the body in Syria, keeping it in Alexandria, and establishing a temple, sacrifices, and magnificent rites. His power rests on Egypt, strong garrisons, wealth, fleets, armies, and support won through loyalty and affection. He defends Egypt against Perdiccas, treats enemy dead honorably, and declines a higher protectorship. He expands into Syria, Phoenicia, Coele-Syria, Cyprus, Cilicia, and Greek cities, while repeatedly aligning with or opposing Antipater, Cassander, Seleucus, Lysimachus, Antigonus, Demetrius, and Eumenes. He defeats Demetrius at Gaza, yet later withdraws to Egypt, destroying or abandoning Syrian and Phoenician positions when Antigonus approaches. The record emphasizes both force and calculated generosity: he rewards commanders, releases prisoners, returns Demetrius's dead and household without ransom, aids Rhodes with supplies and soldiers, and is later worshipped there as a god. He also suffers major reverses, notably defeat by Demetrius at sea near Salamis, after which he assumes the title of king. He fortifies the Nile and preserves Egypt against invasion, later joins leagues against Antigonus, and returns from a truce at Sidon after a false report. The final material also portrays a Ptolemy who loses Egypt, is blamed for surrendering it without a struggle, is expelled, and travels toward Rome.

Ptolemy was greatly loved for his valor and obliging behavior. He was wounded by a poisoned weapon during the fighting near Harmatelia and was believed to be in desperate danger, but Alexander's treatment restored him to perfect health.

CHAP. II.

Ptolemy possessed himself of Egypt, treated its people mildly, used a treasure of eight thousand talents to raise mercenaries, and formed a league with Antipater against Perdiccas. He later sent Ophelas with forces to assist the exiles from Cyrene, and Cyrene and its neighboring cities were delivered into his hands.

Ptolemy was condemned for surrendering his rich kingdom without a struggle when the enemy was still far away. His weakness was attributed to Eulaius, who had raised him from childhood in pleasures and womanish recreations; after being driven out, Ptolemy traveled toward Rome and declined Demetrius's offer of royal insignia.

He gained Egypt and allied with Antipater. He defeated or opposed several rivals, gained Syria and Phoenicia through Nicanor, and later joined Cassander, Seleucus, and Lysimachus against Antigonus.

Ptolemy acts in Cilicia and elsewhere, comes to Corinth, and later goes to Cyprus. He fights Demetrius at sea and is routed, joins Cassander, Lysimachus, and Seleucus against Antigonus, and besieges Sidon before returning to Egypt on a false report.

Ptolemy in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Ptolemy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Ptolemy, said to be son of Philip by the Macedonians and putatively of Lagus, became king and ruler of Egypt after Alexander’s death. He supported dividing Alexander’s dominions, seized Egypt, killed Cleomenes, Argæus, Eurydice’s son, and another brother, and brought Alexander the Great’s corpse from Memphis. He expanded into Syria, Phœnicia, and Cyprus, resisted invasions, allied against Antigonus, aided Rhodes and Pyrrhus, opposed Magas and Antiochus I, and used then abandoned Galatian mercenaries. He married his sister Arsinoe but had sons by Lysimachus’s daughter.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

Ptolemy distinguished himself in Asia Minor and aided Alexander at Oxydracæ. After Alexander’s death, he resisted giving Alexander’s dominions to Aridæus, supported dividing the empire into separate kingdoms, seized Egypt, and killed Cleomenes. He later took Syria, Phœnicia, and Cyprus, allied with Cassander, Lysimachus, and Seleucus against Antigonus, defended Egypt from invasion, aided Rhodes, and restored Pyrrhus to Thesprotian Epirus.

CHAPTER VII.

Ptolemy I Soter married his sister Arsinoe, killed his brother Argæus and another brother, and brought Alexander’s corpse from Memphis. He opposed Magas of Cyrene and Antiochus I, sent forces against Antigonus I, and had sons by the daughter of Lysimachus rather than by Arsinoe.
