At the cottage, Bruin takes on extensive household work, including washing, churning, baking, brewing, milking, tending the pig, and preparing meals. He sleeps on the hearth-rug, does not hibernate because the Madam would disapprove, and enjoys company, stories, games, and Russian backgammon. His size causes comic difficulties: dancing school leaves him sore, he cannot master the waltz, and he ruins the grandmother’s custard pie when he falls on it. Yet he remains willing to practice and help, serving as a step-ladder, polishing the floor with brushes on his feet, and wearing a red rose in his left ear. By the end, when the animals depart, Bruin cannot say goodbye. He hides in the wood-shed and splits excessive kindling, explaining that he cannot leave Toto, the blind grandmother, the calf, the pig, or the cottage work. He stays with the grandmother and settles permanently as her devoted friend, protector, and domestic helper.

Narrative, section by section

8 sections; each heading opens the text

Toto's Merry Winter / CHAPTER I.

Bruin enters with Toto carrying a pail of milk and says he has been talking to the pig. He ties on a large apron, tends the fire, stirs the soup-kettle, and helps prepare supper. He asks the grandmother to tell a story after admitting that dancing-school left him sore.

CHAPTER III.

Bruin misunderstands the meaning of a Chinese pig-tail and is corrected by the grandmother. He secures the cottage, sleeps on the hearth-rug, and wakes briefly when his fleas disturb him before going back to sleep.

CHAPTER IV.

Bruin examines the cupboard door and identifies the marks as those of mice. He had previously stopped Toto and the wild-cat from choking each other during their fight, then invited Toto and <DW53> to supper. During the mice’s nighttime escape, the mice tried to climb his back, but he shook them off.

CHAPTER V.

Bruin struggles with the dancing steps, falls heavily while trying to jump and change his feet, and later practices by himself in the corner. He loses his balance while waltzing, sits on the grandmother's custard pie, ruins the pie and platter, and concludes that waltzing is not his forte.

CHAPTER VIII.

Bruin agrees to help the injured Baldhead and walks with Toto to the hermit's cave carrying food. He worries that his large, shaggy appearance is frightening and even considers shaving the hair from his face. Although his thunderous whispers cause Baldhead to faint, Bruin is later welcomed after Pigeon Pretty explains his kindness, and he laughs with the others.

_" CHAPTER XII.

Bruin plays backgammon with the hermit, prepares and warms his broth, and tries to improve it with pine-tree tops or frog dumplings. He captures Ger-Falcon after the hawk attacks Pigeon Pretty, but spares him after hearing that he was starving and accepts the hermit's plan to shelter him.

CHAPTER XIV.

Bruin helps decorate the cottage by serving as a step-ladder, polishes the floor with brushes fastened to his feet, and wears a red rose in his ear. He entertains the company with his story of rescuing the raccoon from a hollow tree and later plays blindman’s buff and other games.

CHAPTER XV.

Bruin says nothing during the farewells because he cannot bear to say good-by, and he hides in the wood-shed instead of joining the others in their departure. He is found splitting an excessive supply of kindling and explains that he cannot leave Toto or the blind grandmother, while also worrying about the calf, the pig, and the cottage work. Bruin settles permanently at the cottage, where he washes, churns, bakes, brews, milks the cow, and keeps the pig in order.
