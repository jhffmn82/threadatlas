Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Cassander is a Macedonian general and king who seizes power, conducts wars and political alliances, and eliminates rival claimants to Alexander’s succession. In Description of Greece, Cassander rules Macedonia under Antigonus, remains allied with Lysimachus, opposes Æacides, and campaigns against the young Pyrrhus. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Cassander is the Macedonian ruler who opposes Alexander’s family, restores Thebes, orders Olympias and Alexander’s sons killed, campaigns unsuccessfully, and dies of disease.

Cassander in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Cassander first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassander is a Macedonian general, ruler, king, and successor of Antipater who rises from exclusion from Macedonia’s chief command to sovereign power. After Alexander’s death, he is presented as hostile to Alexander’s interests: he resents Polyperchon’s elevation, secretly gathers allies, leaves Macedonia to seek Antigonus’s help, and works to win the chief command. With a fleet and troops he seizes the Piraeus, besieges Salamis, makes peace with Athens, and gains support in Greece. When conflict in Macedonia intensifies, including Olympias’s killing of his brother Nicanor and his friends, he abandons the siege of Tegsea, marches north, besieges Olympias at Pydna, obtains her surrender, and arranges her execution. He marries Thessalonice, founds Cassandria, rebuilds Thebes, and confines Roxana and her son at Amphipolis. His rule is sustained through campaigns, garrisons, commanders, alliances, and repeated struggles in Greece, Macedonia, and beyond, including shifting agreements and wars with Antigonus, Ptolemy, Lysimachus, Seleucus, Polyperchon, and Demetrius. Although peace terms make him sovereign in Europe until Roxana’s son reaches adulthood, fear of that son’s future claim leads him to order the secret murder of both Roxana and Alexander. He also persuades Polyperchon to murder Hercules, another son of Alexander. By the end, Cassander remains a central military and political power, defending and extending his position through alliances, warfare, control of cities, and the elimination of rivals.

CHAP. XII. /

Cassander succeeded his father, Antipater, in the kingdom after Alexander’s death. He was described as a great enemy of Alexander’s interests, allowed Olympias’s murdered body to remain unburied, and worked to rebuild Thebes, which Alexander had destroyed.

CHAP. IV.

Cassander resented Polyperchon’s appointment and considered it dishonorable that supreme authority should pass to someone outside his family. He secretly gathered supporters, sought alliances with Ptolemy and others, and plotted to obtain the chief command.

Cassander was excluded from the chief command of Macedonia but resolved to obtain sovereign authority. He secretly left Macedonia, went through the Chersonesus to the Hellespont, and sought Antigonus's assistance, claiming that Ptolemy would join him.

CHAP. V.

Cassander is mentioned in the chapter summary as later arriving at the Piraeus, opposing Polyperchon, and making peace with the Athenians, but these events are not described in the present passage.

Cassander had left Macedonia and gone to Antigonus. Nicanor said that Cassander had commissioned him to govern the garrison and that he therefore lacked authority to negotiate on his own.

Cassander sailed into the Piraeus with thirty-five long ships and four thousand men, took possession of the Piraeus and its harbour, and later besieged Salamis. He withdrew from Salamis when Polyperchon sent forces against him, made peace with Athens, and gained support from several Greek cities after Polyperchon’s failures.

Cassander was in the Peloponnese when Eurydice sent an express asking him to hasten to her aid. His supporters in Macedonia were later targeted by Olympias, who killed Nicanor, Cassander's brother, and one hundred of his friends.

Cassander abandoned the siege of Tegsea after hearing of events in Macedonia and marched there with his army. He crossed into Thessaly, recovered the passes at Perrhaebia, besieged Olympias at Pydna, defeated the relief effort of Aeacidas, and gained the support of the Epirotes and Macedonians.

Cassander besieged Pydna, secured Olympias’s surrender, and arranged for her execution after the Macedonians condemned her. He killed Aristonus, married Thessalonice, founded Cassandria, imprisoned Roxana and her son, rebuilt Thebes, and led an expedition toward Peloponnesus.

CHAP. IV,

Cassander joined Ptolemy and Lysimachus in demanding Cappadocia and Lycia, among other concessions, from Antigonus. He prepared for war against Antigonus and had forces operating in Cappadocia, while Ptolemy's declaration accused him of murdering Olympias, mistreating Roxana and the young king, rebuilding Thebes, and seeking the Macedonian kingdom.

Cassander's commander Apollonides seized Stymphalus, punished conspirators at Argos, and attacked places in Boeotia, Corinth, Orchomenus, and the Peloponnesus. Cassander later campaigned through the Peloponnesus, Etolia, Illyria, and Caria, while making alliances and placing garrisons. He also sent an army under Eupolemus into Caria, but Ptolemy defeated and captured Eupolemus.

CHAP. VI.

Cassander agrees with Antigonus and acts in Greece. His general routs the Epirots and Italians, and Cassander later goes against the Apolloniates.

He agreed with Antigonus to surrender soldiers and grant liberty to the Greek cities in Asia, but soon repudiated the arrangement and sought aid from Ptolemy and Seleucus. He fought for control of Greece, took Oreum, moved against Euboea and Macedonia, and left commanders to defend his interests.

CHAP. VH.

Cassander made peace with Ptolemy, Lysimachus, and Antigonus, under terms that made him sovereign lord of all in Europe until Alexander, the son of Roxana, came of age. Fearing that Alexander would assume his father's kingdom, he ordered Glaucias to murder Roxana and Alexander and conceal their deaths.

Cassander in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Cassander first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassander, married to Thessalonica, was driven by hatred of Alexander: he opposed Alexander’s house, restored Thebes, ordered Olympias stoned, and poisoned Alexander’s sons Hercules and Alexander. He led Macedonians in the ineffective blockade of Elatea, then died of dropsy and worms; his sons later died violently or prematurely.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

Cassander restored the Thebans to Thebes, chiefly because of his hatred for Alexander. He ordered the Macedonians to stone Olympias, Alexander's mother, and poisoned Alexander's sons, Hercules and Alexander. Cassander died suffering from dropsy and worms, and his sons later met violent or untimely ends.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Cassander blockaded Elatea with an army, but Olympiodorus mainly rendered the blockade inoperative.
