Antigonus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Antigonus is a Macedonian commander who becomes a dominant ruler in Asia. Initially friendly to Antipater and aware of Perdiccas’s hostile intentions, he escapes to Europe, warns Antipater and Craterus, and is later rewarded with Susiana and command of the royal army. He defeats and besieges Eumenes, then increasingly pursues sovereignty for himself rather than serving the kings or their guardians. He expands his forces, wealth, and influence through campaigns, provincial appointments, alliances, and the seizure of money and territory. Although he offers Eumenes rewards and status, he treats him as his chief enemy, repeatedly pursues him, defeats his forces, captures him through the Macedonians, and has him executed. Antigonus then eliminates Python, reorganizes governments across Asia, takes Susa’s treasure, and becomes absolute lord of Asia. He builds fleets, commands land and sea forces, contests Ptolemy, Cassander, Lysimachus, and Seleucus, and uses Demetrius, his son, and other generals in campaigns in Syria, Greece, Babylon, and elsewhere. He makes peace on terms recognizing him as lord of Asia, but later again resists rivals, recalls Demetrius to Asia, takes the title of king, and marches against Egypt. His course is marked by military command, political maneuvering, harsh treatment of enemies and rivals, and a sustained effort to secure kingship and sovereignty over Asia.

Antigonus was rewarded with Susiana because he had first caused the overthrow of Perdiccas and was appointed commander of the royal army. He defeated Eumenes in Cappadocia, besieged him at Nora, and began aspiring to greater power while secretly resolving not to remain subordinate to Antipater or the kings.

CHAP. IV.

Antigonus marched from Cappadocia into Pisidia, defeated Alcetas, captured several commanders, and absorbed many prisoners into his army. After Alcetas’s death, he dishonored and abandoned his body, then pursued wider power in Asia by opposing Aridaeus, seizing money intended for the kings, and attempting to remove provincial governors. He also released Eumenes from the siege of Nora after securing his cooperation.

Antigonus marched from Mesopotamia to Babylon, allied with Seleucus and Python, crossed the Tigris by a bridge of boats, and pursued Eumenes. Antigonus marched through the desert to attack Eumenes's scattered forces but was deceived by Eumenes's fires and turned aside toward inhabited country. He defeated Eumenes's horse, captured Eumenes after the battle, executed several of his supporters, and later had Eumenes put to death.

Antigonus discovered Python’s plan to win over the soldiers in Media, concealed his suspicions, and lured Python to him with the promise of a governorship. He had Python convicted and executed, defeated the associated conspirators, marched into Persia and became absolute lord of Asia, reorganized provincial governments, and seized great treasure at Susa.

CHAP. IV,

Antigonus left Aspeisas governor of Susiana, carried away the province's money, and marched to Babylon. He rejected the demands of Ptolemy, Cassander, and Lysimachus, sought alliances, entered Phoenicia, built a fleet, captured Gaza and Joppa, and besieged Tyre. He also declared Cassander an enemy unless Cassander released the young king and Roxana, razed certain cities, submitted to Antigonus's authority, freed the Greek cities, and withdrew his garrisons.

Antigonus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Antigonus is a successor general, military ruler, potent prince, and eventually king, formerly a private man. He opposed Perdiccas, defeated Eumenes and Alcetas, besieged Nora, and sought sovereignty over all while grasping at other princes’ dominions. As father and commander of Demetrius, he repeatedly directed him and his fleet against rivals. Antigonus used stratagem against the Nabataeans, falsely denying responsibility for their mistreatment to lower their caution; his Lake Asphaltites revenue project failed when Arabians attacked its workers. He faced revolts and opposition from Ptolemy, Cassander, and Ptolemy of Egypt, sending Philip and Demetrius to recover cities. He ordered Demetrius to liberate Grecian cities and fight in Cyprus; after Demetrius’s victory there, both assumed kingship. Antigonus’s Egyptian expedition failed amid defenses, terrain, shortages, and naval losses, and he returned to Syria before continuing conflict with Ptolemy, Seleucus, Cassander, and Lysimachus.

Antigonus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Antigonus is a king of the Macedonians, recorded as both son and father of Demetrius. He opposed Ptolemy, seized Syria and Phœnicia, and put them under Demetrius, but failed in campaigns against Egypt and Rhodes. He attacked and invested Athens, made peace, placed then withdrew a garrison. Later he fought Lysimachus, Cassander, and Seleucus, lost most forces and power, and died. After failing to reinforce Pyrrhus, his troops were defeated and Upper Macedonia and Thessaly were taken.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

Antigonus opposed Ptolemy and seized Syria and Phœnicia while Ptolemy was in Libya, placing them under Demetrius. He later besieged Egypt by land and sea and sent Demetrius against Rhodes, but failed against both Rhodes and Egypt. He subsequently fought Lysimachus, Cassander, and Seleucus, lost most of his forces, and died.

CHAPTER XIII.

Antigonus failed to bring reinforcements to Pyrrhus in Italy, after which Pyrrhus proclaimed war against him. Pyrrhus defeated Antigonus’s troops and the Galatian contingent with him, pursued them to the maritime cities, and took Upper Macedonia and Thessaly.

CHAPTER VI.

Antigonus, son of Demetrius, attacked Athens by land and sea and closely invested the city. He later made peace on terms allowing him to place a garrison at the Museum, though he subsequently withdrew it.
