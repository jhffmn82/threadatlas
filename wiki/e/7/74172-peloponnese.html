Peloponnesus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Peloponnesus is a place containing Sicyon, Corinth, the battle of Mantinea, and Greek cities of Sicily. It is marked first by severe internal disorder after democratic governments were restored: exiles, conspiracies, punishments, tumults, and seditions affected Phialia, Corinth, Megara, and Sicyon. Extraordinary earthquakes and inundations then destroyed cities and killed many, especially at Helice and Bura in Achaia. It became a theater of invasion and war as Boeotians, Phocians, Locrians, Arcadians, Argives, and Elians entered, and Epaminondas broke through Corinth’s defenses. Peace after Mantinea quickly failed over towns incorporated into Megalopolis. Later it hosted discharged soldiers near Taenarus and communities joining the Greek confederacy. Successive struggles brought Polyperchon, Cassander, Alexander, Aristodemus, Antigonus, Ptolemy’s commanders, and Telesphorus: cities and garrisons changed hands, Cassander controlled Argos and most of Messenia, and Telesphorus freed Alexander’s garrisons except at Sicyon and Corinth.

Peloponnesus in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Peloponnesus is a place containing Pylos, largely subdued by Pelops after he gained the kingdom of Pisa and named after him. After the translation of Hercales to the gods, the Heraclidae invaded under Hyllus; after his death they delayed their return for fifty years, then returned. It later received people withdrawing from Athens, Boeotia, and other threatened regions, who treated it as a refuge and defense and fortified its isthmus. Its central history is conflict: allied with the Lacedaemonians, it sent forces from its borders to invade Attica, while Athenians repeatedly raided its coasts, burned towns, destroyed castles, plundered, invaded, and ravaged it. Fortified Pylos made it a major theater of war, though a five-year peace followed. Finally, cities conspired against the Lacedaemonians, put war direction in Argos, and the region fractured through shifting alliances, fighting between Argos and Sparta, and political upheaval.

Peloponnese in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Peloponnese is a place divided into five parts, including Arcadia, Achaia, and three Dorian regions; Arcadians and Achæans are indigenous, while others arrived from elsewhere. The Heraclidæ returned under Tisamenus, claiming Argos and Messenia and displacing earlier rulers. After Ithome fell, Messenians evacuated, were scattered, and were later recalled and restored. It later received Demetrius’s fleet expedition and Pyrrhus, who ravaged Lacedæmonian territory, nearly took Sparta, and reached Argos.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIII.

Pyrrhus entered the Peloponnese after Cleonymus persuaded him to leave Macedonia. He ravaged Lacedæmonian territory, nearly captured Sparta, and later arrived at Argos.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Heraclidæ returned to the Peloponnese during the reign of Tisamenus. They claimed Argos and Messenia and displaced the ruling descendants of Pelops and Nestor.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Messenians were allowed to evacuate the Peloponnese after the fall of Ithome. The Thebans later recalled them from exile, and they were restored to the Peloponnese 287 years after the capture of Eira.

CHAPTER XXIX.

The Peloponnese was the destination of Demetrius, son of Philip, when Philip sent him with a fleet to obtain money. The Messenians were restored there after having been scattered to distant places for a long time.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

The Peloponnese is described as divided into five parts, including Arcadia, Achaia, and three Dorian regions. Arcadians and Achæans are identified as indigenous peoples, while other inhabitants came from elsewhere.

Peloponnese in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Peloponnese is a place with coastal states—Corinth, Epidaurus, Trœzen, Hermion, Lacedæmonian territory, Messenia, Sicyon, Achaia, and Elis—and inland Arcadia, which lacks sea access. Its record moves from its divisions and towns to conflicts: Hyllus’s Dorians failed at the Isthmus but later landed near Rhium; an Argive host was destroyed at Ismenius except Adrastus; and Epaminondas aided invited Arcadians, allied with Argives, restored Mantinea, and helped establish Megalopolis.

5 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK VIII.--ARCADIA. / CHAPTER I.

The Peloponnese is described as having coastal states including Corinth, Epidaurus, Trœzen, Hermion, Lacedæmonian territory, Messenia, Sicyon, Achaia, and Elis. Arcadia forms its inland division and has no access to the sea.

CHAPTER V.

The Dorians under Hyllus attempted to enter the Peloponnese by way of the Isthmus of Corinth but were defeated. Their later return was made by sea, with a landing near the Promontory of Rhium.

CHAPTER LIV.

The unit concludes by referring to the divisions of the Peloponnese, including their towns and notable features.

CHAPTER IX.

The Argive host came from the middle of the Peloponnese to the middle of Bœotia. Peloponnesian forces were destroyed at Ismenius except for Adrastus.

CHAPTER XIV.

Epaminondas turned his attention to the Peloponnese after capturing Ceressus because the Arcadians eagerly invited his cooperation. He made the Argives his voluntary allies, restored Mantinea, and helped establish Megalopolis.
