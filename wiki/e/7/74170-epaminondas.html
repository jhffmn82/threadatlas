Epaminondas in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Epaminondas is a Theban philosopher and military commander, esteemed by his people and praised among the Grecians for learning, courage, justice, patience, frugality, contempt for wealth, and freedom from covetousness. He studied the liberal sciences, especially Pythagorean philosophy, learning from Lysis. After arguing for the Thebans before the common Greek assembly against a peace decree, he was appointed general and entrusted with the whole war. Against the Lacedaemonians he used an oblique phalanx, concentrating his strongest soldiers on his own wing, and helped win at Leuctra. He then led Boeotian and allied campaigns in the Peloponnesus and Laconia, withdrew from a costly attack on Sparta, urged the rebuilding of Messene, and fought near Corinth. Though once demoted to private soldier on suspicion of treason, he was chosen to lead a desperate retreat from Thessaly, protected the army with light troops and cavalry, repulsed Alexander’s forces, and brought it safely off. He advocated Theban dominion of the sea and brought Rhodes, Chios, and Byzantium into Theban obedience. Finally, commanding against Mantinea, he charged the Lacedaemonians, killed their general, broke their division, received a mortal breast wound, and died after learning his side had won.

Narrative, section by section

8 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. IV.

Epaminondas argued on behalf of the Thebans before the common assembly of the Greeks and advised them to oppose the peace decree. He is described as an exceptionally capable commander, learned in the liberal sciences and especially in Pythagorean philosophy.

CHAP. VL

Epaminondas was appointed general of the Theban army and commanded the war against the Lacedaemonians. He used an oblique phalanx, placing the strongest troops in his own wing, and his strategy helped secure the victory at Leuctra.

CHAP. vn.

He commanded the Boeotian invasion of the Peloponnesus with Pelopidas and led the allied army into Laconia. He attacked Sparta, withdrew when the assault became costly, persuaded the allies to rebuild Messene, and later broke through the wall and trench near Corinth before attacking Corinth.

CHAP. VIIL

Epaminondas was serving as a private soldier when the Boeotian army chose him as its general during a desperate retreat in Thessaly. He placed light-armed troops and cavalry in the rear, repulsed Alexander’s forces, and safely brought off the army. He later invaded the Peloponnesus and helped free Pelopidas.

CHAP. IX,

Epaminondas persuaded the Thebans to seek dominion of the sea, arguing that their land power would make maritime supremacy attainable. He was sent with forces to Rhodes, Chios, and Byzantium, frightened the Athenian general Laches into withdrawing, and brought those cities under Theban obedience before later being killed at Mantinea.

CHAP. X.

Epaminondas commanded the Boeotian forces, attempted to surprise Sparta, and later marched against Mantinea. At the battle of Mantinea he personally charged the Lacedaemonians, killed their general, and broke their division, but he received a mortal wound and died after learning that his side had won. The text praises his military skill, courage, justice, contempt for wealth, and other virtues.

3G. When Thericles was chief magistrate at Athens in the sixty^

Epaminondas learned from Lysis and from the sparks of knowledge gained through Pythagorean philosophy. He consequently surpassed the Thebans and other Greeks of his time in patience, frugality, and other virtues.

BOOK XIV.

Epaminondas led the Thebans at Leuctra and in invasions of the Peloponnesus, including an assault on Corinth. He brought Rhodes, Chios, and Byzantium over to the Thebans and was killed at Mantinea; the text commends him.

Epaminondas in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Epaminondas, son of Cleommis, restores the Messenians’ country and cities after the Theban victory at Leuctra, prompted by a dream. He consults Epiteles, recovers the Messenian Mysteries, chooses and builds Messene as a base against the Lacedæmonians, and secures Aristomenes’ shield before Leuctra. He is later honored with statues at Messene.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIV.

Epaminondas decided to restore the Messenians after the Theban victory at Leuctra and received a dream urging him to give them back their country and cities. He consulted Epiteles about the dream, directed the recovery of the Messenian Mysteries, selected the site for the new capital, and organized the building of Messene.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Epaminondas, son of Cleommis, had a statue in the temple of Æsculapius at Messene and an iron effigy made by another artist. The Thebans also honored Aristomenes before the battle of Leuctra, following the oracle of Trophonius, and Epaminondas persuaded Xenocrates to send for Aristomenes' shield.

Epaminondas in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Epaminondas was a Greek general and Theban leader, son of Polymnis, of illustrious descent though raised in poverty. He studied national education carefully and, in youth, was a pupil of Lysis the Tarentine. He was especially mild and completely self-controlled, and later served as Philopœmen’s model in character and military achievement. He rescued Pelopidas, confronted the Lacedæmonians during an embassy, vowed vengeance for the daughters of Scedasus, and led the decision to fight at Leuctra. After victory, he exposed the Lacedæmonians’ losses, expelled their inland garrisons and governors, put down decemvirates, and aided the recovery and freedom of Greece. He supported the Arcadians, restored Mantinea, stirred the colonization of Megalopolis, and sent 1,000 picked Thebans to defend it; he also turned to Messene’s colonization and was credited with restoring Messene. As Bœotarch, including beyond the lawful term, he marched against Sparta, routed Iphicrates near Lechæum, and was acquitted for exceeding his term. He commanded near Pylæ and defeated Lacedæmonians, Achæans, and Athenians. Mortally wounded at Mantinea in victory, he watched the battle while holding his wound, then died and was buried on the field, commemorated by pillars and a dragon-bearing shield.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XI.

Epaminondas was mortally wounded at the battle at Mantinea and was carried alive from the fighting. He watched the battle while holding his wound, then died after the battle and was buried on the field, where a pillar, a shield bearing a dragon, and two other pillars commemorated him; he is praised as a Greek general of exceptional talent who raised the Thebans to a foremost position.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Epaminondas the Theban was regarded as the founder of Megalopolis because he stirred the Arcadians to colonize the city. He sent 1,000 picked Thebans under Parmenes to defend the Arcadians if the Lacedæmonians tried to stop the colonization.

CHAPTER XLIX.

Epaminondas was Philopœmen’s model in character and military achievements. He is described as especially mild and as having complete control over his temper, qualities Philopœmen did not fully attain.

CHAPTER LII.

Epaminondas, son of Polymnis, helped recover Greece when it was in a pitiful state. He expelled Lacedæmonian garrisons and governors inland, put down the decemvirates, and added Messene and Megalopolis to Greece's importance.

CHAPTER XIII.

Epaminondas was of illustrious descent but grew up in poverty and studied under Lysis the Tarentine, a pupil of Pythagoras. He once risked his life to rescue Pelopidas, challenged the Lacedæmonians during an embassy to Sparta, and led the decision to fight at Leuctra. After the victory, he required the Lacedæmonians’ allies to bury their dead first so that their losses would be revealed.

CHAPTER XIV.

Epaminondas released the other Peloponnesians after the battle but kept the Lacedæmonians shut up at Leuctra until he heard that the Spartans were coming to relieve them. He captured Ceressus, supported the Arcadians in the Peloponnese, restored Mantinea, established Megalopolis, continued as Bœotarch beyond the lawful term, marched against Sparta, and turned to the colonization of Messene. He later routed Iphicrates and his forces near Lechæum, pursued them toward Athens, and was acquitted for continuing as Bœotarch.

CHAPTER XV.

Epaminondas took command of the Theban force near Pylæ, causing Alexander to release Pelopidas. As Bœotarch, he defeated the Lacedæmonians, Achæans, and Athenians, spared Bœotian deserters at Phœbia by stigmatizing them, and was killed by an Athenian at Mantinea in the moment of victory. He was credited with restoring Messene and helping the Greeks gain freedom.
