Demetrius in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Demetrius, son of Antigonus, is a Macedonian military commander, admiral, king, and renowned besieger known as Poliorcetes for skill and violence in sieges. Young when Antigonus left him in Syria to oppose Ptolemy, he commanded mixed forces with elephants, infantry, cavalry, Macedonians, Lycians, and Pamphylians. Near Gaza he chose battle despite advice against facing the larger force of Ptolemy and Seleucus; his army was defeated after the loss of elephants and flight of cavalry, and he retreated with heavy losses. He subsequently recovered by surprising Celles, campaigned against the Nabataeans, and led an expedition toward Babylon. Acting on Antigonus's orders to liberate Greek cities, Demetrius sailed from Ephesos, took the Piraeus and Munychia, and restored Athens to liberty. In Cyprus he defeated Menelaus, besieged Salamis, routed Ptolemy's fleet, and, after the victory, assumed the title of king. He also commanded Antigonus's fleet in the Egyptian campaign. His greatest sustained enterprise was the siege of Rhodes. Seeking control of its harbour to stop supplies, he brought elaborate engines: Testudoes, four-story siege towers, a floating rampart, fortified boats, and archers and specialists. Storms, resistance, setbacks, and the surrender of four hundred of his men prevented success. Ordered by his father to make peace, he ratified an agreement with the Rhodians. He then returned to Greece, allied with the Aetolians, freed Chalcidians from a garrison, captured Sicyon and Corinth, restored liberty while retaining Corinth's citadel garrison, and acted in Achaia, Thessaly, and nearby cities. He made conditional peace with Cassander when recalled to Asia, recovered Ephesus, and fortified the mouth of Pontus. After Antigonus was killed, he fled to Cyprus with Stratonice and wealth. Later records portray him killing Alexander and Antipater, suppressing Boeotian rebels while pardoning most, and finally being imprisoned at Pella; Seleucus refused a proposal to kill him and considered restoring him to his kingdom. In war he is described as active and serious, while in peace as given to drunkenness and banqueting.

Demetrius leads 4,000 light-armed infantry and 4,000 other troops against the Nabataeans, but withdraws after failing to capture their rock and accepts gifts and hostages. He later leads an expedition toward Babylon, takes one fort, leaves Archelaus to besiege another, and returns to Lesser Asia.

CHAP. ni.

Demetrius sailed from Ephesos with armies by land and sea and orders from Antigonus to free the Grecian cities, beginning with Athens. He captured the Piraeus and Munychia, restored Athens to liberty, and later captured cities in Cyprus, defeated Menelaus, besieged Salamis, and routed Ptolemy's fleet. After taking Cyprus, he sent his father news of the victory and assumed the title of king.

Demetrius commanded Antigonus’s fleet during the Egyptian campaign and later led a large expedition against Rhodes. He suffered losses in storms near Egypt and then landed on Rhodes, fortified his camp, devastated the surrounding country, and began preparations for a siege.

Demetrius besieged Rhodes and sought chiefly to seize its harbour and cut off provisions. He constructed Testudoes, towers, a floating rampart, fortified water-boats, and other engines, repeatedly attacked the harbour and walls, and repaired his machines and ships after setbacks. Storms, Rhodian resistance, and the surrender of four hundred of his men prevented him from securing the city.

Demetrius was initiated into the sacred mysteries at Athens before the legally appointed day. He moved his army into Thessaly, took Larissa's citadel by force, captured Prona and Pteleum, and entered Pherae, where he took the citadel and restored the inhabitants' liberties. On Antigonus's command, he made a conditional peace with Cassander, sailed to Asia, recovered Ephesus, subdued cities near the Hellespont, and fortified the mouth of Pontus.

BOOK XXI,

Demetrius, the son of Antigonus, fled with Stratonice and their wealth to Cyprus after Antigonus was killed. He later killed Alexander, the brother of Antipater, and also killed Antipater, who had murdered his own mother. Demetrius received Agathocles's son kindly, gave him royal gifts, and sent Oxythemes with him as a spy.

After forcing the remaining rebels into submission, King Demetrius pardoned all but fourteen leading Boeotian insurgents. While Demetrius was imprisoned at Pella, Lysimachus urged Seleucus to kill him, but Seleucus rejected the proposal and considered releasing him and restoring him to his kingdom.

BOOK XVII.

Antigonus left him in Syria to watch Ptolemy. He defeated Ptolemy’s general at Gaza, took Tyre, and was later sent against Seleucus in Babylon.

BOOK XX.

Demetrius defeats the Greeks, takes the Piraeus, and acts in Cyprus and Greece. He fights Ptolemy at sea, besieges Rhodes, later leaves Greece for Asia, and makes peace with Cassander.

Demetrius in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Demetrius, son of Antigonus, was a boy in years but a man in intellect, a military commander and ruler who deposed an Athenian ruler, fortified Museum, governed Syria and Phœnicia, won victories in Cyprus, and besieged Rhodes unsuccessfully. After succeeding Antigonus, he faced Lysimachus, crossed into Asia Minor, and was captured by Seleucus.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

Demetrius, the son of Antigonus, was placed in control of Syria and Phœnicia and defeated some Egyptian forces after Ptolemy’s return. He later defeated Menelaus at Cyprus, helped blockade Ptolemy in Egypt, and besieged Rhodes with a large army and fleet, but did not succeed.

CHAPTER VII.

Demetrius I Poliorcetes succeeded Antigonus I and caused Lysimachus to anticipate war. Lysimachus fought him at Amphipolis, while Pyrrhus aided Lysimachus; Demetrius later crossed into Asia Minor and was captured by Seleucus.
