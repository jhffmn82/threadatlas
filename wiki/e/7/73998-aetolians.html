Aetolians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Aetolians were a people of Aetolia described as warrior people, with ten thousand brave and sprightly men and mountain strongholds. They opposed Alexander’s recall of exiles, expecting to punish Oenians expelled from Aetolia, then supplied Leosthenes with seven thousand soldiers and joined the Greek confederacy before public concerns drew them home. Resisting Antipater and Craterus, they held difficult passes and fortified cities, repelled initial attacks, but winter hardship led to a temporary peace after Antigonos disclosed Perdiccas’s plans. They later campaigned in Thessaly under Alexander, besieging Amphissa, ravaging Locrian territory, defeating Polycles, and then withdrawing forces when Acarnanians invaded Aetolia. They supported Antigonus against Cassander, fought Acarnanians, and killed surrendering people at Agrinium despite safe conduct. After Philip defeated their allies and killed Aecidas, they fled with their families to mountain strongholds. Finally, their peace envoys faced Rome’s demand to surrender their country or pay one thousand talents; appeals based on services to Rome failed.

Ætolians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Ætolians were a group inhabiting Pleuron, active in war, alliances, commemoration, and later migration. They sought release from the Achæan League, aided the men of Elis through kinship, protected Heraclea, and suborned an assassin against Nabis. After subduing the Acarnanians, they displayed effigies of their generals, Apollo, and Artemis; they also dedicated shields, a trophy, and a statue of Ætolia from a fine imposed on the Galati. Harassed by Philip’s raids, they received nominal Roman help but were later opposed by Romans and Achæans. Their most prominent action was resisting the Galati: people of every age and women joined, harrying the retreat with missiles, attacking Acichorius’ troops, and helping prevent most Galati from returning. After Augustus removed many to Nicopolis, many migrated to Amphissa.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

The Ætolians were among those especially harassed by Philip's raids. Roman forces were nominally sent to assist them against Philip and were later ordered to protect them.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Ætolians were opposed by the Romans and the Achæans. The Achæans fought against them partly to satisfy a long-standing grudge.

CHAPTER XXII.

The Ætolians left Thermopylæ rapidly after hearing of the disaster at Callion and gathered forces from their towns. Young men, old men, and women joined the army, and they attacked the retreating Galati with missiles along the roads. Their retaliation helped ensure that fewer than half of the 40,800 Galati returned safely to Thermopylæ.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Ætolians initially sent only a few men, but Philomelus later brought 1,200, while the flower of their army attacked Acichorius’s division. They repeatedly harried the retreating barbarians with javelins and other missiles and attacked them all the way to the river Sperchius.
