Amphictyons in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Amphictyons are a religious and political court with an Amphictyonic senate or council. They issue decrees, condemn the Phocians and Lacedaemonians to fines, and order Phocian land confiscated and devoted to the deity if payment fails; the Greek approval of this decree prompts Phocian resistance. They then decree war on the Phocians for sacrilege and violation of Apollo's temple treasures, while threatening death for mercenaries who aid them. After Phocian surrender, the council imposes penalties and restores the oracle and religious peace. Later, at Pylene, it agrees to make Alexander generalissimo of Greece.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. VI.

The Amphictyons condemned the Phocians and Lacedaemonians to pay fines, and they ordered that the Phocian lands be confiscated and devoted to the deity if payment was not made. Their decree was approved by the Greeks and became the immediate cause of the Phocian resistance.

CHAP. VII.

The Amphictyons decreed war against the Phocians for sacrilege and violation of Apollo's temple treasures. They also condemned the Lacedaemonians to heavy fines and declared that mercenaries who fought with the Phocians should be put to death.

CHAP. X.

After the Phocians surrendered, Philip, the Thessalians, and the Boeotians arranged for the Amphictyonic Council to assemble and decide the outstanding matters. The council later imposed penalties on the Phocians and made provisions for restoring the oracle and religious peace.

BOOK XVII.

Alexander appeared before the Amphictyonic Council at Pylene and managed affairs so that all present agreed to make him generalissimo of Greece.

Amphictyones in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Amphictyones were delegates from neighboring peoples who assembled at Delphi as the general Council of the Greeks; over time their name prevailed over Amphictiones. They built the existing temple at Delphi from sacred money and employed the Corinthian Spintharus as architect. They shaped the Pythian contests, beginning with prizes for harping and adding pipes and singing to the pipes, then later athletic, chariot, and further racing events; singing to the pipes was discontinued, and the contests became crown competitions in the second Pythiad. They also made or offered statues of Scyllis of Scione and Hydna at Delphi. In war, they resolved to attack Cirrha for impiety toward Apollo, chose Clisthenes as leader, sought Solon’s counsel, consecrated Cirrha’s land to Apollo, captured the town, and punished its inhabitants.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

The Amphictyones built the temple that still exists at Delphi using sacred money. Its architect was the Corinthian Spintharus.

CHAPTER VII.

In the third year of the 48th Olympiad, the Amphictyones established prizes for harping and added contests for pipes and singing to the pipes. They later introduced athletic contests, a chariot race, harping without vocal accompaniment, and additional racing events, while changing the Pythian contest from prize competitions to contests for a crown in the second Pythiad.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Amphictyones were originally delegates from neighboring peoples who assembled at Delphi, although the name Amphictyones eventually prevailed over Amphictiones. In the narrator’s time they numbered thirty members representing several Greek peoples and cities.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

The Amphictyones resolved to wage war against the people of Cirrha because of their impiety toward Apollo. They chose Clisthenes as leader, sought Solon’s counsel, consecrated the land around Cirrha to Apollo, captured the town, and took vengeance on its inhabitants.

Amphictyonic Council in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Amphictyonic Council, held at Thermopylæ and Delphi, punished temple plunderers and fined the Phocians. After the Sacred War it admitted Macedonians, gave them the Phocian votes, and excluded Phocians and Lacedæmonians; the Phocians were later restored for bravery against Brennus. Augustus transferred several votes to Nicopolis. In the author’s time it had thirty members, with Athens, Delphi, and Nicopolis represented at every meeting.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II.

The Amphictyonic Council imposed a fine on the Phocians for reasons the author could not ascertain. After the seizure of the temple at Delphi, it was said to have imposed the same death suffered by Philomelus on all who had plundered the temple.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Amphictyonic Council changed after the Sacred War, when the Macedonians were admitted and the Phocians and Lacedæmonians were excluded; the Phocians were later restored after their bravery against Brennus. Augustus transferred several votes to Nicopolis, and in the narrator’s time the Council had thirty members, with Athens, Delphi, and Nicopolis sending delegates to every meeting.
