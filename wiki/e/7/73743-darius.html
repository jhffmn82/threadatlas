Darius in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Darius is a Persian king, son of Arsanes and regarded as the most valiant among the Persians for courage and single combat. He became king of Persia after the royal family was extinguished, ruled Asia, and faced Alexander’s rise by raising forces and preparing for war. After Memnon’s death, he personally commanded his army, rejected Charidemus’s advice, and had him executed. He marched toward Cilicia with a vast force and his mother Sisygambis, wife, son, and two daughters, but was defeated at Issus and fled; his family was lost to him in the battle. At Babylon, he rallied surviving troops, recalled forces, sent Alexander letters offering ransom and part of Asia, and, when agreement failed, prepared again for war. He marched from Babylon with foot soldiers, horsemen, and hooked chariots, made further peace offers, and fought from his chariot near Arbela. When Alexander’s attack threw his troops into panic, he fled again. He retreated toward the upper provinces and Ecbatana, sought to raise another army, withdrew through Bactria, and was treacherously murdered there by Bessus. Alexander later found Darius’s body and had it honorably interred.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP, XV.

The Persian king was the target of Philip's planned expedition. Philip asked the Delphic oracle whether he would be victorious over him and interpreted the answer as saying that he would lead the Persian king away as a sacrificial victim.

BOOK XVII.

Darius was advanced to the throne of Persia after Bagoas had extinguished the royal line. He was praised as the most valiant of the Persians and, after learning of Alexander's rise, raised forces and prepared a fleet for war.

CHAP. III.

After Memnon's death, Darius consulted his friends, rejected Charidemus's advice, and ordered Charidemus's execution. He gathered an army of four hundred thousand foot and one hundred thousand horse, marched toward Cilicia with his family, and was defeated at Issus, where he fled when his army broke.

But to return to the kings themselves : Darius with all his army

After his army was routed, Darius fled rapidly, changing from one of his best horses to another in an effort to escape Alexander and reach the upper provinces. He remained alive, and Alexander assured the captives that he would respect their former dignity.

As for Darius, being now got to Babylon, he mustered up his

Darius reached Babylon and mustered the broken troops that had escaped from the battle at Issus. Though he had suffered a great overthrow, he was not discouraged. He wrote to Alexander offering ransom money for the captives and part of Asia, then prepared extensively for war when the letters failed.

CHAP. IV.

The Tyrians resisted Alexander partly because they expected Darius to reward their loyalty and believed their resistance would give him time to recruit his army. Other survivors of the battle of Issus continued to support the Persian interest.

As soon as Darius had intelligence of his coming, he got all his

Darius gathered his forces, lengthened his swords and lances, prepared two hundred hooked chariots, and marched from Babylon with a very large army. He offered Alexander increasingly favorable peace terms, but after Alexander rejected them, he trained his soldiers and commanded the defense of the Tigris. During the battle, he fought from his chariot but fled after Alexander's attack caused his troops to believe he had been killed.

CHAP. VU.

After being routed at Arbela, Darius fled toward the higher provinces and attempted to raise another army. He later withdrew from Bactria with Persians and mercenary Greeks, but was treacherously murdered by Bessus. Alexander found his body and had it honorably interred.

CHAP. VIII.

Darius had appointed Bessus lord-lieutenant of Bactria. After Darius's death, Bessus and other supporters escaped Alexander and continued resistance against him.

BOOK XVII.

He prepared armies against Alexander, was defeated at Issus, and lost his mother, wife, two daughters, and son. He sent letters and offers to Alexander and was later murdered.
