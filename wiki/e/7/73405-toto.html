Abstract written by a model

Toto is a very good, fresh and cheery young boy, a child of the family and the blind grandmother's boy and companion. He helps his grandmother with longstanding household work, is proud of her, and loves the forest deeply. With curly hair, honest gray eyes, ruddy-brown cheeks, marbles in his pockets, a whistle, and an old fiddle inherited from his grandfather, he is a merry whistler and capable fiddler. He also inherited a porringer from his great-grandfather, owns the Black Spanish chickens, and has known the cow-and-moon rhyme since infancy. In winter, Toto comes home with Bruin carrying milk, after splitting kindling, building a snow fort, and snowballing with him; he helps make supper and listens to Granny's story. He is imaginative and eager for stories, wishing he had been Chop-Chin, requesting a fairy story from the hermit, and later telling The Naughty Comet after identifying a comet but admitting he does not know exactly what comets are. His friendship with Bruin began in childhood after Toto fought a wild-cat at seven and then met Bruin and the raccoon. He is hospitable to the raccoon, offering his bed as a foot-warmer, lending him a red cravat, and later reassuring Mr. Woodmouse, Senior, that the raccoon means no harm. Toto joins Cracker and the raccoon at the woodmouse family wedding, where he provides lively fiddle music, dances with the raccoon, and receives checkerberries, nuts, and messages of regret and apology to Bruin. He conducts a dancing-school lesson, plays and demonstrates the steps, and joins other festivities. He also proves helpful and brave: traveling with Bruin to Baldhead's cave, entering first, bringing water and jelly, and feeding Baldhead until he recovers. He returns home with Jim Crow, praises <DW53>, Miss Mary, and Cracker, and later visits the hermit with the crow and food for Bruin. He recognizes Ger-Falcon as the hawk that tried to take his chickens. At the cottage party he gathers eggs, decorates, plays for the birds' quadrille, and games with the others. Though saddened that spring may separate him from forest life and distressed when Bruin seems to leave without farewell, he finds Bruin in the wood-shed. Bruin stays, and Toto continues visiting the forest and sharing its happenings with his grandmother.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

11 sections; each heading opens the text

Toto's Merry Winter / CHAPTER I.

Toto enters the cottage with Bruin, carrying a pail of milk, and helps prepare the evening meal. He has spent the afternoon splitting kindling-wood, building a snow fort, and snowballing Bruin. He invites his grandmother to supper and joins the others for her story.

CHAPTER III.

Toto says he would like to have been Chop-Chin and is excited by the thought of speaking boldly to the Emperor. He asks the raccoon to sleep on his bed because the raccoon makes a good foot-warmer.

CHAPTER IV.

Toto questions <DW53> about his trouble and joins him on a winter walk. He recalls fighting the wild-cat in a hollow tree when he was seven, then meeting Bruin and <DW53>, who invited him to supper and became his friends. He agrees to attend the woodmouse family wedding and provide the music.

CHAPTER V.

Toto conducts the dancing-school lesson, plays his grandfather's old fiddle, and demonstrates the steps when Bruin misunderstands the command to jump and change feet. He warns Bruin about the custard pie, but becomes dizzy and cannot remember at first whether he put it out to cool.

" CHAPTER VI.

Toto travels with Cracker and the raccoon to attend the wedding. He reassures Mr. Woodmouse, Senior, that the raccoon no longer intends harm toward the woodmouse family, plays lively music for the dancing, and dances a waltz with the raccoon.

CHAPTER VII.

Toto identifies the object in the sky as a comet and explains that comets are heavenly bodies with tails, though he admits he does not know exactly what they are. He tells <DW53> and Cracker the story of The Naughty Comet after <DW53> mocks his explanation.

CHAPTER VIII.

Toto agrees to carry aid to Baldhead and travels with Bruin to the cave. He enters first, gives Baldhead water and jelly, and feeds him until he regains strength. Toto tries to explain that Bruin is kind despite his frightening appearance, but Bruin's voice frightens Baldhead into fainting; Toto later joins the others in welcoming and laughing with him.

CHAPTER X.

Toto returns home at night with Jim Crow as a guest and happily praises <DW53> and Miss Mary for defending the cottage against the attempted robber. He tells the family part of his and Bruin's adventures, interrupts Cracker's story several times, and then praises Cracker for telling a good story.

_" CHAPTER XII.

Toto visits the hermit's cave with the crow and a basket containing gingerbread, eggs, apples, and butter-milk for Bruin. He identifies Ger-Falcon as the hawk who tried to carry off his Black Spanish chickens and joins the others in asking the hermit for a story.

CHAPTER XIV.

Toto collects eggs for the party’s custard and helps decorate the cottage, polish the tankards, and prepare for the guests. He plays his fiddle for the birds’ quadrille and joins in the games, but his face falls when the old hermit says that spring is near.

CHAPTER XV.

Toto prepares to return to the forest with his friends, though he says he will miss the blind grandmother and is also eager for forest life. He is upset when he thinks Bruin has left without saying farewell, then finds him in the wood-shed and brings him back to the cottage. Toto remains happy when Bruin decides never to leave, and he continues visiting the forest and sharing its happenings with the grandmother.
