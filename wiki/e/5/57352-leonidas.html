Leonidas in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Leonidas is a Spartan and Lacedaemonian king and general commanding the Greek force at Thermopylae. Seeking immortal honour for himself and his Spartans, he leads a small, determined army, including Thespians, that repeatedly defeats Persian assaults. When he learns the Persians are approaching from the rear, he holds a council, orders the other forces to depart, and remains with the Lacedaemonians and Thespians. He leads a night attack, is destroyed after encirclement at daybreak, and exemplifies undying valour to posterity.

Leonidas is identified as the Spartan king and is singled out in Simonides' poem. He is presented as having shown posterity that real valour cannot die.

Leonidas in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Leonidas is presented as a king of Sparta and of the Lacedæmonians, succeeding Cleomenes because he was the only surviving male of Eurysthenes’ family. He commanded 300 Lacedæmonians at Thermopylæ and died resisting Xerxes’ invasion. Charges by Lysander and Cleombrotus led to his deposition and exile in Arcadia, after which he was recalled to kingship. Forty years after his death, Pausanias moved his remains to Sparta, where his tomb hosts annual Spartan speeches and a contest.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Leonidas succeeded Cleomenes because Cleomenes had no male children. During his reign he led 300 Lacedæmonians at Thermopylæ and died resisting Xerxes’ invasion.

CHAPTER VI.

Leonidas became king because he was the only surviving male of the family of Eurysthenes. Lysander and Cleombrotus brought charges against him, leading to his deposition, but after going into exile in Arcadia he was recalled and made king a second time.

CHAPTER XIV.

Leonidas has a tomb in Sparta opposite the theatre, where speeches and a contest restricted to Spartans are held every year. His remains were removed from Thermopylæ by Pausanias 40 years after his death, and a pillar records the names and pedigrees of those who fought against the Medes at Thermopylæ.
