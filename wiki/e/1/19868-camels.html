Abstract written by a model

Camels were scarce in Bedouin districts because a disorder caused great mortality, yet the Bey of Tunis possessed many and farmed them through overseers for mills and agricultural work; others were acquired through tribal fines for manslaughter. They were valued for crossing vast plains, though they could not supersede mules in Mediterranean islands. An expedition and camp had about two thousand camels, which carried no provender and lived on coarse grass, weeds, and thorns, especially sharp prickly thorns. Some bore women in enclosed palanquins, with bells and rigging that earned them the name “ship of the Desert.” On the return to Tunis, clayey ground prevented many from reaching camp, and 550 died. Prices ranged from 60 to 200 piastres.

Rode to Kairwan.

The Bey of Tunis possessed many camels and farmed them out through overseers for mills and agricultural work. Camels were also acquired through tribal fines for manslaughter and were considered useful mainly for crossing vast plains, while mules were thought better for the Mediterranean islands.

The expedition and camp had about two thousand camels, which carried no supplied provender and survived on coarse grass, weeds, and thorns. The camels were especially fond of sharp prickly thorns despite their apparently tender mouths. Some women traveled in enclosed palanquins mounted on camels, whose bells and rigging made them resemble ships of the Desert.
