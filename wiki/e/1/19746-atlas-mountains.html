Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Mount Atlas is the greatest mountain in the region bordering the Tritonis morass and extending to the ocean, whose inhabitants were attacked by the Amazons. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Atlas’s mountain form supports the whole heaven, with woods, ridges, stones, and a star-filled summit. In Travels in Morocco, the Atlas Mountains are Morocco’s principal mountain chain, shaping its climate, rivers, forests, minerals, water supply, valleys, routes, regions, and settlements.

Atlas Mountains in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860 · the work’s page · read it where Atlas Mountains first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Atlas Mountains are the principal heights of the Barbary region and Morocco’s central great mountain chain, called the Deren by natives and Dyris or Derem by ancient and modern aborigines. They extend across Morocco from northeast to southwest, from the Algerian frontier toward Cape Gheer, while records also place their broader span from Tripoli to Santa Cruz and from Cape Noun toward the Great Syrte. Their highest point is Miltsin, more than 15,000 feet above sea level; their greatest heights are in Morocco, especially east of Morocco and southeast of Fez, before diminishing eastward through Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli. The range shapes Morocco’s climate, rivers, forests, minerals, water supply, fertile valleys, and routes south. It separates northern regions from the southern quasi-Saharan region and contains Berber settlements, including quasi-independent tribes beyond much of the Sultan’s authority. Its snowy ridges shelter Shelouh Jews and frame towns, districts, and Tafilett.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

The Atlas Mountains shelter the main population of Shelouh Jews, especially around Jebel Melge, described as a vast ridge capped with eternal snows. The mountain setting is associated with their physical strength, isolation, independence, and freedom.

CHAPTER III.

The Atlas contains quasi-independent Berber tribes that oppose both the Arabs and the Moorish government. Its eastern and mountainous regions limit the Sultan’s authority, and some inhabitants of its snowy mountains are reported to have European-looking customs.

The nature of the soil and climate of Morocco are not unlike those of Spain and

The Atlas Mountains traverse Morocco from northeast to southwest, and their highest stated point, Miltsin, rises more than 15,000 feet above sea level. The mountains influence Morocco’s climate, produce numerous rivers, and contain forests, mineral resources, and the highest peaks of the Moroccan portion of the range.

CHAPTER IV.

The Atlas is presented as Morocco’s central great mountain chain, called the Deren by the natives, extending from the Algerian frontier to Cape Gheer. It separates the northern regions from the southern quasi-Saharan region and includes or borders districts such as Fez, Morocco, Todla, Dukala, Abda, Haha, and Sous.

CHAPTER V.

The Atlas lies near Morocco, whose city spreads across a plain at its foot. The Atlas supplies water through streams and aqueducts and forms the geographical setting for Tafilett on its south-eastern side.

CHAPTER VI.

The Atlas range is associated with picturesque mountain scenery, Berber settlements, fertile valleys, and routes toward the southern provinces. Several towns, including Tefza, Pitideb, Ghuer, and Tednest, are situated on or near its mountains.

notes
