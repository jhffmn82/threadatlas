Abstract written by a model

Mogador is a Moroccan coastal port, with an isle, water-port, consulates, merchants, a Governor, and a European Jewish community. It is governed by Moorish authority and hosts European merchants, British consular authorities, European residents, and Jews whose social life increasingly overlaps with European society. Commerce is central, yet merchants are tightly controlled by the Moroccan government and at times barred from visiting vessels or supervising the stowing of their goods despite treaty provisions. It is also a setting for disputes involving Jewish merchants, London Jew-boys, the British Consul, and the Governor, and for anti-slavery tracts and a circular distributed among British merchants. Its gravest episode is the French bombardment of 13 August 1844: batteries were silenced, the Governor and most inhabitants fled, wild tribes sacked and burned the city, and the Jewish community endured massacre, plunder, homelessness, and starvation, leaving Mogador largely ruined and unoccupied.

CHAPTER I.

Mogador contains a European society in which European and native Jews increasingly mix with European merchants and attend social functions. Its Jewish community suffered massacres, destruction, plunder, homelessness, and starvation during the French bombardment.

Mogador is described as a place where European merchants, including Mr. Saferty, conducted business under the authority of the Moroccan government. The text says merchants there were sometimes prohibited from visiting vessels in or outside the port, despite a treaty provision appearing to permit it.

CHAPTER VII.

Mogador is the setting for disputes involving London Jew-boys, the British Consul, Jewish merchants, and the Governor. The narrator also distributed Arabic anti-slavery tracts and prepared an anti-slavery circular for British merchants there.

On our march back to Tunis, we encamped for two days by the foot of a range of h

Before leaving Mogador, the narrator formally took leave of the Governor and received civilities from Moorish authorities and European residents. Merchants in Mogador were closely controlled by the Moorish Government and could not even go aboard vessels to supervise the stowing of their goods.

APPENDIX.

Mogador was bombarded by the French on 13 August 1844, and its batteries were silenced after sustained fighting. The Governor and most inhabitants fled, after which wild tribes sacked and burned the city, leaving it largely ruined and unoccupied.
