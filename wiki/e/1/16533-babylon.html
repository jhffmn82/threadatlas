Babylon in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Babylon first appears

Abstract written by a model

Babylon is a wealthy place and province, marked by armies, government, welcomes, warnings, and repeated struggles for control. After the battle at Issus, Darius reached Babylon and mustered his broken troops. Alexander later came toward it after conquering the Cussaei. Though Chaldean astrologers warned him not to enter, he did so after first camping two hundred furlongs away; Babylon received him cheerfully, entertained the Macedonians splendidly, and supplied abundant provisions. He remained more than thirty days, appointed governors and a castle commander, distributed money, and ordered forces and transport from the city. He also visited its newly made harbor, where separation from the other ships left him in danger for several days before he returned safely. Alexander ultimately died there, had a funeral pile built there, and news of his death was carried from Babylon, helping prompt open war by the Athenians. In the succession settlement, Babylon’s province went to Archon, while it was later associated with Patrocles, Seleucus, Python, Eumenes, and the Argyraspides. Under Seleucus, Babylon became contested by Antigonus and Demetrius: Antigonus claimed its revenues, Seleucus asserted his grant from the Macedonians, and Seleucus recovered the place with a small army and popular support. He captured the castle held for Antigonus by Diphilus and regained the whole government. When Demetrius later arrived, the inhabitants had fled, and he besieged its forts, taking one and leaving Archelaus at another.

Babylon in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Babylon first appears

Abstract written by a model

Babylon is a city and province on the Euphrates. When Ninus invaded Babylonia, the later Babylon had not yet been built; the province instead held several considerable cities whose inhabitants he subdued and made tributary. Later Babylon became a great city, marked by massive walls, palaces, a temple, bridge, hanging garden, and a remarkable highway with an obelisk. It also had abundant brimstone, a dangerous sulphurous spring, and a nearby morass deadly to the unfamiliar. After the overthrow of the Assyrian empire, Belesis helped lead a revolt, received Babylon’s government without tribute, and brought Sardanapalus’s ashes and royal treasure there. Its opposite sea-coasts supported fishing and fertile fruit-growing country. Cyrus marched toward it after crossing the Euphrates, and Conon later reached it by sailing down that river and gained Artaxerxes’s support. In time, much of the city’s structures were consumed or damaged, leaving only a small inhabited part and turning much enclosed land to tillage and pasture.
