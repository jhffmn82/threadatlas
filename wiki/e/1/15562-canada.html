Canada in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916

Canada is a British colony that came under British rule after conquest by British arms in the Seven Years’ War. The conquest created a situation fraught with tragic possibilities, yet French and English Canadians generally lived together peacefully for a hundred and fifty years apart from the Rebellion in Canada. British constitutional arrangements did not treat the French inhabitants as a conquered people: they preserved French civil law, recognized Catholic religious obligations, divided the territory into Upper Canada and Lower Canada in 1791, and later permitted French and English in Lower Canadian political proceedings. During fears of enemy agents while Napoleon was powerful, Sir James Craig governed Canada; Sir George Prevost later pursued conciliation toward French Canadians. Severe conflict then developed among the Assembly, executive government, and British authorities over constitutional reform, revenues, French-English relations, and supplies. Rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada included attempted armed invasion by Swanton refugees and Canadian expeditions against rebels at St Eustache and St Benoit. Britain sent Durham to investigate; he arrived in Quebec in 1838, departed that year, and reported on causes and remedies. After the rebellion, Canada remained in the British Empire, in part through one party’s loyalty, gained responsible government under a new order, and saw former Patriote leaders enter public service and shape political development.

Canada received constitutional arrangements that preserved French civil law, recognized Catholic religious obligations, and divided the territory into Upper Canada and Lower Canada in 1791. French and English were subsequently used in the political proceedings of Lower Canada.

{13} CHAPTER III 'THE REIGN OF TERROR' During the session of 1805 the Assembly was confronted with the apparently innocent problem of building prisons.

Canada was governed by Sir James Craig during a period when Napoleon was at the height of his power and British authorities feared enemy agents might be active there. Craig’s successor, Sir George Prevost, adopted a conciliatory policy toward the French Canadians.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

Canada was experiencing severe political conflict between the Assembly, the executive government, and British authorities. The dispute involved constitutional reform, control of revenues, relations between French and English inhabitants, and the withholding of supplies.

{89} But the end of rebellion was not yet in sight.

The Swanton refugees attempted an armed invasion of Canada, expecting habitants to join them. Canadian forces later mounted an expedition against the rebels at St Eustache and St Benoit.

{104} CHAPTER X THE LORD HIGH COMMISSIONER The rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada profoundly affected public opinion in the mother country.

Canada was sent Durham to investigate after rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada exposed serious political grievances. Durham arrived in Quebec on May 28, 1838, left on November 1, 1838, and reported on the causes and remedies of the Canadian troubles.
