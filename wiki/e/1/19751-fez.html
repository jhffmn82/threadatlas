Fez

Abstract written by a model

Fez is an ancient city and, historically, an independent kingdom; it is also described as the capital of the realm of Fez, the western royal court, and a leading city of Morocco. Separated from Morocco by the river Bouragrag, the kingdom once extended eastward to the gates of Tlemsen and is identified in a modern division with the northern kingdom of Fez. The city’s chief importance lies in learning, religion, wealth, industry, and commerce: it has old and new sections, hundreds of mosques, schools, markets, El-Karoubin, the formerly celebrated University of Fez, and Al-Kaisseriah. Its caravans travel annually to Timbuctoo and Mecca, while its manufactures include woollen haiks, silk handkerchiefs, slippers, shoes, and red felt caps. Though once powerful, its walls and defenses are decayed. Its population is uncertain, estimated from about thirty thousand to eighty-eight thousand or more, with fifty thousand considered probable. During a succession conflict, Muley Suleiman was saluted as Emperor there. Its foundation and full early history remain disputed or unknown.

[5] In the Shereefian lineage of Muley Suleiman, copied for Ali Bey by the Emper

Fez was the place where Muley Suleiman was saluted as Emperor in the north during the succession conflict. The text also refers to the war party and diplomatic activity at the Court of Fez.

CHAPTER IV.

Fez was historically a powerful independent kingdom extending eastward to the gates of Tlemsen. In the modern geographical division it is identified with the northern kingdom of Fez and is listed among the districts of the central Atlas region.

CHAPTER V.

Fez is described as one of the leading cities of Morocco in learning, wealth, industry, commerce, and religious importance. It contains old and new sections, hundreds of mosques, schools, markets, caravans, and manufactures, but its walls and defenses are decayed and its inhabitants are characterized as fanatical. Its population is uncertain, with estimates ranging from about thirty thousand to eighty-eight thousand or more.

CHAPTER VI.

Fez is used as a point of reference for the distances of several towns, including Muley Driss, Dubdu, Taza, Oushda, and Aghla. The surrounding kingdom is described as containing important commercial and agricultural districts.

notes

Fez is described as the capital of the realm of that name and as the court of the king in the west. The notes give conflicting traditions about its foundation and suggest that its true history may remain unknown until its library is examined.
