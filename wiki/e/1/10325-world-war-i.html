the First World War in Dr. Elsie Inglis

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918 · the work’s page · read it where the First World War first appears

Abstract written by a model

The First World War is an international conflict and the setting for invasion, epidemic, retreat, bombardment, shortages, hospital evacuations, and dangers at sea. As the approaching storm arrives, it tests nations and people’s hearts, suspends acute political disputes, and redirects women’s organisations toward national service. Mobilisation creates urgent need for medical care for the wounded and sends Dr. Elsie Inglis and her unit to battle fronts to aid nations allied in arms. The war brings deserted villages, refugees, destroyed stations, burning fuel tanks and grain ricks, disrupted schooling, and severe hospital conditions, alongside the typhus epidemic and the retreat involving Serbia and the Serbian Army. It also creates opportunities for women to replace men in wartime work and helps open a new era of women’s emancipation. Its hazards continue through the unit’s return voyage, with North Sea and other wartime maritime dangers. While the war is still ongoing, Elsie Inglis is honored at her funeral as a Red Cross worker who served abroad, with military honors, Allied flags, and representatives of the nations connected with her work.

World War I in Dr. Elsie Inglis

Lady Frances Balfour · 1918 · the work’s page · read it where World War I first appears

Abstract written by a model

World War I, beginning in 1914, is an armed conflict involving the Allied armies, the Serbian army, and fighting against a common foe, including near the Serbian and Romanian fronts. It created the circumstances for Elsie Maud Inglis and the Scottish Women’s Hospitals to provide foreign medical service in France, Serbia, and Russia, demonstrating women’s work in hospitals, camps, and support for armies and populations. As fighting intensified, the Serbian 1st Division faced a major battle against General Mackensen; retreats, movements of refugees and soldiers, bombardment, air raids, shortages, and overcrowded hospitals placed severe pressure on medical services. The conflict shaped Russia, Serbia, and other European nations and forms the setting for service during 1915 and Inglis’s death in 1917.
