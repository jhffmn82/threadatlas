Sahara in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860 · the work’s page · read it where Sahara first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sahara is an immense sandy desert and a broader hot, dry region extending from the Atlantic toward Egypt, including the quasi-Saharan country south and southeast of the Atlas. It forms Morocco’s southern boundary, receives southeastern Moroccan slopes and streams that vanish in its sands, and begins beyond the Mulweeah in an extremely sterile tract. Its defining features are heat, drought, blowing sand, salt flats, sparse marshes, rocky hills, scattered elevations and mountain branches, and routes so difficult that hidden wells are essential. Yet it contains distant oases and settled districts: Touat, Agobli, Taoudeni, and the oasis cluster of Figheegh, with gardens, wells, agriculture, commerce, and mining. Morocco’s claimed sovereignty over some oases was little or not effectively exercised. Tatta and Akka gather caravans crossing the Great Desert. Travelers encounter the Sahara around the Jereed as an immeasurable ocean of sand; at Ghafsa, its sands begin to disappear and land becomes more fit for corn.

CHAPTER III.

The Sahara forms Morocco’s southern boundary and contains distant oases over which the Shereefs formerly claimed, and sometimes still nominally claim, sovereignty. The oases of Touat, Agobli, and Taoudeni are described as having little or no effective dependence on Morocco.

The Sahara receives the southeastern slopes and streams of Morocco, including waters that feed the Wad Draha and other rivers before disappearing into desert sands. Southern Morocco has more desert heat and less rain because of its proximity to the Sahara.

CHAPTER IV.

The Sahara is included in the quasi-Saharan region south of the Atlas, particularly in the area southeast of the mountain chain. The text treats the Sahara as a comparison for the dangers of travel in the Rif, stating that travel in the Rif is more dangerous than in the farthest wastes of the Sahara.

CHAPTER VI.

The Sahara begins beyond the Mulweeah, more than forty leagues from Tlemsen, and is described as extremely sterile, particularly in summer. Tatta and Akka serve as meeting points for caravans crossing the Great Desert.

Of the Maroquine Sahara, I have only space to mention the interesting cluster of

The Maroquine Sahara contains the oasis cluster of Figheegh, or Figuiq. Its settlements are described as a Saharan district with gardens, wells, agriculture, commerce, and mining.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Sahara is described as the hot, dry country lying near the Great Desert. El-Jereed is identified as the Tunisian Sahara, while most of the broader region between the Atlantic and Egypt is known among Arabs as the Sahara or Sahra.

CHAPTER X.

The Sahara appears to the travelers as an immeasurable ocean of sand extending around the Jereed. They observe its heat, blowing sand, salt flats, sparse marshes, rocky hills, and difficult routes where knowledge of hidden wells is essential.

notes

The Sahara is described as containing elevations and secondary branches of the mountain system, including branches in southern Tripoli and Libya. Ghafsa is identified as the gate of the Tunisian Sahara, where the sands begin to disappear and the land becomes more suitable for growing corn.
