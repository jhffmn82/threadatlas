Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar: is_cursed_by Tantalos-stone [as an intolerable curse] Hellas had been under an intolerable terror compared with a Tantalos-stone hanging above its head. In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Hellas is the western land whose people defeat Xerxes's invading Persians at Salamis and Plataea. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Greece is the homeland and common land whose people wage war against Troy, and to which Iphigenia and Orestes seek to return. In Hecuba and other plays, Greece sends warriors and ships against Troy, suffers war losses, and serves as the homeland and intended return destination of Greek warriors and several characters. In Iphigenia in Tauris: Greece is a homeland and desired destination: Iphigenia was taken from it and longs to return, while Orestes recounts Greek leaders’ suffering after Troy. In The Argonautica, Hellas is Jason's homeland and the origin and intended destination of the Argonauts and their golden fleece. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Greece is a contested collection of cities whose liberties, governments, wars, and relations with Lacedaemon, Athens, Philip, Alexander, and their successors are described. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Greece is threatened by Persian and Carthaginian attacks, preserves its liberty through Greek victories, prospers, and later suffers conflicts among Athens, Sparta, and their allies.

In The Library, Greece suffers infertility because Pelops killed Stymphalus, and the infertility ends after Aeacus prays on its behalf. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Greece receives the worship of Isis, seeks Egyptian corn during famine, develops agricultural practices and mysteries, and supplies youths for Jason's expedition. In The Library: is_flooded_by the Flood [the greater part] Zeus floods most of Greece with heavy rain, destroying its inhabitants except for those who escape to nearby high mountains. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Greece is invaded, gains and loses freedom, becomes subject to Rome, and is ultimately described as extremely weak and later overrun by the Costoboci. In Musical Instruments: Greece is described as a center in which instruments from Asia and other regions were used and adapted. Hellas in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · 471 BC · the work’s page · read it where Hellas first appears

Abstract written by a model

Hellas is a western land of ancient memory, myth, poetry, and enduring spirit, figured as a mother to those with grave and fair dreams. Though its urns, cups, crowns, and tombs are broken, its memories survive. Persian forces advance against it under Xerxes, but Hellas protects Greeks: victory at Salamis and Plataea stops its subjugation, while scarcity and famine weaken the remaining invaders, who are punished for attacking its gods and temples.

Greece in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Greece first appears

Abstract written by a model

Greece is a region and homeland, described as the greatest of cities and as a land of shared reputation, assemblies, valor, and grief. Bacchus reaches it after travels through Asia and foreign lands, first raising his Bacchic shout in Thebes; Tiresias says he will become great throughout Greece. Yet the Heraclids are driven through every part of it, wandering city to city, while Iolaus appeals to Athens’s standing in Greece through Hercules and Theseus. Greece then gathers for vengeance over Helen’s abduction: its army is drawn into war against Troy, despite the suffering the conflict will bring and fears of humiliation if Agamemnon stops it. Its safety, victory, and freedom from barbarian rule are made to depend on Iphigenia’s willing sacrifice, offered for Greece’s common good and whole land, enabling the ships to pass and Troy to be destroyed. Greece is also Pylades’s homeland and the country to which Iphigenia and Orestes long to return. Though Iphigenia hates Greece for having destroyed her, news from there lets her recognize Orestes, and she ultimately escapes there with him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

10 sections; each heading opens the text

PYLADES, ORESTES, CHORUS.

Greece is invoked in the lament for the lost happiness and valor of the descendants of Atreus. Electra addresses the land of Greece while mourning the destruction of her family.

THE BACCHÆ.

Bacchus says that he has come to Greece after establishing his mysteries in foreign lands and that he first raises his Bacchic shout in Thebes. Tiresias says that Bacchus will become great throughout Greece.

THE HERACLIDÆ.

The Heraclids have been driven from every part of Greece and have wandered from one city to another. Iolaus appeals to the wider Greek reputation of Athens and says that Greece recognizes the former deeds of Hercules and Theseus.

IPHIGENIA IN AULIS.

Abstract written by a model

Greece is a country repeatedly described as a contested principality and as a collection of cities whose liberty, laws, and forms of government were central to its affairs. It first appears under pressure from Lacedaemonian attempts at sovereignty: the seizure of the Cadmea was widely condemned, and campaigns against Mantinea and Olynthus accompanied claims that the Lacedaemonians ruled all Greece by sea and land. Opposition gathered around Athens and the Thebans. Continual wars exhausted Greece, and a peace proposal won broad agreement for self-government and the removal of garrisons, though the Thebans were excluded after rejecting its terms. Political division then became decisive. Lacedaemonians backed oligarchies, Athenians protected democracies, and Greece suffered anarchy, seditions, renewed wars, and instability despite efforts by Artaxerxes to restore peace. It supplied soldiers for Tachos and remained entangled in disputes involving Athens, Megalopolis, and Peparethos. As Philip won victories, Greek cities voluntarily submitted to him and made him general of all Greece, although Athens urged the cities to defend their liberties and the record reports traitors and bribery throughout Greece. Greeks also joined Athens in aiding Byzantium. After Philip's death, several peoples and cities considered revolt, but Alexander restored authority through persuasion, fear, and force. At Corinth the Greeks recognized him as general and approved aid against Persia. Further revolts, especially at Thebes, ended in severe measures and fear among other rebels. Greece was also presented as the reason for Alexander's expedition, intended to add Asia to it. While Alexander was in Asia, tumults and seditions produced the Lamian War. Thereafter Greece's cities faced Macedonian garrisons, oligarchic rule, and rivalry among Antipater, Cassander, Antigonus, Ptolemy, Polyperchon, and Demetrius. Campaigns, alliances, sieges, promises to restore former governments, and efforts to free cities continued; parts were wasted and harassed. Peace between Cassander and Demetrius stipulated that Greek cities in Greece and Asia retain their ancient laws and liberties, but contests and military actions persisted.

Greece in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Greece first appears

Abstract written by a model

Greece is a country containing Megalopolis, rich in silver, gold, and temple offerings, and important enough to serve as a comparison with distant Libya, India, and Arabia. Its history is presented as a movement between freedom, external threat, internal betrayal, and Roman control. It was invaded by Xerxes and the Medes, while the Achæans stood apart from the principal Greek forces; after the Galati crossed into Asia Minor, no Greek state was clearly preeminent. Philip threatened it through garrisons and raids. Greece was weak when Brennus and the Galati targeted its wealth, but the expedition that reached Delphi ended in the invaders’ ruin. Greece later achieved autonomy and freedom through the arms of Thebes and the deeds associated with Epaminondas, after having been nearly ruined by leaders of the Peloponnesian War and partly recovering through Conon and Epaminondas. It was freed by Titus Flaminius, yet treason repeatedly destroyed its cities more than plague had done. After Rome defeated Perseus, Greece became subject to Rome: taxes, restrictions, and the suppression of national meetings followed, though the meetings, cross-border landholding, and remission of fines were later restored. Philopœmen warned that submission to Rome hastened Greece’s fate; he was its last public benefactor, after Miltiades. Nero made Greece entirely free, but discord under Vespasian brought taxes and obedience to a Roman prætor. Greece is ultimately described as extremely weak, having ceased to produce distinguished men, and later was overrun by the Costoboci.

Greece suffered repeated destruction through treason, and its cities were said to have been ruined by treason more than formerly by the plague. After the Romans defeated Perseus, the Achæans became subject to Rome.

Gallus acted with hauteur toward the Greeks and treated the Lacedæmonians and Argives contemptuously. The Athenians were described as having been reduced to greater poverty than any of the Greeks by the war with the Macedonians.

CHAPTER XVI.

Greece was subjected to Roman taxes, political restrictions, and the suppression of general meetings after the war. Not many years later, the Romans restored the old national meetings, allowed landholding across borders, and remitted fines imposed on Greek communities.

CHAPTER XVII.

Greece was reduced to extreme weakness and great straits by successive disasters and conflicts. Nero later made Greece entirely free, but under Vespasian it suffered intestine discord and was again subjected to taxes and ordered to obey the prætor because, Vespasian said, Greece had unlearnt how to use liberty.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Greece is described as having supported the peopling of Megalopolis through the best wishes of all Greece.

CHAPTER LI.

Aristænus urged the Achæans to do whatever pleased the Romans and not resist them, but Philopœmen told him that he was hastening the fate of Greece.

CHAPTER LII.

Greece is said to have stopped producing a stock of distinguished men, with Philopœmen identified as its last public benefactor. It was nearly ruined by the most renowned leaders of the Peloponnesian War, then partly recovered through the actions of Conon and Epaminondas.

CHAPTER XV.

Greece became autonomous and free through the deeds associated with Epaminondas.

CHAPTER XXI.

Greece is used as the standard for comparison with the remote regions of Libya, India, and Arabia. The speaker says that someone seeking the wild animals found in Greece would not find them in those distant lands, but would encounter different animals.

CHAPTER XIX.

Greece was targeted by the Galati because Brennus considered it weak and exceptionally wealthy in silver, gold, and temple offerings. The greatest confrontation between the Galati and the Greeks during the invasion took place at Delphi.

CHAPTER XXI.

Brennus sought to enter Greece by way of Thermopylæ after advancing from Heraclea.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Celtic expedition to Greece ended in the utter ruin of the invading force that reached Delphi. The surviving Celtic forces were attacked during their withdrawal, and the remaining Celts crossed back to Asia Minor the following year.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Titus Flaminius was sent from Rome to free all Greece. In the speaker's day, the Costoboci overran all Greece before coming to Elatea.
