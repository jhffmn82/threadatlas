Arabians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Arabians gather balsam sap in season, knocking poles to frighten sacred vipers away rather than killing them.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Arabians gather the sap of the balsam tree when the proper season arrives. They knock two poles together to frighten away the vipers and do not kill them because they regard them as sacred.

Arabs in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

Arabs are a group associated with North Africa through conquest and later migration. Around the middle of the seventh century they invaded North Africa from Asia, fought Romans and indigenous Berbers, and later ruled Morocco. In the first years of the eighth century they invaded Spain; after expulsion from Spain, many refugees settled in Barbary and helped maritime cities grow. In Morocco they appear among mixed, nomadic populations, although their numbers are said to have been overstated relative to the aboriginal Amazirgh population. During the expedition, Arab troops, camp followers, and temporary welcoming groups supplied much of the supporting force. They joined the camp for inspection by the Bey, fired long guns, presented or lost horses to him, hunted, and displayed horsemanship. Records also describe their food, punishments for theft, women’s looloos, chiefs’ wives in camel palanquins, and ritual words spoken while slaughtering game.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER III.

The Arabs invaded North Africa from Asia around the middle of the seventh century and fought both the Romans and the indigenous Berbers. Arabs later ruled Morocco and, after being expelled from Spain, many refugees settled in Barbary, contributing to the growth of maritime cities.

CHAPTER VI.

Arabs appear among the mixed and nomadic populations of Morocco and inhabit or visit many of the described districts. The author argues that previous writers have overstated their numbers relative to the aboriginal Amazirgh population.

CHAPTER IX.

Arab troops and camp followers formed most of the expedition's supporting force, and additional Arab groups joined temporarily as honorary welcomes to the Bey. Arabs were described as eating dogs, foxes, wolves, and other animals, while some were flogged for stealing barley; Arab women saluted the passing camp with shrill looloos.

Rode to Kairwan.

A hundred Arabs joined the camp and passed before the Bey for inspection, arriving in small groups and firing their long guns. An Arab insulted R. after R. cut the throat of a gazelle in the Moorish manner; the Bey ordered him to receive two hundred bastinadoes and be chained, but R. obtained his release after the whipping began.

Arabs joined the expedition in increasing numbers, brought horses as presents or had them seized for the Bey, and performed displays of horsemanship called playing at powder. They hunted gazelles, hares, hobaras, and foxes, and when an animal was secured they cut its throat while saying, "Bismillah, Allah Akbar." Some chiefs traveled with their wives in enclosed camel palanquins.

Arabs in Musical Instruments

Carl Engel · 1875-08

Arabs adopted Persian instruments and musical practices after conquering Persia, developed theorized seventeen one-third-tone octaves, then carried instruments into Spain and Europe.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

THE PERSIANS AND ARABS.

The Arabs adopted Persian instruments and musical practices after conquering Persia, including the harp, lute, and kemangeh. Their theorists described a musical system dividing the octave into seventeen one-third-tones, and Arabian instruments were later carried into Spain and Europe.
