Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Tunis is a city and strategic site captured, razed, fortified, besieged, and regained during the wars involving Agathocles and the Carthaginians. In Travels in Morocco, Tunis is a regency and starting point for journeys, supplying water, camels, commerce, markets, and equal rights for Jews.

Tunis in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Tunis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tunis is a city two thousand furlongs from Carthage. Agathocles captured it after moving his army there and razed it rather than garrisoning it. Later he fortified a camp near Tunis; the Carthaginians took the camp, besieged Tunis, fled before his apparent large approach, then besieged it again and regained surrounding country. After Archagathus divided forces, Greeks in Tunis were hemmed in and starved.

Tunis in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860 · the work’s page · read it where Tunis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tunis is a regency of the Port of Tunis, ruled by an hereditary Bey, where severe punishments are rare. Nearly its entire surface is covered with Roman, Christian, and Moorish aqueducts. It is a starting point for journeys: tourists left it, sent baggage to Giovanni, obtained supplies there, and returned from the march. It supplied water for the Bey by camel and was the source of his extensive camel holdings, including animals exported to Sicily. Its commerce passed through Nefta toward the Sahara, and Dutch tiles from Tunis appeared there. The Bey’s decree gave Jews equal rights and privileges with other subjects. Tunis also had markets, Bardo ceremonies, Ghamart near Carthage, and sacrifices during drought and a frigate’s return.

CHAPTER IX.

The tourists began their journey from Tunis and sent baggage to Giovanni, the 'Guarda-pipa.' Water for the Bey was carried from Tunis by camels, and the tourists returned there briefly to obtain neglected supplies before rejoining the expedition.

Rode to Kairwan.

Tunis was the source of the Bey's extensive camel holdings, which he farmed out through district overseers. A Sicilian nobleman once exported about eighty camels from Tunis to Sicily, with the duty remitted by the Bey.

CHAPTER X.

Tunis is the source of commerce sent through Nefta toward the Sahara. It is also associated with the Bey’s authority, with Dutch tiles brought from Tunis appearing in Nefta’s houses, and with a decree placing Jews on the same footing of rights and privileges as other subjects.

The party returned toward Tunis and later arrived there, calling at the Bardo to see the guard mounting and hear a military band. Tunisian prices are given for camels, horses, and sheep, and the city is described as a market where a tolerably good horse sells for 800 to 1000 piastres.

notes

Tunis is described as containing the hill of Ghamart near Carthage, where a red heifer was sacrificed during a drought. A Bey's frigate returning from Malta also prompted the sacrifice of two sheep to tutelar saints.
