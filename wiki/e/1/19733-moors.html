Moors in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860 · the work’s page · read it where Moors first appears

Abstract written by a model

Moors is a name used for inhabitants of Barbary and, in these records, especially the inhabitants and rulers of Morocco. They are described as living mainly in towns and cities, governing or possessing places including Arzila, El Araish, Mehedia, Salee, Rabat, and Dar-el-Beida, and as having retaken some coastal strongholds while leaving other fortifications weak or neglected. Accounts distinguish them from Arabs in some population classifications, while also giving varying accounts of their descent: Arabs in Spanish usage, people affected by the expulsion from Spain, or aboriginal Amazirghs. Their history is presented through conflicts, law, and treaties involving England, France, Spain, and other European powers. Social descriptions attribute religiously required bathing, particular building preferences, and contested accounts of cleanliness and conduct to them. One account reports contempt toward London Jew-boys and disagreement over abolition; another condemns feastday behavior as lascivious. In the military episode, they fire on French forces, fight effectively for two hours, then lose resolve after Omar Ei-Haj is killed, abandon the batteries, and flee. The closing notes reject an entirely uniform portrayal of them as dirty, lazy, inhuman, or indomitable while recording severe judgments about sacrifice and the origin of their name.

The Moors possess or govern many of the towns described, including Arzila, El Araish, Mehedia, Salee, Rabat, and Dar-el-Beida. They have retaken several coastal strongholds from the Portuguese or Spanish, but some Moorish fortifications and ports are described as weak, neglected, or decaying.

CHAPTER VI.

Moors are described as inhabitants of towns such as Oushda, Messa or Assah, and Akka, and as part of the mixed population estimates for Morocco. The text distinguishes them from Arabs in some demographic classifications and notes that many people called Moors may descend from aboriginal Amazirghs.

CHAPTER VII.

The Moors are said to treat London Jew-boys with contempt, and some reportedly wished that a fire at a Jewish merchant's house had spread so they could appropriate goods. The Governor says that most Moors oppose abolition, although some favor it.

The text describes masked men performing revolting, lascivious, and indecent actions during Moorish feastdays. It presents this behavior as the worst side of the character of the Moors.

APPENDIX.

The Moors opened fire before the French had taken up position and fought effectively for about two hours. After their best gunner, Omar Ei-Haj, was killed, they became dispirited, abandoned the batteries, and fled.

notes

The Moors of Barbary are described as having no conception of the enormity of sacrificing human beings to God or to a ruler. The notes also discuss competing explanations for the name Moors and criticize an account that portrays them as a uniformly dirty, lazy, inhuman, and indomitable people.
