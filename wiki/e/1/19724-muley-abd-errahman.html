Muley Abd Errahman in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

Muley Abd Errahman is a Shereefian prince and Sultan, the eldest son of Muley Hisham and nephew of Muley Suleiman. Suleiman chose and declared him legitimate hereditary successor instead of his own children. Portrayed as frugal, pacific, and generally not bloodthirsty, he consolidated imperial authority and preserved internal quiet with his vizier Bouseilam, though he was accused of avarice, confiscations, and imprisonments. He had three harems, used the Imperial parasol, and had sons governing Fez and Rabat.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II.

Muley Abd Errahman was chosen by his dying uncle, Muley Suleiman, instead of Suleiman’s own children. He is portrayed as a frugal and pacific ruler who consolidated imperial authority, preserved the empire through long periods of internal quiet, and generally avoided bloodshed, although he was accused of avarice, confiscations, and imprisonments.

[5] In the Shereefian lineage of Muley Suleiman, copied for Ali Bey by the Emper

Muley Abd Errahman was declared by Muley Suleiman to be the legitimate and hereditary successor of the Shereefs. The text later describes him as pursuing a pacific policy with the assistance of his vizier, Bouseilam.

Sultan of Morocco in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

The Sultan of Morocco is a sovereign Shereef and head of the orthodox religion of the Mussulmen of the West, claiming authority across territories where direct power is often nominal. He has influence in Western Barbary and among Muslims in Central Africa, while Sous has no real power under him and Wadnoun offers only nominal acknowledgment and occasional presents. He uses religious titles and honors to divide chiefs and strengthen his position. Residing mainly at Fez, Mequinez, and Morocco, he maintains northern allegiance and restrains disaffected tribes, retreating to Morocco during difficult European relations. He refuses abolition of slavery unless superior Christian force compels it.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER III.

The Sultan of Morocco is the head of the orthodox religion of the Mussulmen of the West and claims authority over territories where his direct power is often only nominal. He can use religious titles and honors, including those associated with the Shereefian Parasol, to set chiefs against one another and strengthen his position.

CHAPTER V.

The Sultan of Morocco resides mainly at Fez, Mequinez, and Morocco, using his presence to maintain allegiance and restrain disaffected tribes. He sends troublesome members of his family to Tafilett, and he sometimes retreats to Morocco when relations with European powers become difficult. The text says that the Emperors never visit Tafilett except as dethroned exiles.

CHAPTER VII.

The Governor says that the Sultan of Morocco will never listen to an appeal for abolition, even if presented by another official representative. He says the Sultan might yield only if Christians conquered the country and imposed the matter by superior force.
