Chaldeans in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Chaldeans first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Chaldeans are the most ancient Babylonians, holding a role in Babylonia comparable to Egyptian priests in Egypt; an Egyptian account claims they were colonies of Egypt. They continuously study philosophy as a religious duty and are especially famous for astrology, which they say they have pursued for 470,000 years before Alexander was in Asia. Their hereditary learning passes from father to son and trains philosophers from childhood. They observe stars and planets exactly, call planets Interpreters, use dreams and other signs for divination, and perform divine offices, sacrifices, purifications, and enchantments. They hold the world eternal and its motions ordered by gods.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

We shall first speak of the barbarians; not that we judge them

Diodorus says he will speak of the barbarians first, not because he judges them more ancient than the Grecians, but to distinguish their antiquity from matters foreign to them.

CHAP. III.

The Chaldeans are described as the most ancient Babylonians and as holding in Babylonia a position comparable to that of the Egyptian priests in Egypt. They devote themselves to divine offices, philosophy, astrology, divination, sacrifices, purifications, enchantments, the interpretation of birds, dreams, prodigies, and sacrificial entrails. They preserve their learning by hereditary tradition and claim to have observed the stars for 470,000 years when Alexander was in Asia.

Chaldeans in Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa

Augustus Le Plongeon · 1881 · the work’s page · read it where Chaldeans first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Chaldeans are an ancient people described as settling the lower Mesopotamian valleys in remote ages, with Ur or Hur as their capital in Abraham’s time. They used a Chaldean writing system and built primitive mud-and-brick monuments with rectangular, cardinally oriented, stepped forms, staircases, and crowning shrines. Their traditions included a creation account, Thalatth and the sea, while early kings reportedly ruled four races. Their clothing included a tunic, abba cloak, and camel-hair ornamented head band; learned men were called Magi. Their architecture, writing, customs, dress, and religion are compared with those of the Mayas.
