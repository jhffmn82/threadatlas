In “His Last Bow,” the British government owned and tightly guarded the Bruce-Partington submarine’s invention, treated its theft as a national-security matter, employed Mycroft Holmes, maintained a fifty-million special war tax, and promised Sherlock Holmes the full force of the State. British government in The 'Patriotes' of '37

Alfred D. Decelles · 1916 · the work’s page · read it where British government first appears

Abstract written by a model

The British government is the governing authority directing policy toward Canada and Lower Canada. It began with a conciliatory approach toward the French inhabitants of Canada: it did not treat them as a conquered people, accepted French in the law-courts, granted Catholics a described measure of just and generous liberty, and made concessions that maintained loyalty to the British crown during the wars of 1775 and 1812 while strengthening French national identity. In Lower Canada it appointed the governor and Executive Council through the Colonial Office, initially supplied deficit funds, accepted the House of Assembly’s 1818 offer to vote most provincial revenue, and later proposed a financial compromise involving a permanent civil list. As disputes and racial conflict intensified after 1830, it was asked through the Ninety-Two Resolutions to remedy administrative abuses, but the resolutions’ demands and criticism were unlikely to win favorable consideration. It then established a royal commission, appointing Lord Gosford, to investigate and if possible settle the disputes, while rejecting major constitutional change, especially an elective Legislative Council. By 1836 it abandoned conciliation without concessions and supported the Russell resolutions. After rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada, it suspended Lower Canada’s constitution and sent Durham to investigate grievances; when his ordinance was attacked, it disallowed it and passed an Act of Indemnity for those who carried it out rather than firmly supporting him.

The British government pursued a conciliatory policy toward the French inhabitants of Canada. It made no objection to the use of French in the law-courts and its concessions helped keep French Canadians loyal to the British crown.

{21} CHAPTER IV THE RISE OF PAPINEAU In the year 1812 a young man took his seat in the House of Assembly for Lower Canada who was destined to play a conspicuous part in the history of the province during the next quarter of a century.

The Imperial government appointed the governor and Executive Council through the Colonial Office and initially supplied funds needed to cover Lower Canada's deficit. In 1818 it accepted the Assembly's offer to vote most provincial revenue, and in 1830 Lord Goderich proposed another financial compromise involving a permanent civil list.

{33} CHAPTER V THE NINETY-TWO RESOLUTIONS After 1830 signs began to multiply that the racial feud in Lower Canada was growing in intensity.

The British government was asked through the Ninety-Two Resolutions to address abuses in Lower Canada's administration. The Resolutions' tactless criticism of the constitution, republican claims, reference to the American Revolution, and demand for Lord Aylmer's impeachment were unlikely to receive favorable consideration.

{44} CHAPTER VI THE ROYAL COMMISSION A general election followed soon after the passing of the Ninety-Two Resolutions and revealed the strength of Papineau's position in the country.

The British government sent a royal commission to investigate the disputes in Canada and appointed Lord Gosford as governor and chief royal commissioner. It did not contemplate major constitutional changes, especially an elective Legislative Council.

{57} CHAPTER VII THE RUSSELL RESOLUTIONS When the legislature of Lower Canada met in the autumn of 1836, Lord Gosford earnestly called its attention to the estimates of the current year and the accounts showing the arrears unpaid.

The British government abandoned its effort to conciliate the Assembly without concessions and supported the Russell resolutions. It had previously granted Catholics in Canada a measure of liberty that the Church feared might not be preserved under a republican government.

{104} CHAPTER X THE LORD HIGH COMMISSIONER The rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada profoundly affected public opinion in the mother country.

The British government suspended Lower Canada's constitution and sent Durham to investigate the colony's grievances. When Durham's ordinance was attacked, it disallowed the ordinance and passed an Act of Indemnity instead of firmly supporting him.
