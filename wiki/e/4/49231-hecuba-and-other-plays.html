Hecuba and other plays

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The document is a sequence of tragic and satyric dramatic records centered on the aftermath and wider consequences of Troy’s fall, the sufferings of Trojan survivors, and the destructive force of divine will, war, vengeance, and unstable fortune. It begins with Hecuba: Polydore’s ghost exposes Polymestor’s murder for Priam’s gold, while the Greeks sacrifice Polyxena. Hecuba receives both children’s bodies, wins Agamemnon’s covert support, and uses Trojan women to kill Polymestor’s sons and blind him; Agamemnon judges him guilty. It then follows Hercules’ family under Lycus’s tyranny. Hercules returns from the Underworld, kills Lycus, but Iris and a Fiend, acting for Juno, drive him mad so that he kills Megara and his children. Theseus persuades the devastated Hercules to live and accept refuge in Athens. The children of Hercules subsequently seek sanctuary in Athens from Eurystheus. Macaria offers herself to save Athens and her brothers; the allies defeat Eurystheus, who is captured and executed at Alcmene’s command despite an oracle concerning his burial. At Troy, Rhesus arrives too late to aid Hector and is killed at night by Ulysses and Diomede; the Muse promises his restoration as a god. The Trojan-captive records depict Cassandra’s assignment to Agamemnon, Andromache’s loss of Astyanax, Hecuba’s enslavement to Ulysses, Polyxena’s death, Helen’s confrontation with Hecuba and Menelaus, Astyanax’s burial, and Troy’s burning. Ulysses’ encounter with Polypheme follows: he gives the Cyclops wine, blinds him after Polypheme devours Greeks, and escapes. A separate Helen narrative clears Helen of blame: a phantom went to Troy while the real Helen remained faithful in Egypt. Reunited with shipwrecked Menelaus, she deceives Theoclymenus with a false funeral, escapes by sea, and is vindicated by Castor and Pollux. The final records return to Andromache in captivity in Phthia. Menelaus and Hermione threaten her and her son Molossus, but Peleus saves them. Hermione flees with Orestes, who has arranged Neoptolemus’ death. After Neoptolemus is killed at Apollo’s temple, Thetis orders his burial, directs Andromache and Molossus to a future with Helenus, and promises Peleus immortality.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

HECUBA.

Polydore’s ghost reveals that Polymestor murdered him for the gold sent by Priam and left his corpse unburied on the Thracian shore. Hecuba learns that the Greek army has resolved to sacrifice Polyxena at Achilles’ tomb, and she unsuccessfully begs Ulysses to spare her daughter. Polyxena accepts her death rather than endure enslavement, and Ulysses leads her away to be killed. Hecuba is left grieving, enslaved, and fearful for the fate of her remaining son.

CHORUS.

Hecuba learns from Talthybius that Polyxena was willingly sacrificed at Achilles’ tomb by the Greeks, who honored her courage and burial. Hecuba then receives Polyxena’s corse and orders that it be washed and properly adorned despite her captivity and poverty. An attendant brings the corse of Polydore, revealing that the Thracian king killed him after Priam had placed him in the king’s protection. The chorus recalls how Paris’s choice of Venus and his voyage from Sparta brought war, the destruction of Troy, and grief to the Greeks and Phrygians.

AGAMEMNON, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Hecuba reveals to Agamemnon that the dead youth is her son, whom Polymestor murdered after Troy fell and whose body he cast into the sea. She begs Agamemnon to avenge the violation of hospitality and justice, but he fears blame from the Greek army. Agamemnon agrees to conceal Hecuba’s plan, restrain the Greeks if necessary, and allow the dead son and Polyxena to be buried together. Hecuba intends to use the captive Trojan women to punish Polymestor.

CHORUS.

The Chorus laments Troy’s destruction and recalls how Hecuba escaped after seeing her husband dead and her native city ruined. Hecuba lures Polymestor into the Greek encampment by claiming that Priam’s treasure is hidden there, then has the Trojan women kill his sons and blinds him. Polymestor admits that he murdered Polydore to keep his gold and defends the act as protection against a renewed Trojan threat. Agamemnon hears both sides, judges Polymestor guilty, and orders him cast onto a deserted island, while Hecuba and the Trojan women prepare to bury the dead and depart for Greece.

HERCULES DISTRACTED.

Amphitryon and Megara shelter with the children of Hercules at the altar of Jupiter because Lycus has seized Thebes and threatens to kill them. Lycus argues that Hercules was not truly courageous and orders that the family be burned, while Amphitryon and the Chorus defend Hercules’ honor and condemn Lycus’ tyranny. Megara accepts that death is unavoidable but asks to dress the children in funeral robes before their execution. Lycus allows them to enter Hercules’ house for this purpose, and Amphitryon reproaches Jove for failing to protect his son’s family.

CHORUS.

The Chorus praises Hercules and recounts his many labours, including slaying the Nemean lion, the Centaurs, the Hydra, Geryon, and other monsters, and capturing Diomedes’ horses. It describes Hercules’ journey to the Hesperian gardens, the Euxine, and the underworld. Hercules has entered Pluto’s mansion, where the Chorus fears that he and his sons face an eternal fate. Hercules’ wife, his sons, and his aged father are seen in funereal vestments, and the Chorus mourns them.

MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

Megara laments that she, her children, and Amphitryon are being forced toward death and imagines them as victims destined for Pluto’s realm. She recalls Hercules’ former promises that his sons would inherit Argos, Thebes, and Oecalia, while her plans for their marriages have been destroyed. Megara calls on the deceased Hercules to appear and protect his family, but Amphitryon declares that they must die and appeals unsuccessfully to Zeus. Amphitryon tells the Chorus that Fortune has suddenly stripped him of his former glory and urges the aged men to make the best use of their remaining lives.

HERCULES, MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

Hercules returns from the Underworld with Cerberus and finds Megara, Amphitryon, and his children dressed for their impending deaths under Lycus’s rule. After learning that Lycus killed Megara’s brothers and intends to kill his family, Hercules hides in the palace and vows vengeance. Hercules kills Lycus inside the palace, and the Chorus celebrates the tyrant’s downfall and the restoration of justice. The unit ends with the Chorus seeing a frightening spectre hovering over the palace.

IRIS, A FIEND, CHORUS.

Iris and the Fiend conspire at Juno’s command to drive Hercules into madness and make him kill his children and Megara. The Fiend enters Hercules’ house, while the Chorus foresees disaster and a Messenger recounts how Hercules, believing his family to be Eurystheus’ children, kills them and Megara before Athena stops him. Hercules awakens bound among the corpses, learns what he has done from Amphitryon, and considers suicide, while awaiting the arrival of Theseus.

THESEUS, AMPHITRYON, HERCULES, CHORUS.

Theseus arrives with Athenian youths to help Hercules, but finds Hercules after he has killed his children and wife in a divinely induced frenzy. Hercules laments his suffering, rejects the gods, and considers death because he expects exile and universal pollution. Theseus persuades him to live, offers him refuge and wealth in Athens, and promises purification and honor. Hercules agrees to leave for Athens after Amphitryon buries the children, while Theseus leads him away as a steadfast friend.

THE CHILDREN OF HERCULES.

Iolaus brings the children of Hercules to the altar of Jupiter at Marathon after Eurystheus has pursued them from city to city and sought their destruction. Copreus arrives from Argos to seize the children, but Demophoon and the Chorus of Athenian old men defend their right to remain as suppliants. Demophoon refuses to surrender them, invoking the gods, kinship, past services, and the shame of violating the altar. Copreus threatens to return with Eurystheus and an Argive army, prompting Demophoon to prepare Athens for war.

DEMOPHOON, IOLAUS, CHORUS.

Demophoon reports that Eurystheus and the Argive army are approaching Athens, while the oracles require the sacrifice of a noble-born maiden to save the city and the foreign suppliants. Demophoon refuses to sacrifice his own daughter or any Athenian citizen, and Iolaus laments that the suppliants may be expelled. Macaria voluntarily offers herself as the victim so that her brothers and Athens may be saved, then departs for the sacrifice. A servant announces that Hyllus is leading an Athenian force into battle, and Iolaus insists on joining the fight despite his extreme old age.

SERVANT, ALCMENA, CHORUS.

The Servant reports to Alcmene that the allied forces have defeated the Argive army, while Hyllus and the rejuvenated Iolaus pursued and captured Eurystheus. Iolaus regained his youth temporarily through the favor of Hercules and Hebe and brought Eurystheus back alive. Alcmene demands Eurystheus's death, but the Messenger explains that Athens forbids killing a captive taken alive in battle. Eurystheus reveals an oracle of Phoebus promising that his buried corpse will protect Athens against the descendants of the race he hates, and Alcmene orders his execution.

RHESUS.

Hector learns from the Chorus of Trojan sentinels that the Greek camp is ablaze and in tumult during the night, possibly preparing to flee, but Aeneas advises sending a spy rather than attacking immediately. Dolon volunteers for the nocturnal spying mission in exchange for Achilles’ steeds and prepares to enter the Greek camp disguised as a wolf. A shepherd reports that Rhesus, son of Strymon, has arrived from Thrace with a large army, golden armor, and exceptionally white horses to aid Troy. Hector agrees to receive Rhesus as a friend and ally, although he initially scorns the late assistance.

RHESUS, HECTOR, CHORUS.

Rhesus arrives at Troy with Thracian forces and promises Hector that he will destroy the Greek fortifications and fleet in a single day. Hector rebukes Rhesus for coming late, while Rhesus explains that war with the Scythians delayed him and claims that he can end the siege. They discuss the Greek commanders, especially Achilles, Ajax, Tydeus’ son, and Ulysses, before Hector assigns Rhesus a place to encamp and gives Apollo as the watchword. After Rhesus departs, Hector orders the watch extended while the Chorus waits anxiously for Dolon, who was sent to reconnoitre the Greek fleet.

ULYSSES, DIOMEDE.

Ulysses and Diomede enter the Trojan camp at night, kill Rhesus and seize his chariot and white horses. Minerva directs them away from Hector and Paris, then helps them escape after the Trojan guards are alerted. Rhesus’s charioteer reports the destruction of the Thracian force and accuses Hector, while Hector blames Ulysses and condemns the guards. The Muse reveals Rhesus’s parentage, mourns him, and declares that he will be restored to life as a god, while Hector prepares Troy for battle at dawn.

THE TROJAN CAPTIVES.

Neptune and Minerva agree to punish the Greeks for Ajax’s violation of Minerva’s temple by sending storms against their homeward voyage. Hecuba learns from Talthybius that the Trojan captives have been assigned to Greek lords: Cassandra to Agamemnon, Andromache to Achilles’ son, Hecuba to Ulysses, and Polyxena to Achilles’ tomb. Cassandra arrives in ecstatic prophetic frenzy, treating her enslavement to Agamemnon as a wedding and foretelling the destruction of his house and the sufferings of the Greeks. Talthybius leads Cassandra away, while Hecuba and the chorus mourn Troy’s destruction and their enslavement.

CHOR. Attendants of the aged Hecuba,

Hecuba mourns the destruction of Troy, the deaths of her family, and her own impending enslavement in Greece. Andromache arrives with Astyanax and learns from Talthybius that the Greeks have decreed her son must be thrown from Troy’s towers because he is Hector’s child. Andromache laments Astyanax’s impending death, while Hecuba and the Chorus mourn Polyxena’s sacrifice, Hector’s death, and the fall and sack of Troy. The Chorus recounts how the Greeks deceived the Trojans with the wooden horse and destroyed their city.

MENELAUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Menelaus arrives at the ruined Troy to take Helen to Greece, intending that she eventually be slain for the deaths and suffering caused by the war. Helen defends herself by blaming the Judgment of Paris, Venus, Paris, and forces beyond her control, while Hecuba rejects her arguments and accuses her of willingly abandoning Menelaus for wealth and luxury. Menelaus accepts Hecuba’s accusation and orders Helen taken to the ships, though he agrees that she will not enter his own vessel. The Chorus mourns the destruction of Troy, the loss of its religious rites, and the suffering of the Phrygian women, and prays that Helen never reaches Greece.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Talthybius tells Hecuba that Neoptolemus has sailed for Phthia with Andromache and has entrusted Astyanax’s body to Hecuba for burial. Hecuba mourns Astyanax, condemns the Greeks for killing him, and buries him in Hector’s shield after performing funeral rites with the Chorus. Talthybius then orders the burning of Troy and tells Hecuba that she must follow Ulysses into slavery. Hecuba and the Chorus lament the flames, the destruction of Troy, and their removal from their homeland.

THE CYCLOPS.

Silenus and the Chorus of Satyrs serve Polypheme the Cyclops on Mount Ætna after being captured while seeking Bacchus. Ulysses arrives with his men from Troy and exchanges Bacchus’s wine for lambs and cheeses, but Polypheme returns and threatens to eat the strangers. Ulysses appeals to the laws of hospitality and to the gods, while Polypheme rejects those laws and prepares to devour the Greeks. The Chorus laments the danger and urges escape by ship.

ULYSSES, CHORUS.

Ulysses recounts how Polyphemus killed and ate two of his Grecian companions in the Cyclops’s cave. Ulysses uses wine to intoxicate Polyphemus, then plans and carries out the blinding of Polyphemus with a heated olive pole, while the Chorus assists only reluctantly. Afterward, Ulysses reveals his name and declares that he will sail toward his country across Sicilia’s waves, while Polyphemus threatens him and his crew.

HELEN.

Helen explains that Hera gave the Greeks and Trojans a phantom resembling her while Hermes carried the real Helen to Egypt, where she has remained faithful to Menelaus. Teucer arrives at Theoclymenus’ palace, reports that Troy has fallen, Menelaus is presumed dead after the Greek fleet was scattered, and Leda has died. Helen mourns the destruction caused by her false reputation and considers suicide, while the Chorus urges her to consult Theonoe about Menelaus’ fate.

MENELAUS.

Menelaus, shipwrecked after the fall of Troy, reaches Egypt with a few surviving companions and searches for aid while Helen remains hidden in a cave. At Proteus’s palace, he meets Helen, who reveals that the woman he brought from Troy is only a phantom created from ether, while the real Helen was brought to Egypt by the gods. A messenger reports that Helen’s phantom has vanished into the heavens, confirming Helen’s innocence and reuniting her with Menelaus. Menelaus sends the messenger to tell the Greeks and prepare their escape from Egypt, while the messenger rejects reliance on prophetic signs.

CHOR. My sentiments on prophets well accord

Helen and Menelaus learn from Theonoe that she will conceal Menelaus’s arrival and help them escape, because justice and respect for her father forbid her from betraying them to her brother. Helen and Menelaus devise a false funeral for Menelaus, claiming that he died in the sea, so that Helen can obtain a ship and they can seize it and flee. Theonoe’s prophecy also reveals that Juno favors their return to Sparta, while Venus seeks to prevent it. Helen prepares to spread the report of Menelaus’s death and to exchange her clothing for mourning dress.

CHORUS.

Helen and Menelaus deceive Theoclymenus by claiming that Menelaus has died and by arranging funeral rites at sea. Theoclymenus supplies a Tyrian bark, sailors, arms, gifts, and permission for Helen and Menelaus to conduct the rites, while Helen and Menelaus prepare to escape. The Chorus recalls the Trojan War, Paris’s abduction of Helen, the destruction of Troy, and the divine phantom that caused the Greeks to fight. Theoclymenus plans to marry Helen, unaware that Menelaus is alive and that the funeral is part of their escape.

Swift bark of Sidon, by whose dashing oars

Helen escapes from Egypt with Menelaus after he deceives Theoclymenus by claiming to have died and arranging a mock funeral at sea. Menelaus and his Greek sailors seize the Sidonian ship, defeat the Egyptian sailors in a battle aboard it, and begin the voyage to Greece. Theoclymenus learns of Helen’s escape and attempts to punish his sister Theonoe, but the Dioscuri, Castor and Pollux, intervene and reveal that fate and divine will required Helen to return to Menelaus. They promise Helen future divine honors and Menelaus a residence among the happy islands.

ANDROMACHE.

Andromache, formerly Hector’s wife and queen in Troy, lives as a captive in Phthia and seeks protection in Thetis’ temple from Hermione and Menelaus. Hermione accuses Andromache of using magic drugs to make her barren and hateful to Neoptolemus, and threatens to drive her from the sanctuary or kill her. Andromache defends her conduct, her loyalty to Hector, and her belief that female virtue, not beauty or coercion, secures domestic harmony. The chorus recalls how Paris, Helen, and the judgment of the goddesses brought about the destruction of Troy and the suffering of its people.

MENELAUS, MOLOSSUS, ANDROMACHE, CHORUS.

Menelaus brings Molossus from Thetis’s altar and threatens Andromache with death unless she leaves the shrine, while claiming that Hermione will decide whether Molossus lives. Andromache argues that Menelaus is punishing her for a wrong committed by Hermione and presents herself as a captive who was forced into Hector’s bed. She leaves the altar to save Molossus, but Menelaus orders both of them seized and condemned. Andromache and Molossus plead for mercy, yet Menelaus sends them together to the underworld, saying that Hermione’s sentence will determine Molossus’s fate.

PELEUS, MENELAUS, ANDROMACHE, MOLOSSUS, CHORUS.

Peleus intervenes to free Andromache and Molossus from chains and protect them from Menelaus, who intends to kill them. Peleus and Menelaus argue over Menelaus's authority, Helen's conduct, Andromache's status, and the justice of vengeance. Menelaus leaves for Sparta, promising to return and confront Neoptolemus, while Peleus leads Andromache and Molossus to safety in Phthia. The Chorus reflects on inherited nobility, lawful victory, and the dangers of pursuing power through injustice.

THE NURSE OF HERMIONE, CHORUS.

Hermione, fearing punishment from Achilles’ son for attempting to kill Andromache and her son, tries to hang or stab herself but is restrained by the Nurse and servants. Orestes arrives, learns of Hermione’s distress, and promises to take her away to her father while revealing that he has arranged Achilles’ son’s death. Hermione and Orestes leave together, and the Chorus sings of Troy’s destruction, the deaths caused by the Trojan War, and the divine command that led Orestes to kill his mother. The unit also recalls that Hermione’s marriage resulted from her father’s betrayal of Orestes, to whom she had first been betrothed.

PELEUS, CHORUS.

Peleus learns from the Chorus and a Messenger that Hermione has fled and that Achilles’ son has been killed by Orestes and Delphi’s citizens at Apollo’s temple. The Messenger describes how Achilles’ son resisted the ambush before being overwhelmed, and his body is brought back to Peleus. Thetis appears, orders that the body be buried at Delphi, assigns Andromache to Helenus in Molossia with her young son, and promises to make Peleus immortal. Peleus accepts her commands, while the Chorus concludes that the gods bring unexpected outcomes through fate.
