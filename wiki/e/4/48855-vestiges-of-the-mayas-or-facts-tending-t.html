Abstract written by a model

The document is a wide-ranging study of Yucatan, the Maya past, and alleged connections between the Mayas and ancient peoples elsewhere. It begins with Yucatan’s flat limestone land, underground water, caves and senotes, lack of surface rivers, climate, resources, ruins, and current population and conflict between the Santa Cruz Indians and Yucatecans. It notes the dominance of hennequen in white-controlled territory and resulting maize shortages. The central figures are Augustus Le Plongeon and Mrs. Le Plongeon, whose investigations at Chichen-Itza, Uxmal, and other sites are presented as discoveries of Chaacmol’s tomb, statue, and funeral chamber. The narrative emphasizes Maya cities, monuments, writing, sciences, language, funerary customs, surviving traditions, and the need to recover hidden ancient books with Mexican government permission. From these archaeological claims, the work expands into comparative argument. It distinguishes religious associations at Uxmal and Chichen-Itza, discusses the decline of ancient Maya religion, and derives Mayab from a word for a sieve or tammy, linked to Yucatan’s porous soil. It then argues that monuments, names, rites, languages, architecture, writings, burials, costumes, and symbols show relationships between the Mayas and peoples of Chaldea, Asia, Hindostan, Egypt, Nubia, Equatorial Africa, and other regions. The author supplies proposed Maya etymologies for foreign place-names, deities, biblical words, and Jesus’s final words, and compares Maya traditions involving Chaacmol, Moo, Aac, Cay, Kukulean, and Quetzalcoatl with Egyptian figures such as Osiris, Isis, Typho, and Horus. The later sections compare Maya and Egyptian religious practices, calendars, astronomy, writing, color symbolism, beliefs about the soul, initiation mysteries, and funerary customs. They also describe workers who regarded the narrator as a reincarnated Maya leader. The document repeatedly advances the view that the Mayas were an ancient and highly civilized people whose influence or migrations reached the Old World. Yet it ends by maintaining that the Mayas were indigenous to the western continent, using the Ake monument and its thirty-six katun columns to claim great antiquity, while conceding that decipherment of inscriptions and recovery of Maya libraries are needed before any unquestionable theory can be formed.

The text argues that monuments, customs, religious rites, languages, and traditions link the prehistoric inhabitants of America with peoples of Asia, Hindostan, Egypt, and other regions. It proposes that the Mayas migrated westward from the American continent across the Pacific toward Asia and the Nile valley, citing similarities in stepped architecture, place-names, religious practices, and physical types. It compares Maya traditions in Yucatan, especially at Chichen-Itza and Uxmal, with accounts from India, Afghanistan, Babylon, Asia Minor, and Egypt. The discussion presents the red hand imprint, the name Maya, and the traditions associated with Atlantis as evidence for this proposed migration and for broader comparative cultural connections.

The unit compares the primitive Chaldeans and the Mayas in their architecture, writing, burial customs, clothing, ornaments, and religion. It argues that Chaldean and Maya sacred buildings used superposed platforms, thick walls, staircases, and similar chambers, while their burial places and costumes also showed similarities. It compares Chaldean and Maya writing systems as using several classes of signs, including letters and monograms. It then proposes Maya-language etymologies for the names of several Chaldean divinities, including Ra, Ana, Bil, Beltis, Hea, Sin, San, Vul, and Ishtar.

The unit argues that the Maya language provides etymologies for names associated with places, peoples, deities, and ancient languages of Asia and the Near East. It links Urukh, Assyria, Asshur, Canaan, Tyre, and the Khati and their cities to Maya words, while comparing Chaldean, South American, Chinese, and Hindoo traditions concerning four divisions or provinces. It claims that the Book of Daniel’s words “Mene, mene, Tekel, upharsin” have meanings corresponding to Maya words. It also interprets Jesus of Nazareth’s reported final words as the Maya phrase “Helo, Helo, Lamah Zabac Ta Ni,” meaning that he was sinking and darkness covered his face.

The author argues that similarities between Maya and Egyptian languages and inscriptions raise the question of which people taught the other.

The unit argues that the Mayas were an ancient, highly civilized nation whose traditions, customs, language, and supposed colonies extended across Hindostan, Afghanistan, Egypt, Asia Minor, and other regions. It compares Maya traditions and figures from Mayab, especially Chaacmol, Moo, Aac, and Cay, with Egyptian accounts of Osiris, Isis, Typho, and related deities. The author uses similarities in names, religious symbols, writing, dress, monuments, and mural paintings at Chichen-Itza and Uxmal to argue for Maya influence or migration across the Old World. The unit also describes alleged Maya-Egyptian parallels involving leopard, macaw, serpent, sun, and baboon symbolism.

The unit argues that the Mayas were indigenous to the western continent and that their civilization did not originate in Asia or Africa. It connects the name Mayas to MAYAB, a porous land in the region beyond the Atlantic, and compares Maya language, beliefs, symbols, and monuments with those of Hindostan, Greece, and Egypt. It presents the Ake monument and its thirty-six katun columns as evidence that Maya civilization was at least 5,760 years old when the monument was completed. The author concludes that further research, especially the interpretation of inscriptions and recovery of Maya libraries, is necessary before forming an unquestionable theory.
