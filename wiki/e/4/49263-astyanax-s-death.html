Astyanax's death in The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · -0414

Astyanax's death is a Greek-ordered child killing. On Odysseus's advice, the Greek army fears Hector's son may restore his fallen land and become a threat. Soldiers take him from Andromache, who cannot save him, to dash him from Troy's wall. Hecuba condemns the murder as shameful and mourns his lost youth and greatness.

Astyanax's death is presented as a murder committed by the Greeks out of fear that the child might restore his fallen land. Hecuba calls it a strange and shameful death for a supposedly brave people and mourns that he died before reaching youth or greatness.

Astyanax’s execution in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Astyanax’s execution, ordered by the Greeks after Ulysses argues Hector’s son must not be raised, follows Troy’s fall: Andromache bids farewell before he is thrown from Troy’s towers.

Narrative, section by section

CHOR. Attendants of the aged Hecuba,

Astyanax’s execution is ordered by the Greeks after Ulysses argues that Hector’s son must not be raised. He is to be thrown from the towers of Troy, and Andromache gives him a final farewell before he is taken away.

death of Astyanax in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Astyanax’s death is described as a fall from the battlements. The notes preserve differing accounts naming Ulysses or Neoptolemus as the agent and differing accounts about whether the killing occurred during the sack or afterward by decree.
