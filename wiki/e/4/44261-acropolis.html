Acropolis in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Acropolis is a precipitous, walled place with one approach. Its marble-roofed entrance contains vestibules, the shrine of Wingless Victory, paintings, Hermes called Propylæus, and Graces attributed to Socrates. Within are statues, paintings, monuments of mythic and historical battles, the temples of Athene Polias and Pandrosus, sacred offerings, Callimachus’s golden lamp, and Athene’s olive. Its notable votives include brazen images and a chariot; its walls were largely attributed to Pelasgians, except Cimon’s part.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIX.

The Acropolis had only one approach because it was otherwise precipitous and walled off. Its entrance included marble-roofed vestibules, the shrine of Wingless Victory, paintings, a Hermes called Propylæus, and the Graces attributed to Socrates.

CHAPTER XXV.

The Acropolis contained statues of Pericles, Xanthippus, Anacreon, Io, Callisto, and Olympiodorus, along with paintings and monuments commemorating mythic and historical battles. It housed the temples of Athene Polias and Pandrosus, the ancient statue of Athene, sacred offerings, the golden lamp of Callimachus, and the olive associated with Athene’s contest with Poseidon. Olympiodorus and Leocritus were among those honored there.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

The Acropolis contained famous votive offerings, including the brazen statue of Athene, a brazen chariot made from spoil of the Bœotians and Chalcidians, and statues of Pericles and the Lemnian Athene. Its walls, except for the part built by Cimon son of Miltiades, were said to have been drawn out by the Pelasgians Agrolas and Hyperbius.
