Abstract written by a model

In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Alexander succeeds Philip, secures Macedonia and Greece, conquers territories from Egypt to India, founds cities, leads his army and fleet, and dies after a reign of twelve years and seven months. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Alexander is the Macedonian king who conquers Persia and extensive territories toward India, combines severity with generosity, and dies at Babylon after a career of command and conquest. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Alexander conquers Asia and subdues Indians, reaches the Ganges, abandons further conquest after learning of the Gandarides' elephants, and builds a city named after himself. In Description of Greece, Alexander, son of Philip, rules the Macedonian empire, conquers Porus and India, brings elephants to Europe, and after his death has his dominions divided and his corpse moved from Memphis by Ptolemy I Soter. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Alexander the son of Philip builds modern Smyrna, connects Clazomenæ to the mainland, bribes Chæron, makes offerings to Æsculapius, and is traditionally said to have died from Styx. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Alexander the Great cuts the Gordian Knot after failing to untie it, then conquers Asia and is regarded as fulfilling an oracle.

Abstract written by a model

Alexander is a Macedonian king, descended from Hercules on his father’s side and Achilles on his mother’s side. He succeeded Philip, the son of Amyntas, punished Philip’s murderers, secured Macedonia, overcame opposition in Greece, and was recognized as Greece’s general with support for war against Persia. After taking Gaza, he entered Egypt, where cities submitted without fighting; he consulted the oracle of Ammon, which declared him divinely born, and founded Alexandria. He repeatedly led and commanded the Macedonians through long campaigns, persuading them to continue, rewarding and discharging Greek auxiliaries, and extending control through Hyrcania, the Mardian country, Drangina, Arachosia, the Paropamisadae, Bactria, and India. His campaigns brought severe hardship, conquests, submissions, city foundations, and violence: he massacred Massaga mercenaries, subdued Musicanus and Porticanus, and wasted Sambus, while pardoning Brachmanes except leaders of defection. He crossed Caucasus and the Indus, refreshed his army in Taxiles, defeated Porus, restored Porus to his kingdom, and built cities near the river and another Alexandria on the Indus. Wounded but recovered, he sacrificed to the sun, buried Macedonians killed in battle, built ships, and led army and fleet down the Indus. He loved Ptolemy and, through a dream, discovered a healing herb that restored Ptolemy and others. He regarded Hephaestion as a friend with unusually close access, and later arranged Hephaestion’s funeral on an extraordinary scale. Alexander received ambassadors from nearly all parts of the world, then fell severely ill and died after a reign of twelve years and seven months. He gave his ring to Perdiccas and left the kingdom to the most deserving. His death left government without a head and triggered disputes; plans in his notebooks were judged impracticable and set aside.

Alexander succeeded Philip, punished his father's murderers, secured Macedonia, and rapidly overcame opposition in Greece. He was recognized as general of Greece and received a decree of aid against Persia, then returned to Macedonia after arranging affairs in Greece.

Alexander entered Egypt after the taking of Gaza, and the Egyptian cities submitted to him without fighting. He journeyed to the temple of Ammon, received favorable answers from its oracle, and then returned to Egypt to found Alexandria.

CHAP. VIII.

Alexander persuaded the Macedonians to continue the war, rewarded and discharged the Greek auxiliaries, and conquered or secured territories in Hyrcania, the Mardian country, Drangina, and Arachosia. He recovered his war horse, received Thalestris, adopted some Persian customs and court practices, punished alleged conspirators, and campaigned against the Arimaspi.

CHAP. IX.

Alexander led his army against the Paropamisadae, endured severe snow and cold, crossed Caucasus in sixteen days, and founded Alexandria and other cities. He then marched toward Bactria, where Bessus had usurped the crown, and later campaigned in India, massacring the Massaga mercenaries, capturing Aornos, crossing the Indus, and receiving Mophis into his protection.

Alexander's death left the government without a head and caused disputes over the succession. Abstract written by a model

Alexander is a Macedonian king, son of Philip and Olympias, descended from Hercules and Achilles, whose career is defined by command, conquest, and the overthrow of Persia. After securing his authority by suppressing revolts and destroying Thebes, he seeks quiet in Greece so that he can invade Asia. He crosses into Asia, claims it with a spear cast ashore, wins at Granicus, Issus, and Arbela, and takes or subdues cities and regions including Miletus, Caria, Tyre, Gaza, Babylon, Persepolis, Sogdiana, and territories toward India. His campaigns depend on personal courage, siegecraft, army organization, and repeated direct leadership, but bring wounds, hardship, mutinies, and resistance. His rule combines severity with generosity and clemency. He honors and supports Darius's family, restores Ada, aids maimed Greek captives, grants liberty to Nysa, rewards soldiers, and treats Persian prisoners civilly; he also plunders Persepolis, destroys cities, executes mutiny leaders, and kills Clitus in drunken rage, then mourns and condemns himself for it. He increasingly joins Macedonian and Persian interests through Persian appointments, Persian bodyguards, mass marriages, his marriage to Statira, and his trust in Persians, provoking Macedonian opposition. He is also married to Roxana, whom he loves. After reaching India and surviving severe danger and exhaustion, he returns through Gedrosia, reaches Susa and then Babylon, and orders Greek exiles recalled shortly before his death. He dies at Babylon, leaving no child to succeed him according to one record, though other records name a son. His body is carried toward Egypt and honored there. His legacy is the ruined Persian empire, cities called Alexandria, and enduring memory of extraordinary deeds, kindnesses, ambition, and power.

SUP. I.

Alexander marched through a waterless desert with a band of light-armed men and suffered with his army from extreme heat and thirst. He refused to drink water brought for his own use because it had been intended for the soldiers’ children. After reaching the river Oxus, he ordered fires to guide those behind and directed the refreshed soldiers to carry water to their companions.

SUP. III.

Alexander ordered his infantry to prepare scaling ladders and led campaigns against the rebellious cities. He captured and razed several towns, took Cyropolis by entering through the dry river channel, founded and populated a city, defeated the Asiatic Scythians at the Iaxartes, and spared several Sogdian noblemen after questioning them.

SUP, V,

Alexander marched again to the river Oxus and resolved to campaign against the Sogdians. He divided his army, led one part toward Maracanda, reduced revolted cities, and later assaulted the rock where the Sogdians had taken refuge. He promised safety to those who surrendered and offered rewards to the Macedonians who first climbed the rock.

SUP. VII.

Alexander entered a long-preserved hunting ground with his army and ordered the game to be roused throughout it. When a huge lion attacked him, he stopped Lysimachus from intervening and killed the lion with one blow. Afterward, the Macedonians decreed that he should not hunt on foot without a guard of his captains and friends.

SUP. VIIL

Alexander neglected Bacchus on the god's sacred day and sacrificed to Castor and Pollux instead. While drunk and enraged by Clitus's reproaches, he killed Clitus with a spear or sarissa, then repented, refused food and drink, and blamed himself for the deed.

Alexander is urged by Anaxarchus and others to accept divine honors, but Callisthenes argues that he should receive only the greatest honors appropriate to a human king. SUP. X.

The king remained patient during the disaster, walked around the camp, gathered the dispersed, lifted up those who had fallen, and directed them toward nearby refuges. He endured more hardship than the soldiers and helped encourage them to remain with him.

SUP. XI.

Alexander fell in love with Roxana upon seeing her and married her without treating her dishonourably as a captive. He also spared Darius's wife and induced many of his friends to marry the daughters of chief men among the barbarians.

SUP. XII.

Alexander moved from Bactria toward India, reorganized Alexandria in Paropamisus, appointed governors, and directed his commanders to secure the country and prepare to cross the Indus. He led a rapid advance through the territories of the Aspians, Phyrseans, and Arosacans, captured the first city he attacked, and was slightly wounded by a dart.

SUP. XIII.

Alexander arrived at Nysa and received Acuphis's request that he leave the city free in honor of Bacchus. He granted the Nysaeans liberty under their own laws, required three hundred horsemen, and appointed Acuphis governor of Nysaea. He later visited Nysa and Mount Meros, sacrificed to Bacchus, and feasted with his friends.

SUP. XIV.

Alexander led the Macedonians against Massaga and directed the battle, the assaults, and the siege works. He agreed to spare the mercenary Indians if they joined his army, but when they planned to flee, he surrounded their hill at night and put them all to the sword. He then took Massaga by storm.

BOOK XVII.

He became commander of the Amphictyons, invaded the Illyrians and others, destroyed Thebes, and invaded Asia. He defeated the Persians at Issus and Arbela, captured Tyre, Gaza, Persepolis, and many other places, marched as far as the Indus, and died at Babylon.

BOOK XX.

Alexander is referenced through his son Hercules, whom Polyperchon murders at Cassander's instigation, and through his sister Cleopatra, who is killed by the governor of Sardis.

Alexander was aided by Ptolemy when danger befell him at Oxydracæ. After his death, his dominions were divided into separate kingdoms, and his body was taken to Memphis and buried there with Macedonian rites.

CHAPTER VII.

Alexander the Great’s corpse was brought from Memphis by Ptolemy I Soter. He is described as having first brought elephants to Europe after conquering Porus and India, and as being related by his mother to the Æacidæ of Epirus.
