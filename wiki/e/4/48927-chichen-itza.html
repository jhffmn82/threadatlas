Chichen-Itza

place · 1 work · 5 quoted facts · 7 connections
Also called: Chichen, Chichen and Uxmal, Chichen and other places in Mayab, Chichsen

Abstract written by a model

In "Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa," Chichen is described as containing Chaacmol's funeral chamber and mausoleum, along with mural paintings, sculptures, and inscriptions depicting Maya life and beliefs. In "Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa," Chichen-Itza is described as a city of the holy and learned Itzaes, containing monumental architecture and the funeral chamber and mausoleum associated with Chaacmol.

Chichen-Itza in Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa

Augustus Le Plongeon · 1881 · the work’s page · read it where Chichen-Itza first appears

Abstract written by a model

Chichen-Itza is a city of the holy and learned Itzaes, said to have been founded by Cans together with Uxmal. Its architecture includes stone buildings with triangular-arched ceilings and platforms diminishing upward, a conception compared with Hindostan. It contains monumental buildings, sculptures, bas-reliefs, mural paintings, inscriptions, statues of dead personages, and a palace or museum. At the southern end of the eastern wall of the gymnasium is Chaacmol's funeral chamber; a colossal statue of Chaacmol was discovered in a burial context. The site is also identified as the place where Chaacmol and Moo supposedly reigned and where their mausoleum and related remains were found. Its imagery includes mastodon-head worship, reverential arm gestures, bearded and royal figures, leopard and macaw imagery, serpents, Moo, and a baboon; one panel is interpreted as the cosmic egg. The monuments are documented through photography, surveys, plans, and analysis of ornament and design. They are repeatedly used for comparisons with Asia, Hindostan, Chaldeans, Egyptians, and alleged Maya-Egyptian traditions, while the narrator says symbols of Nahualt phallic worship are absent.
