Nile in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Nile flows through Egypt. Its seven mouths, forts, bridges, height, and guarded waters made entry and military movement difficult. Overflowing waters ruined works and obstructed crossings; current, shifting sand, crocodiles, and capture caused severe losses. Its guarded mouths and river-boats ultimately blocked land forces and limited fleet operations.

Nile in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Nile is a river, described as the greatest and most beneficial in the world. Rising from unknown springs near Ethiopia’s utmost borders, it flows south to north through Ethiopia, where the inhabitants of Meroe call it Astapus, and forms islands including Meroe. It makes many turns and channels, then divides near Memphis, flows around the city, and forms Delta before reaching the ocean through seven mouths. Its central importance is its annual increase: it begins to swell at the summer solstice, overflows Egypt during the dog-days, reaches full height after its Ethiopian course, and falls after the autumnal equinox when the Etesian winds have passed. The flooding can drown much of Egypt and cover Memphis’s southern side, but it also brings water, fish, food, nourishment, canes, fruits, beans, fertile soil, slime, and mud. Yearly deposits at its mouths increase Egypt, and the river is said gradually to have formed Egypt; its banks, marshes, islands, and nearby lands support people and animals. Trenches, sluices, canals, dykes, and the lake of Meris later regulate its water and make it more useful. It is also portrayed as defending Egypt with crocodiles. Its names unfold through Oceanus or Oceaines, Eagle after a violent eruption, Egyptus, and Nileus or Nile after a king; Egyptians also call it Oceanus. Hercules is said to have repaired breaches and confined it to its channel.

Nile headwaters in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Nile’s headwaters are unknown to writers and informants. No reliable witness is reported to have seen them. Priests of Egypt attribute them to Ocean; Troglodites to streams from many springs; Meroe’s inhabitants call the Nile Astapus, water issuing from darkness.

No writer is said to have seen the Nile’s headwaters or to have heard a reliable report from anyone who had seen them. The Troglodites say that the Nile is formed from streams issuing from many springs, while the inhabitants of Meroe call it Astapus, signifying water issuing from a place of darkness.
