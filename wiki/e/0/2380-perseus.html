Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Perseus is Danaë’s son and an ancient chieftain who defeats Medusa, carries her head to Seriphos, and brings stony death or grievous doom to the island folk. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Perseus is a son of Jupiter and a Greek hero who makes war against and conquers the Gorgons during Medusa’s reign. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Perseus is a hero and son of Danaë and Jupiter who kills Medusa, rescues and marries Andromeda, defeats Phineus and Prœtus, and turns enemies to stone with Medusa’s head. In The Library, Perseus is Danae’s son who beheads Medusa, rescues Andromeda, turns his enemies to stone, installs Dictys as king, gives Medusa’s head to Athena, and rules Tiryns. In Description of Greece, Perseus is Danaë’s son who kills Medusa, accidentally kills Acrisius, exchanges kingdoms with Megapenthes, and founds Mycenæ. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Perseus is the child of Jupiter and Danae who slays Medusa, rescues and marries Andromeda, defeats Phineus and his followers, and accidentally kills Acrisius.

Perseus in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · 497 BC · the work’s page · read it where Perseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Perseus, Danaë’s son, is presented as an ancient chieftain and hero, reportedly sprung from a living stream of gold. He visits the Hyperborean folk during their sacrifice, then, led by Athene, defeats Medusa, carries her head to Seriphos and Polydektes’ bridal, and brings stony death or grievous doom to island folk.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

X.

Perseus is presented as an ancient chieftain who visited the Hyperborean folk while they sacrificed asses to Apollo. With Athene leading him, he also slew the Gorgon and carried home her serpent-haired head, which brought a stony death to the island folk.

XII.

Perseus struck down Medusa, the third sister, and carried her doom to Seriphos and its people. He also blinded the wondrous brood of Phorkos and brought Medusa's head as a grievous gift to Polydektes' bridal.

Perseus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Perseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Perseus is a hero, youth, son of Danaë and Jupiter or Jove, grandson of Acrisius, and, in the stated descent, descended from Danaë. He is associated with Danaë’s removal to Seriphus and with Polydectes’ care of his education; later, required to bring Polydectes Medusa’s head, he undertakes many toils. Using a reflecting shield associated with Minerva, Talaria, and a Cyllenian falchion, he kills Medusa without being seen, takes her head, and escapes the pursuing Gorgon sisters. The head becomes both proof of his victory and a weapon, though its power to turn enemies to stone is also interpreted as a metaphor for his heroism and the terror of his victory. Carrying it, he travels over Africa and throughout the world. When Atlas refuses hospitality and threatens him, Perseus exposes the head and turns Atlas into a mountain; he also lays it on sea plants beneath the sea, changing sea-weed into coral. In Æthiopia, he kills the sea monster threatening Andromeda, rescues and marries her. At their wedding, attacked by Phineus and his followers, he fights until he uses the head to petrify Phineus. He later enters his native city with Andromeda, attacks Prœtus in defense and vengeance for Danaë, and returns victorious to Polydectes, turning him to stone after finding Danaë and Dictys at the altars of the Deities. Records also credit him with recovering the Golden Fleece and include differing interpretations of him as a fugitive from Argos or an enemy of Andromeda.

Perseus was the son of Danaë and Jupiter, and Acrisius’s predicted killer. After being exposed with Danaë, he was cast ashore near Seriphus, where Polydectes received them and cared for his education; Perseus later killed Medusa and became associated with several interpretations of the Gorgon myth.

opening

Perseus uses a reflecting shield associated with Minerva to see Medusa without being seen, cuts off Medusa’s head, and escapes her pursuing sisters. He later rescues Andromeda from the approaching sea monster.

Perseus is attacked by Phineus and his followers during the wedding feast. He fights them with great valor, kills or wounds many enemies, and uses the Gorgon head when their numbers threaten to overwhelm him. He later petrifies Phineus and enters his native city with Andromeda to attack Prœtus.

notes .. notes

Perseus is described as having an alleged power to turn his enemies into stone by showing them the Gorgon’s head. The explanation treats this power as a metaphor for his heroism and for the terror produced by his victory over the Gorgons.

opening

Perseus is still regarded as having endured many toils and sorrows, though Polydectes denies his achievements. Perseus proves the truth of Medusa’s death by turning Polydectes to stone with Medusa’s face.

Perseus was taken with his mother Danaë to Polydectes’ kingdom by Dictys. He was required to bring Polydectes the head of Medusa, returned victorious, and turned Polydectes into stone after finding his mother and Dictys seeking refuge at the altars of the Deities.

Perseus in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Perseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Perseus, a hero and Danae’s son, was cast into the sea with her, rescued and raised by Dictys, then sent by Polydectes for the Gorgon’s head. Guided by Hermes and Athena and equipped with sickle, shield, wallet, cap, and sandals, he beheaded Medusa. In Ethiopia he killed a sea monster, rescued Andromeda, and fathered her children. Using the head, he turned Phineus’s conspirators and Polydectes’s friends to stone, installed Dictys as king, and gave it to Athena. After accidentally killing Acrisius, he exchanged Argos for Tiryns with Megapenthes, ruled there, and fortified Midea and Mycenae.

Perseus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Perseus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Perseus, Danaë’s son, is a figure credited with killing and beheading Medusa, whose head he displayed to the Greeks. He was said to have warred with Dionysus before becoming friendly with him. At Larissa, while showing skill with quoits, he unintentionally killed his grandfather Acrisius; ashamed, he exchanged kingdoms with Megapenthes and founded Mycenæ. He received greatest honours at Seriphus and honours at a hero-chapel near Mycenæ.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XII.

Perseus unintentionally killed his grandfather Acrisius at Larissa while displaying his skill with quoits. Ashamed of the deed, he exchanged kingdoms with Megapenthes and founded Mycenæ, whose name was explained either by his fallen sword scabbard or by a fungus he found there.

CHAPTER XIX.

Perseus fought and killed Medusa, according to the accounts reported in the unit, and cut off her head to display to the Greeks. A tomb identified as that of the Gorgon-slayer Perseus stood in Argos near the monument associated with Medusa.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Perseus was said to have warred with Dionysus and then become friendly with him. The Argives subsequently honored Dionysus and dedicated an enclosure to him.
