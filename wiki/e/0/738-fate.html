Fate in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Fate is an irrevocable divine ordering that fixes outcomes for cities, peoples, journeys, marriage, rule, death, and the Underworld. It matters most because neither courage, suffering, hostility, sympathy, nor divine intervention can finally overturn what it has decreed, though some events, especially Turnus’s death and the Trojan marriage and peace in Italy, may be delayed. It begins by compelling Troy’s fall, transferring its state to Greek hands, and making the Trojans unable to save it. It then drives Aeneas from Troy, decrees Creusa’s death, and sends him through long travel toward Latium, where he must found a kingdom and restore the Trojan line. Fate reserves a future city, martial race, Ascanius’s succession, Alba Longa, Romulus, and Rome’s unbounded empire. It also regulates access, punishment, and passage in the Underworld. In Latium, it decrees Aeneas’s marriage to Lavinia and Trojan rule through their race, while governing the war, promised armor, and future triumphs. Finally, it fixes the deaths of Pallas, Lausus, Halesus, and ultimately Turnus, whose end can be postponed but not escaped.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK I

Fate fixes the Trojans’ future despite Juno’s hostility and their present sufferings. It guarantees Aeneas’s arrival in Italy, the succession through Ascanius and Alba Longa, the rise of Romulus, and the unbounded empire of Rome.

BOOK II

Fate is said to compel the Greeks’ actions and to determine the destruction of Troy. Aeneas states that Troy might have survived if Heaven had not destined its fall and the Trojans had not been fated to blindness.

“’Twas in the dead of night, when sleep repairs

Pantheus says that the appointed hour has come and that Jove’s irrevocable doom transfers the Trojan state to Greek hands. Aeneas also concludes that courage cannot prevail when heaven’s powers refuse their aid.

“Then, not before, I felt my curdled blood

Fate requires Aeneas to abandon the defense of Troy and undertake a long journey over land and sea. Creusa’s ghost declares that fate will bring him to Latium, where he will establish a kingdom and restore the Trojan line.

BOOK IV

Fate has assigned Aeneas a future city and a martial race in Latian or Ausonian lands. Jupiter's message says that Aeneas must not delay in Libya because Ascanius and Rome's imperial name are owed their promised future by fate.

Ye realms, yet unreveal’d to human sight,

The Fates determine the terms of passage and punishment in the Underworld. The Sibyl says that fate forbids the unburied Palinurus from crossing and that the Fates allow Aeneas and her to continue toward Pluto’s palace.

BOOK VII

The Fates decree that a foreign prince will come to Latium, marry Lavinia, and establish a race destined to rule widely. Juno acknowledges that the Trojan race is fated to reign in Italy but intends to delay the marriage and peace.

Now night had shed her silver dews around,

Fate has decreed that Aeneas’s race will rule in Italy and has fixed the end of Troy. It also governs the promised armor, the coming war, and the future Roman triumphs shown on Aeneas’s shield.

But, in another part, th’ Arcadian horse

Fate governs the deaths of Pallas, Lausus, and Halesus and determines that Turnus’s life can be delayed but not ultimately saved. Jupiter insists that even divine power cannot alter the destined limits of mortal life. Turnus’s temporary escape by ship therefore does not remove his fate.

Fortune in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Fortune is a changeful, unstable power that governs human affairs and life through judgments linked to Fate, the gods, and Zeus’s ordination. It reverses prosperity and suffering: no one remains permanently blessed or afflicted, and it can take away all former glory in a day. Its force makes human plans and boasts vain, assigns harsh destinies in place of hoped-for marriages, and shapes Menelaus’s fortunes and delayed return. Yet its changes also leave room for hope that suffering may alter, though fate and necessity cannot be prevented. From Troy’s ruin and disputed divine causes to Peleus’s imposed suffering and humanity’s debt of death, Fortune unfolds as the source of unexpected reversals under divine guidance.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHORUS.

Fate is said to fulfill the commands of the immortal gods and to weigh the curse upon Priam’s house and Troy. Hecuba also reflects that human plans and boasts are vain because fortune can overturn prosperity.

HERCULES DISTRACTED.

Amphitryon hopes that fortune may change and save them, saying that storms and prosperity do not last forever. Megara counters that what fate ordains cannot be prevented and that resisting necessity may be folly.

MEGARA, AMPHITRYON, CHORUS.

Changeful Fortune has assigned the Destinies as brides to Megara’s children, replacing the marriages she had planned. Amphitryon says that Fortune has taken all his former glory from him in a single day.

MENELAUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Helen attributes her actions to Venus and to Heaven’s decrees, claiming that her crime was compelled and therefore venial. Hecuba rejects this explanation, while the Chorus asks whether Jove will hear Troy’s lament and questions the divine cause of its destruction.

MENELAUS.

Fate is said to govern human fortunes and to prevent Menelaus from returning easily to Sparta. Menelaus accepts that the gods’ decrees and Fate have shaped his sufferings, while the messenger emphasizes that fortune can suddenly change.

PELEUS, CHORUS.

Thetis says that Peleus must endure the suffering imposed by fate because Zeus has ordained it, and that death is a debt owed by all humanity. The Chorus concludes that the gods often bring about what mortals least expect while still guiding events through life’s perplexity.

fate and the Destinies in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Fate and the Destinies are an uncontrollable divine force governing human fortunes, events, prophecy, and outcomes beyond human power. They turn intended marriages into deathly unions: for Megara, they are brides given to Hercules’ sons, bringing tears rather than happiness. They prevent Ulysses and Diomede from killing Hector or Paris, ordain the deaths of Rhesus and his allies, and reserve Achilles for an inevitable shaft. They can overturn noble birth into slavery, as in the sufferings of Hecuba and Andromache. Through divine councils, including Jove’s, they determine events such as Helen’s departure and ensure that the gods produce unexpected, perplexing results.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

Fate in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Fate is an irresistible, inevitable, unseen power over mortals, gods, and the rise and fall of all things. It governs human lives, assigning sufferings, honours, joys, woes, bereavement, good, and evil; no earth-born person can escape its nets, and even Zeus and the Immortals bow to or sanction its deeds. It determines death, including Paris’s death by Philoctetes, and shapes connected outcomes: Oenone’s refusal or capacity to save Paris, Paris’s path to her, and Troy’s coming destruction. Fate also directs wider war and reversal: it leads the Argives toward Troy, assigns the courses of Achilles’ horses and Neoptolemus’s later service, governs battle deaths and the destinies of Aeneas and Eurymachus, and forbids Aeneas to continue fighting outside Troy. It raises obscure people, lowers the exalted, afflicts or exalts people without personal blame, and is indifferent to the doom of people and cities. By the end, it is stronger than the Immortals, misleading and bringing destruction toward the Argives despite prayer.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

Fate is described as an irresistible power hovering over all mortals and as something that even the gods cannot restrain. The Fates have also assigned the course of Achilles' immortal horses and the later service of Neoptolemus.

BOOK VII

Fate is said to assign bereavement to all people and to distribute good and evil unpredictably. Achilles' son maintains that no one dies in war beyond destiny and accepts that he should die if his fate is to serve his country.

At last by Calchas' counsel Achaea's sons

The Fates are described as unseen powers who afflict or exalt human beings and devise their joys and woes. Odysseus and Diomede told Philoctetes that the Fates, rather than any individual among the Achaeans, were responsible for his affliction.

BOOK X

Fate determines that Paris is to die by Philoctetes’ hands and that only Oenone can save him if she chooses. Fate also leads Paris to Oenone, shapes Oenone’s doom, and governs the coming destruction of Troy.

BOOK XI

Fate governs the deaths and reversals in the battle, including the long destinies of Aeneas and Eurymachus and the destruction of many warriors. It forbids Aeneas to keep fighting outside Troy and is described as indifferent to the doom of people and cities.

Then also Menelaus in Helen's bower

Fate oversees the works of men, raises the obscure to fame, and brings down the exalted. Its nets surround the Trojans, and no one can escape them; even the might of Zeus must bow to Fate.

BOOK XIV.

Fate is described as stronger than the Immortals, with Zeus sanctioning its deeds. It is blamed for misleading the Argives and for bringing destruction toward them despite their prayers.

Fate in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Fate is an unavoidable force that determines and binds Oedipus’s life, overcoming even gods and resisting human efforts to escape it. Attempts to avoid prophecies instead bring about their fulfillment: Oedipus’s birth is revealed, and his parricide and marriage occur. Oedipus sees his rescue from death as serving a predestined doom, yet accepts what fate gives and later frames his suffering as imposed through ignorance and divine direction. The Chorus treats his fall as fate’s work, while Creon’s resistance ends in ruin and a crushing recognition of Fate’s power.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

[Enter JOCASTA.]

The Chorus presents Oedipus’s fall as the work of fate and declares that no mortal should be called blessed before life has ended. Events that seemed to defeat the prophecy instead lead to its fulfillment through Oedipus’s birth, parricide, and marriage.

[Enter SECOND MESSENGER.]

Oedipus believes that he was predestined for an awful doom and that he was rescued from death only because fate required him to suffer it. He says he will accept whatever fate deals him.

OEDIPUS AT COLONUS

Oedipus says that suffering, long years, and noble character have taught him endurance. He presents his fate as something imposed through ignorance and divine direction, insisting that he is more sinned against than sinning.

CHORUS.

The Chorus describes fate as having held Oedipus bound. Oedipus presents his marriage and its consequences as the suffering into which he was brought unknowingly.

[Enter TEIRESIAS and BOY]

Creon recognizes that Fate is difficult to resist and that his attempt to fight it has brought ruin. At the end, he feels the crushing weight of Fate and asks to be taken away.
