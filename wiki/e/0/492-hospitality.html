hospitality in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Hospitality is a lasting disposition marked by being hospitable and liberal. Admetus practices it toward Hercules by insisting that he enter and be entertained despite Alcestis’s death, concealing the truth and separating the guest from the mourning household. Though Hercules later criticizes the concealment, he judges Admetus’s generosity noble and repays it by restoring Alcestis.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHOR. And I indeed with thee, as a friend with a friend, will bear this

Admetus insists that Hercules must enter the house and be entertained even though Alcestis has died. He hides the full truth from Hercules, separates the guest chamber from the mourning household, and says that refusing a guest would make his house inhospitable.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Admetus continues to offer Hercules hospitality despite the household's grief and Alcestis's death. Hercules regards this generosity as noble and resolves to repay Admetus by attempting to restore Alcestis.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

Admetus receives Heracles into his house despite his grief and conceals Alcestis's death so that Heracles will not be burdened with sorrow while visiting another host. Heracles criticizes him for this concealment but ultimately repays his hospitality by restoring Alcestis.

hospitality in The Odyssey

Homer · -0632~/-0599~

Hospitality is the provision and proper treatment of strangers and guests. It is shown when Eumaeus feeds respectable visitors, Piraeus promises care for Theoclymenus, Aethon claims to have aided Ulysses and his men, and Penelope orders comfort for a stranger. It also marks Ulysses’s former prosperity, while the suitors’ abuse of Ulysses’s absent house violates it during their contest for Penelope.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Meanwhile Ulysses and the swineherd were eating their supper in the

Eumaeus provides food and drink for respectable strangers who come to his station. Telemachus entrusts Theoclymenus to Piraeus, who promises that the stranger will lack nothing in hospitality.

BOOK XIX

Ulysses's invented Cretan identity includes a story of showing Ulysses and his companions generous hospitality, feeding them and providing wine and oxen while they waited for favorable winds. Penelope also insists that the stranger be treated properly with a seat, a fleece, and care for the night.

BOOK XXI

The suitors are accused of persistently abusing the hospitality of the house while its owner is absent. Antinous acknowledges their presence as a contest for Penelope, but continues directing their attempts to use Ulysses’s bow.
