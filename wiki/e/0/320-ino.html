  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Ino becomes Athamas’s wife, bears Learchus and Melicerta, causes famine at Thebes, is driven mad, flees with Melicerta, and is transformed by Neptune into Leucothoë, later called Matuta.

  • In The Library, Ino is Cadmus and Harmonia’s daughter, Athamas’s second wife, and mother of Learchus and Melicertes; she plots Phrixus’s sacrifice and later plunges into the sea with her dead child, becoming known as Leucothoe.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Leucothea, formerly Ino, becomes a sea goddess and, with Palaemon, saves sailors from shipwreck.

Leucothoë in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Leucothoë is chiefly a maiden and Nymph, daughter of Eurynome and Orchamus, who grows more beautiful than her mother and is attended by twelve handmaids. The Sun, captivated by her, approaches disguised as Eurynome, reveals himself, and embraces her. After Clytie reveals the affair, Orchamus buries her alive; the grieving Sun transforms her body into a frankincense tree. An explanatory account connects her with Persia, while another identifies Leucothoë as Ino’s new sea-deity name after Neptune transforms her.

Leucothoë is the daughter of Eurynome and Orchamus, who rules the Achæmenian cities. The Sun becomes captivated by her beauty, disguises himself as her mother to approach her, and, after revealing his true form, wins her submission to his embrace.

opening

Leucothoë is buried alive by her fierce and unrelenting father after Clytie reveals her affair with the Sun. The Sun grieves for her and transforms her body into a frankincense tree.

Leucothoë is presented in the story as the daughter of Orchamus who fell in love with Apollo and was buried alive by her father. The explanation suggests that she may have symbolized the frankincense tree introduced into Persia.

opening

Leucothoë is the new divine name given to Ino when Neptune transforms her after the fall into the sea.

Ino in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Ino, daughter of Cadmus and sister of Semele, becomes Athamas’s wife after Nephele and bears Learchus and Melicerta. She opposes Nephele’s children, orders their sacrifice, and, according to the myth, causes the famine at Thebes. Poisoned by Tisiphone and driven mad, she flees after Athamas kills Learchus, carrying Melicerta. She calls on Bacchus, leaps into the sea, and Neptune transforms her into Leucothoë, later called Matuta at Rome.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Ino is poisoned by Tisiphone and affected by madness. After Athamas kills Learchus, she flees with Melicerta, calls on Bacchus, and leaps from a rock into the sea; Neptune transforms her into the sea deity Leucothoë.

notes .. notes

Ino was the daughter of Cadmus and the second wife of Athamas. She opposed Nephele's children, caused the seed to be parched during the famine at Thebes, fled with Melicerta from Athamas, and threw herself into the sea. She was later worshipped as the sea deity Leucothoë, and the Romans called her Matuta.

Ino in The Library

Apollodorus · 0070~/0200~

Ino, daughter of Cadmus and Harmonia, becomes Athamas’s second wife and mother of Learchus and Melicertes. She plots to have Phrixus sacrificed by parching wheat and bribing Delphi’s messengers. Later she plunges into the sea with her dead child and is known as Leucothoe.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

IX. Τῶν δὲ Αἰόλον παίδων ᾿Αθάμας, Βοιωτίας

Ino is Athamas’s second wife and the mother of Learchus and Melicertes. She plots against Phrixus and Helle by persuading the women to parch the wheat, then bribes the messengers from Delphi to claim that Phrixus must be sacrificed. Later, she throws herself and Melicertes into the sea.

THE LIBRARY, 111. 10

Ino was a daughter of Cadmus and Harmonia and married Athamas. She later became known as Leucothoe after plunging into the sea with her dead child.
