  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Hecuba is the former queen of Troy and an aged captive who grieves her city and family, opposes Helen, performs burial rites for Astyanax, and departs for slavery.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Hecuba is a former Trojan queen who becomes an aged captive slave, mourns her family, performs funeral rites, and avenges Polydore by killing Polymestor’s sons and blinding him.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Hecuba is a former Trojan queen and aged captive slave who fails to save Polyxena, prepares her body for burial, and leads the Trojan women in avenging Polydore’s murder.
  • In The Iliad, Hecuba is Priam’s wife and Hector’s mother who laments Hector, fears for Priam’s safety, and continues mourning her sons.
  • In The Library, Hecuba is Dymas’s daughter and Priam’s second wife, mother of many children, who becomes a bitch after Troy’s fall and is buried at Bitch’s Tomb.

Hecuba in The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · -0414

Hecuba is the former queen of Troy, wife of Priam, mother of Hector, Paris, Cassandra, and Polyxena, and grandmother of Astyanax. After Troy falls, she is an old, physically weakened captive, allotted as Odysseus's slave. Her story unfolds through grief for her destroyed city, lost royal life, dead family, and the enslavement or loss of those closest to her. She gathers the captive Trojan women and fears their futures; then learns Cassandra, Polyxena, Andromache, and she herself have been assigned to Greek masters or fates. She mourns Priam, her children, Troy, and Astyanax, while urging Andromache to preserve hope through the child. She cannot save him. Hecuba also opposes Helen, urging Menelaus to execute her as punishment for betrayal and Troy's suffering. When Astyanax's body is brought to her, she performs burial rites and laments his violent death. Hecuba in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Hecuba is a former queen of Troy, Ilion, and Phrygia; Priam’s former wife; Cisseus’s daughter; and mother of Hector, Paris, Cassandra, Polyxena, Polydore, Astyanax, and other Trojan royal children. After Troy’s destruction, Priam is slain, Hector dies in the war, and Hecuba becomes an aged, physically decrepit captive and slave without city or children. She is allotted to Ulysses, while Cassandra is assigned to Agamemnon and Andromache to Achilles’ son. Despite her loss and enslavement, she reflects on the constancy of virtue and moral character. Her suffering centers on the deaths and threatened deaths of her family. A foreboding dream concerns her children. She appeals to Ulysses to spare Polyxena, but Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles’ tomb; Hecuba praises her courage and orders her body protected and washed. She mourns Priam, Hector, Polyxena, Polydore, Cassandra’s captivity, Astyanax, her city, and her lost royal status. She advises Andromache to submit to Neoptolemus and preserve Astyanax, then learns the Greeks have ordered the boy killed. After mourning his mutilated body, she adorns him with the poor gifts available and performs his funeral rites before being taken into slavery under Ulysses. Hecuba also confronts Helen, accusing her of willingly following Paris for beauty and Troy’s wealth, rejecting advice to flee, and remaining proud rather than remorseful; she urges Menelaus to kill her. When Polydore is found dead, Hecuba identifies him as her son and asks Agamemnon to punish Polymestor for murder and denying burial. She deceives Polymestor with a promise to reveal Priam’s buried treasure. Assisted by Trojan women, she kills Polymestor’s sons and blinds him, then defends the revenge before Agamemnon, who accepts that Polymestor deserves punishment. Finally, Bacchus’s prophecy destines her to become a dog, and her tomb is known by that transformed form.

Hecuba is Priam’s wife and Hector’s mother, defined above all by grief and fear for her family. When Hector is dishonoured, she tears her hair, casts off her veil, leads Trojan lamentation, and mourns him as Troy’s strength. She fears Priam’s ransom journey to Achilles and urges him to stay, but ultimately supports him with wine and prayer. She continues mourning Hector, recalling the suffering of her other sons.
