Parnassus

mountain · 5 works · 8 quoted facts · 9 connections
Also called: Mount Parnassus, mountain

Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Parnassus is the mountain beneath whose glades Apollo establishes his temple at Crisa, and to whose ridge the Cretans follow Apollo to reach their sanctuary. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Parnassus is a lofty, shady mountain with two peaks that remains uncovered during Deucalion’s deluge, where Deucalion, Pyrrha, and some subjects flee, rest, and remain until the waters abate. In The Library, Mount Parnassus receives Deucalion and Pyrrha after the Flood when their chest lands there after the rains stop. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Parnassus is the namesake of Mount Parnassus; after Deucalion’s flood, surviving humans follow wild beasts to its summit, and it contains the Corycian cavern and hosts Thyiades’ revels for Dionysus and Apollo. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Parnassus is the only mountain said to overtop the floodwaters, where Deucalion and Pyrrha find refuge.

Parnassus is a lofty, shady mountain with two peaks, rising beyond the clouds. During Deucalion’s deluge, its top alone remained uncovered as the sea covered other places. Deucalion, Pyrrha, and some subjects fled there, rested there, and remained until the waters abated.

Parnassus is described in Ovid’s account as the only mountain whose top remained uncovered by the flood. The explanation suggests that this tradition probably referred to Mount Ararat instead.

opening

Parnassus is a lofty mountain with two tops that rises toward the stars and extends its summit beyond the clouds. Deucalion and Pyrrha come to rest there after the sea covers all other places.

Abstract written by a model

Parnassus is the namesake of Mount Parnassus. After Deucalion’s flood, surviving humans followed wild beasts to its summit; the mountain, sixty stades from Delphi, contains the Corycian cavern, rises above clouds and Ambrosus, and hosts Thyiades’ revels for Dionysus and Apollo.

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

Mount Parnassus is said to have been named after Parnassus. After Deucalion’s flood, the human beings who escaped it followed wolves and other wild beasts to the top of Mount Parnassus.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Mount Parnassus is about 60 stades from Delphi by the road leading toward it, and the Corycian cavern lies on its slopes. Its heights are higher than the clouds, and the Thyiades conduct their mad revels there in honour of Dionysus and Apollo.

CHAPTER XXXVI.

Mount Parnassus lies above Ambrosus.
