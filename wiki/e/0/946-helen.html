  • In Heroides and Amores, Helen is Menelaus’s lawful Spartan wife whose beauty attracts Paris, while she struggles between desire and fidelity before leaving for Troy.
  • In The Aeneid, Helen is an adulterous wife and alleged bane of Greece and Troy who hides in Vesta’s temple during Troy’s destruction and is spared by Venus.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Helen is Jupiter and Leda’s young daughter, abducted by Theseus and Perithous, kept at Aphidna, and recovered as a virgin by Castor and Pollux.
  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Helen is Menelaus’s Spartan wife, carried to Troy by Paris, held captive after its fall, and condemned by Hecuba and Menelaus despite pleading compulsion and force.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Helen is a Spartan royal woman, Menelaus’s wife, who is blamed for Troy’s destruction, defends herself, and in an alternative account escapes Egypt with Menelaus.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Helen is Menelaus’s wife whose abduction by Paris prompts the Trojan War; after Troy, she vanishes from Orestes’s attack and becomes an immortal goddess.
  • In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Helen is the bride whose infatuated departure from Sparta with Paris brings the Trojan War and widespread grief.
  • In The Odyssey, Helen is Menelaus’s lawful wife who recognizes Odysseus and Telemachus, recounts Odysseus’s disguise in Troy, and prepares beds for the guests.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Helen is Menelaus’s wife who leaves Sparta with Alexandrus for Ilium, helps the Greeks capture the city, marries Deiphobus, and is recovered by Menelaus.
  • In The Iliad, Helen is Menelaus’s former wife and Paris’s wife in Troy, lamenting her former life and reproaching Paris while the war is fought over her.
  • In The Library, Helen is Zeus’s daughter, abducted by Theseus, married to Menelaus, carried off by Alexander, and involved in the Trojan War before going with Menelaus to the Elysian Fields.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Helen is Menelaus’s wife who is ravished and carried away by Paris, prompting the Greeks to assemble against Troy.
  • In Description of Greece, Helen is a figure associated with competing traditions concerning her burial at Therapne, her death and worship in Rhodes, and her marriage to Achilles on Leuce.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Helen is a woman seated with Eurybates and her handmaids as she agrees to Demophon’s request for Æthra.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Helen is Menelaus’s Spartan wife whose presence causes the battle for Troy; after Paris’s death she is nearly killed by Menelaus but leaves Troy with him after their reconciliation.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Helen is Menelaus’s beautiful wife who elopes with Paris, is recovered after Troy’s fall, and returns with Menelaus to Sparta.

Helen in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Helen is a woman of Spartan and Taenarian identity, born in rural Therapnae and from Eurotas, daughter of Leda, a Tyndarid, and descendant of Tyndareus, Pelops, and Tantalus. She is Menelaus’s lawful wife, initially marked by marital fidelity, virtue, chastity, a blameless reputation, and Menelaus’s trust. Previously abducted by Theseus against her will, she remained untouched and was attended by Aethra and Clymene. Her exceptional beauty makes her, to Paris, the greatest prize and the object of a desire that began before he saw her; Venus favors her and promises her to Paris. When Paris arrives in Lacedaemon, he presses her to become his wife, meet him secretly, and join him in Ilion, where he predicts royal, almost divine honors. Helen condemns his betrayal of hospitality and defends her honor, though she admits his attractiveness and the effect of his attention. She remains fearful and undecided, weighing desire against disgrace, abandonment, violence, and separation from family and support. Oenone later calls her an adulteress, says Paris brought her to Troy, and doubts her fidelity; she is carried from Eurotas in a Phrygian ship and promised a royal welcome in Ilion.

Oenone Paridi 2

Helen is described as the Greek woman whom Paris brought to Troy after leaving Oenone. Oenone calls her an adulteress who had previously been carried away by Theseus and argues that she cannot be trusted to remain faithful.

XVI

Helen is addressed as Leda's daughter and Tyndaris. Paris says that he desired her before seeing her, finds her beauty greater than reported, and asks her to accept his love and fulfill his hopes.

nun ego coniugium generosae degener opto,

Helen is urged by Paris to become his wife and to meet him secretly at night. Paris praises her extraordinary beauty, predicts that she will be honored like a goddess in Troy, and asks her not to reject a Phrygian husband.

XVII

Helen condemns Paris for abusing the hospitality shown to him and for trying to undermine her marital fidelity. She maintains that she has lived without reproach, rejects his advances, and says she will either preserve her unstained reputation or follow Paris for his sake rather than for his gifts. She admits that Paris is handsome and that his attention affects her, but she refuses to surrender her honor.

Prima mea est igitur Veneri plaeuisse voluptas ;

Helen says that she first pleased Venus and then seemed to Paris a greater prize than the honours of Pallas or Juno. She is attracted to Paris but remains inexperienced, fearful, and uncertain whether to yield to the affair or resist it. She fears disgrace, abandonment, violence, and the loss of her family and support if she follows Paris to Troy.

Helen in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Helen is a Spartan and Greek royal woman, daughter of Leda and Tyndarus and also identified as daughter of Jove or Zeus, sister of Castor and Pollux, wife of Menelaus, and mother of Hermione. Renowned as the fairest bride, she is central to conflicting accounts of the Trojan disaster. In one account she is a captive among Trojan women after the Greeks' victory, hated and accused of adultery, abduction, and causing the deaths and destruction associated with Troy. Dragged from the tents, she defends herself before Hecuba and Menelaus: Venus, Paris, Priam, and divine compulsion caused her departure, and Deiphobus forced her into marriage after Paris died. Menelaus nevertheless orders her taken to Greece for execution. Another account makes her guiltless: Juno sent an image, Helen’s eidolon, to Troy, while Hermes took the true Helen to Egypt to preserve her chastity. She never visits Troy, yet her name is slandered throughout Greece. In Egypt she lives as a suppliant near Proteus’s tomb, protected by its refuge, faithful to Menelaus, and pursued by Theoclymenus, who seeks to marry her. Hearing that Menelaus may be dead, she mourns, fears for her reputation and family, and considers suicide. Menelaus arrives, learns of the phantom, and is reunited with her when it vanishes. Helen then faces danger from Theoclymenus and warns Menelaus that Theonoe’s brother may kill him. She appeals to Theonoe for secrecy, refuses to become the king’s bride, and devises their escape: she pretends Menelaus has died, performs mourning and funeral rites, and obtains a ship and sailors for a supposed funeral voyage. She and her living husband board the Sidonian ship and escape Egypt by sea. Castor and Pollux declare that she will later be honored as a goddess. Later descriptions again condemn her as having fled with Paris and caused the Greek expedition, while Menelaus maintains that the gods, not her consent, plunged her into suffering and that sparing her was wise.

Helen in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Helen is a daughter of Zeus, variously by Leda or Nemesis, and in the Nemesis tradition is born from an egg. As a twelve-year-old, she is abducted by Theseus, with Pirithous, and taken from Sparta to Aphidnae or Athens; Pollux and Castor later recover her. She becomes the object of many Greek heroes’ suit, and Tyndareus chooses Menelaus as her bridegroom. She marries Menelaus and has Hermione and, according to some, Nicostratus. After Menelaus departs for Crete, Alexander persuades Helen to leave Sparta or carries her off. Her removal prompts the Greek expedition: the Greeks prepare to campaign in the second year after her rape, and their embassy demands her restoration, which the Trojans refuse. In an alternative account, Hermes takes the real Helen to Egypt at Zeus’s will, while Alexander brings a cloud-made phantom to Troy. During the war, Helen recognizes Odysseus when he enters the Trojan city disguised as a beggar and helps him steal the Palladium. She also walks around the Wooden Horse, calling to the Greek leaders in imitations of their wives’ voices. Afterward Menelaus kills Deiphobus, takes Helen to the ships, and, in accounts locating her with Proteus in Egypt, finds her there and returns with her to Sparta. Finally, Hera makes Menelaus immortal, and Helen goes with him to the Elysian Fields.

Helen in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Helen is a figure linked to competing local traditions. She is mother of Iphigenia and is associated with Sparta, Therapne, Rhodes, and Leuce. The Lacedæmonians say she was buried with Menelaus at Therapne; the Rhodians say that, after Menelaus died, Nicostratus and Megapenthes drove her away and Polyxo had her hanged, leading to worship of Helen Hung on the Tree. On Leuce she married Achilles, though another account disputes Achilles as her suitor.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIX.

Helen is said by the Lacedæmonians to be buried with Menelaus at Therapne. The Rhodians say that after Menelaus’s death she was driven away by Nicostratus and Megapenthes, and that Polyxo had her seized and hanged by attendants dressed as Furies; for this reason the Rhodians worship Helen Hung on the Tree. On Leuce, Helen is said to have married Achilles and to have sent Leonymus to tell Stesichorus that his blindness was punishment from her.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Helen was the woman whom the local tradition said Achilles sought to marry from Tyndareus. The text argues that Achilles could hardly have been one of her suitors, citing Homer’s account of Achilles’ relation to the sons of Atreus and the age of Theseus.

Helen in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Helen is a woman of Troy, from Sparta, wife of Menelaus, child of Zeus, and famed as all-flawless in beauty. Her presence and wealth cause the battle for Troy, and Troy suffers for her sake. Blamed for abandoning Menelaus and their marriage-bed, she remains in Troy, marvels at Eurypylus, and after Paris dies publicly laments while fearing for herself and her guilt. When Menelaus enters her bower, she hides as he kills Deiphobus and nearly kills her, but Aphrodite eases his jealousy and his brother defends her. She leaves Troy veiled in shame and fear, says she was taken against her will, and is forgiven as love with Menelaus is renewed.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK II

Helen remains in Troy while the city suffers for her sake. The Trojans are too afraid of Paris and Helen to openly support Polydamas's proposal that she and her wealth be returned to the Danaans.

BOOK VI

Helen is blamed by Menelaus for the war and for abandoning their home and marriage-bed. She is described as goddess-like in beauty and marvels at Eurypylus when he is brought to Paris's palace.

BOOK X

Helen is identified as the woman for whom Paris was doomed to die. After Paris’s death, she laments publicly but is portrayed as thinking chiefly of her own danger and guilt.

Then also Menelaus in Helen's bower

Helen flees and hides in the palace while Menelaus kills Deiphobus. Menelaus nearly kills her, but Aphrodite causes his jealousy to fade, and his brother argues that Helen was not responsible for the wrong done to the guest-lord.

BOOK XIV.

Helen leaves Troy in shame and fear, following Menelaus with her head veiled. She tells Menelaus that she was taken against her will, and he forgives her; they renew their love.

Helen in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Helen, the fairest woman of her sex and Menelaus’s wife, elopes with Paris to Troy as Venus has destined. After Troy falls, Menelaus recovers her; she is said to have secretly aided the Greeks. Following storms and travels, she returns with Menelaus to Sparta, where they resume royal status.

Chapter XX

Helen is Menelaus’s wife and is described as the fairest woman of her sex. Venus has destined her for Paris, who persuades her to elope with him and carries her to Troy.

Chapter XXI

Helen was recovered by Menelaus after Troy fell and was said to have aided the Greeks secretly, including Ulysses and Diomedes in their attempt to take the Palladium. She and Menelaus endured storms and travels before returning to Sparta, where they resumed their royal status.
