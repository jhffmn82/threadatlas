Abstract written by a model

In Heroides and Amores, the parrot is prized for its beauty and human-voice mimicry, then mourned after its death and commemorated with a tomb. In The Amores, or Amours, the parrot is given to Corinna, distinguished by its appearance and mimicry, then mourned and commemorated after dying.

parrot in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where parrot first appears

Abstract written by a model

The parrot comes from India and is prized for rare beauty, a Punic-red and saffron beak, human-voice mimicry, peaceful companionship, and a frugal diet of nuts, poppy-seeds, and water. After dying, it bids Corinna farewell, is mourned by birds and Corinna, joins pious birds in Elysium, and receives a body-sized tomb.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

VI

The parrot, brought from India, dies after being praised for its rare coloring, red-and-yellow beak, skill at imitating human speech, peaceful nature, and frugal diet. It is mourned by birds and by Corinna, and its dying tongue cries, “Corinna, vale!” It is received among the pious birds in Elysium, where its speech attracts them, and its tombstone says that it pleased its mistress and spoke better than a bird.

parrot in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where parrot first appears

Abstract written by a model

The parrot, from the Indians of the East, was given to Corinna. Its green plumage, purple beak, skill at imitating human voice, peaceful habits, and modest diet distinguished it. After dying with “Corinna, farewell!”, it became the speaker’s subject of mourning, was received by birds in the Elysian grove, and was commemorated by a small sepulchre.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

ELEGY VI.

The parrot, sent from the Indians of the East, was given to Corinna and became the speaker's subject of mourning after its death. It had beautiful plumage, a purple beak, a talent for imitating human speech, peaceful habits, and a diet of nuts, poppies, and pure water. As it died, it cried, "Corinna, farewell!" and was imagined among innocent birds at the foot of the Elysian hill, where a small sepulchre commemorated its devotion and eloquence.
