Morocco in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

Morocco is an independent Mussulman kingdom in North Africa, part of Barbary and ruled by the Shereefian dynasty, whose princes claim descent through Ali and Fatima. Also called El-Gharb, Mogrel-el-Aksa, and sometimes the Country of the Sultan Muley Abd Errahman, it is bounded by the Mediterranean and Straits of Gibraltar in the north, the Atlantic in the west, southern deserts and the Sahara in the south, and Algeria, the Atlas, and Tafilett in the east. Its history is presented through Saracen-founded monarchies, civil war after Yezeed’s death, later rule by Muley Suleiman and Muley Abd Errahman, and relations with European powers involving diplomacy, trade, piracy, slavery, and treaties. The country is a large central ridge, crossed northeast to southwest by the Atlas Mountains, with slopes toward the Atlantic and rivers toward the Sahara. Its climate and soil resemble Spain and Portugal: northern provinces are more like Spain, while southern ones have less rain and more desert heat. Although it has extremes of heat and cold and much desolate, uncultivated land, it is described as among Africa’s healthiest countries. Large rivers are numerous but not navigable far inland; northern rivers are short and full, and southern rivers are longer and often nearly dry. The coast has few, poorly protected ports. Agriculture centers on wheat and barley, with irrigated vegetables, fruits, forests of Argan and oak, cork, cedar, date-palm, and palmetto. Resources include silver, copper, sulphur, salt, amethyst, and materials used for fine leathers. Morocco is divided into Fez, central Morocco, Tafilett, and Sous, as well as natural regions and central districts. Its society includes Jews, Moors, Berbers, Arabs, Amazirghs, Touaricks, Israelites, and Renegades, under unequal social and religious conditions. The city of Morocco, or Maraksh, built by Yousel-Ben-Tashfin at the foot of the Atlas, is a southern capital and Sultan’s residence with mosques, markets, gardens, caravan trade, and visible decay. Commerce exports agricultural products and raw materials but is said capable of greater growth under more liberal government and lower duties.

Narrative, section by section

11 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

Morocco is presented as a country where Jews, Moors, Berbers, and Arabs live under sharply unequal social and religious conditions. The unit discusses Moroccan marriage customs, superstition, intoxication, imperial policy, and the position of Jewish communities.

CHAPTER II.

Morocco is described as an immense remnant of the monarchies created by the Saracens and as the only remaining independent Mussulman kingdom founded by the Saracen conquest of North Africa. Its present Shereefian dynasty claims descent from the Prophet through Ali and Fatima and has ruled the country for more than three centuries.

Morocco is described as an empire divided by civil war after Yezeed’s death and later governed by Muley Suleiman and Muley Abd Errahman. Its relations with European powers, especially England and France, are discussed through diplomacy, trade, piracy, slavery, and treaties.

CHAPTER III.

Morocco is also called El-Gharb, or Mogrel-el-Aksa, meaning the West or Far West, and is sometimes called the Country of the Sultan Muley Abd Errahman. It is bounded by the Mediterranean and Straits of Gibraltar to the north, the Atlantic to the west, the Sahara and southern deserts to the south, and Algeria, the Atlas, and Tafilett to the east.

Morocco is described as one of the healthiest countries in Africa, except perhaps Tunis, because of its Atlantic exposure, Atlas elevations, varied terrain, and lack of extensive marshes. Its surface consists largely of a central ridge with slopes toward the Atlantic and the Sahara, and its coasts have few well-protected ports.

CHAPTER IV.

Morocco is described as having historically been divided into the kingdoms or provinces of Morocco and Fez, with Tafilett and sometimes Sous attached to Morocco. Its modern divisions include northern Fez, central Morocco, eastern Tafilett, and southern Sous, while its practical administration is based chiefly on tribes and kaidats. Its coastal towns, interior regions, and natural resources are surveyed, although reliable population information is difficult to obtain.

CHAPTER V.

Morocco, or Maraksh, is the capital of the South and is sometimes called the capital of the Empire, though it is described as a diminished shadow of its former greatness. It has extensive walls, mosques, markets, gardens, an Imperial palace, and caravan trade, but its streets and buildings are decayed and its learning is limited. Its population estimates range from about thirty thousand to one hundred thousand.

CHAPTER VI.

The unit surveys Morocco's interior towns and cities, including regions of the Kingdom of Fez, Sous, Draha, and Tafilett. It describes Morocco as politically weakened by independent or semi-independent tribes and as having poorly documented geography and population.

CHAPTER VII.

A great part of Morocco is described as having a desolate appearance, with uncultivated land and sparse vegetation. The narrator says that European visitors generally adopt Moorish habits rather than promoting Christian intellectual pleasures.

APPENDIX.

Morocco supplied Tangier, Mogador, Rabat, and Mazagan with agricultural products and raw materials for export, while receiving manufactured goods and provisions. The text states that Moroccan commerce could expand greatly under a more liberal government and lower duties.

Empire of Morocco in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

The Empire of Morocco is a country in North Africa, also called El-Gharb, Mogrel-el-Aksa, and Blad Muley Abd Errahman. It has about six million people, a Sultan who heads the orthodox religion of Western Muslims, a military force, and annual commerce. Its effective dependent territory is estimated at 137,400 square miles, though its wider claims are often nominal or precarious. It includes Sous, Wadnoun, Rif, Atlas, and Tafilett, and claims Touat and Western Sahara. Its royal cities, El-Kesar, Mequinez, Fez, and Morocco, are commercially active but divided, decayed, and poorly defended.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER III.

The Empire of Morocco is presented as having either a restricted effective territory or a much wider sphere of nominal influence. Its dependent territory is estimated at about 137,400 square miles, while its population is approximately six million; its army, commerce, and authority over distant provinces are described as limited and uneven.

CHAPTER V.

The Maroquine Empire contains the royal cities of El-Kesar, Mequinez, Fez, and Morocco, together with Tafilett and its surrounding districts. Its principal cities are portrayed as politically divided, commercially active, and weakened by decay and inadequate defenses.
