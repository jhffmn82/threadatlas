Hesiod in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Hesiod is presented as a Greek, Aeolic-speaking poet of Ascra in Boeotia, also characterized as a practical Boeotian and farmer. Tradition says that while shepherding under Helicon, the Muses gave him a laurel rod and divine song, commanding him to sing of the gods; he thereafter describes himself as their servant and credits them with teaching him to sing. He is associated above all with the Works and Days and the Theogony, while many other poems, catalogues, fragments, and genealogical or heroic accounts are attributed to him with varying degrees of uncertainty. His work ranges from practical and ordinary experience to divine genealogies, gods, heroes, peoples, marriages, and natural subjects. Its reported strengths are sincere, picturesque description, technical arrangement of lists and episodes, and a grave, child-like outlook free from classical form. His life is linked to a quarrel with his brother Perses over their inherited farm. He says he sailed from Aulis to Euboea, won a song contest at Chalcis, and dedicated the prize tripod to the Muses of Helicon; another tradition has him defeat Homer by King Paneides' decision. Later tradition says he was murdered at Oenoe, with differing accounts of his burial at Oenoe, Ascra, or Orchomenus. His poems survived through manuscript, papyrus, fragmentary, editorial, and translation traditions.

PREFACE

Hugh G. Evelyn-White largely formed his own text of Hesiod. He used independent collations of several manuscripts made by W.H.D. Rouse and otherwise relied on the apparatus criticus of several editions, especially Rzach's 1902 edition.

INTRODUCTION

Hesiod is presented as an Aeolic-speaking poet associated with Ascra in Boeotia and as the type of the practical Boeotian. He lived as a farmer, quarreled with his brother Perses over their inherited farm, was traditionally taught song by the Muses while tending sheep, and won a poetic contest at Chalcis; traditions later said that he was murdered at Oenoe and buried there.

The Hesiodic Poems

Hesiod is presented as the authorial figure associated with the Works and Days, the Theogony, and numerous other poems, although several attributions are uncertain or disputed. His literary strength lies in sincere, picturesque descriptions of ordinary experience and nature, while the Theogony displays technical skill in arranging genealogical lists and episodes. His distinctive character is described as freedom from classical form combined with a grave and child-like outlook.

The Ionic School

Hesiod’s Theogony was used as a point of comparison for the account of the Titan War in the Titanomachy. His influence was also clear in the Homeric Hymns, and the Contest of Homer and Hesiod treated their dates, poetry, contest, and legendary lives.

BIBLIOGRAPHY

Hesiod is represented by manuscript and papyrus traditions for the Works and Days, Theogony, Shield of Heracles, and lost Hesiodic works. The unit lists editions, translations, fragments, and modern scholarship concerning Hesiod and his poems.

(ll. 618-640) But if desire for uncomfortable sea-faring seize you;

The speaker claims little experience of sea-faring, having sailed only from Aulis to Euboea, where he won a song contest at Chalcis and dedicated a tripod to the Muses of Helicon. He nevertheless says that the Muses taught him to sing and that he can declare the will of Zeus.

THE ASTRONOMY

Hesiod is credited with The Astronomy and with accounts of the Pleiades, Hyades, Callisto, Arcas, and Orion. He says that Lycaon entertained Zeus and served him the babe he had cut up, and that Orion piled up the promontory by Peloris and founded the close of Poseidon.

THE PRECEPTS OF CHIRON

Many writers earlier than Aristophanes attributed The Precepts of Chiron to Hesiod. Quintilian reports that Aristophanes was the first to reject the work as Hesiod's.

THE GREAT WORKS

Hesiod is credited with the Great Works, which contains the verse about the slaying of Rhadamanthys. According to Proclus, Hesiod makes silver belong to the family of Earth in that work.

THE THEOGONY

Hesiod was shepherding his lambs under holy Helicon when the Muses taught him glorious song. They gave him a laurel rod, breathed a divine voice into him, and instructed him to sing of the gods.

THE CATALOGUES OF WOMEN AND EOIAE1701

Hesiod is cited as the author of genealogical and heroic material concerning gods, heroes, peoples, places, and marriages. The fragments attribute to him accounts of Deucalion’s descendants, the Argonauts and Harpies, Atalanta, Europa, Peleus and Thetis, and Asclepius, while also noting variant readings, interpolations, and works such as the Great Eoiae and the Journey round the Earth.

Hesiod makes Castor and Polydeuces both sons of Zeus. He also makes Helen the daughter of Ocean and Zeus, rather than of Leda or Nemesis.

Fragment #67—Scholiast on Euripides, Orestes 249: Steischorus says that

Hesiod is cited for accounts of Aphrodite’s punishment of Tyndareus’s daughters, Helen’s suitors and marriage, the plans of Zeus concerning demigods, and numerous genealogies and heroic episodes. His Catalogue is also cited for Iphigeneia’s transformation into Hecate, the parentage of several figures, and stories involving the Calydonian boar, Heracles, and Dardanus.

THE MARRIAGE OF CEYX

Hesiod is said to have written The Marriage of Ceyx and to have included accounts of Heracles, Ceyx, tripods used as tables, and wood brought for burning. One source notes that grammar-school boys disputed the poem’s attribution to him.

THE GREAT EOIAE

Hesiod is said to have composed or related material in the Great Eoiae. He is cited for portraying Heracles as both the most toilful and the most excellent, for saying that Phineus was blinded after telling Phrixus the way, and for the story of Hermes and Battus.

AEGIMIUS

Hesiod and Acusilaus are said to have identified Io as the daughter of Peiren. Hesiod also said that oaths concerning the secret deeds of the Cyprian were without penalty for men, and his tale says that Hermes slew Argus. The “Aegimius” is described as being by Hesiod or Cercops of Miletus.

FRAGMENTS OF UNKNOWN POSITION

Hesiod is called the servant of the sweet Muses. It is said that whoever the immortals honor is also followed by the good report of mortals.

OF THE ORIGIN OF HOMER AND HESIOD, AND OF THEIR CONTEST

Hesiod is said to have come from Ascra and to have been the son of Dius and Pycimede in one genealogy. He competed with Homer at Chalcis, received the prize, dedicated his tripod to the Muses, and was later murdered at Oenoe after an oracle led him unknowingly to the sacred place of Nemean Zeus.

Hesiod in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Hesiod is a poet-like figure whose works, practices, and death are reported through differing traditions. He is credited, though questioned, with the Theogony, where Styx is Oceanus’s daughter and Pallas’s wife and the Graces are Zeus and Eurynome’s daughters; On Women and other poems are also attributed to him, while people around Helicon say he wrote only The Works and Days. His poems say he sang with a laurel wand, despite a Helicon statue showing him seated with a harp, and he was excluded from a Delphi contest for not accompanying his voice with it. He won an ancient tripod at Chalcis for song, may have learned divination from Acarnanians, and after death his bones were found at Naupactus and moved to a tomb at Orchomenus.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVIII.

Hesiod is said to have represented Styx in the Theogony as the daughter of Oceanus and the wife of Pallas.

CHAPTER XXX.

Hesiod has a statue at Helicon showing him seated with a harp on his knees, although the text says this was not his usual appearance. His poems indicate that he sang with a laurel wand.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Hesiod is associated with an ancient tripod said to have been awarded to him at Chalcis for a victory in song. The people around Helicon claim that he wrote only The Works and Days, while another tradition attributes several other poems to him; accounts also differ about his divination and his death.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Hesiod, if he wrote the Theogony, said that the Graces were daughters of Zeus and Eurynome and named them Euphrosyne, Aglaia, and Thalia.

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

Hesiod has a tomb at Orchomenus, where his bones were brought from Naupactus after a pestilence. The bones were found in a hollow stone identified by a crow, and an elegiac inscription says that Ascra was his fatherland and that the Minyae received his remains.
