Abstract written by a model

Women’s suffrage is a political reform and a principal political commitment of Elsie Inglis. She followed its developments, celebrated the franchise for parish councils, debated opponents until one became a convert, and viewed the vote as a Liberal issue that could help women resist domestic oppression and reveal abuses, including husbands blocking medical treatment. She criticised parties and women’s organisations when they put party interests before enfranchisement, and ultimately left party organisation to work independently for the cause. After 1906, the movement rapidly expanded its meetings, funds, debates, societies, organisations, and activity. It included militant suffragism as a distinct section, while Inglis was a prominent organiser in the constitutional, law-abiding wing. Before the war, organisations had committees, organisers, speakers, supporters, and networks across political and social groups. The truce suspended suffrage activity during the war, but those established networks supplied leadership, workers, and fundraising for the Scottish Women’s Hospitals.
