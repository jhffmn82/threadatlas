Agrigentum in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Agrigentum was a place of shifting alliances, refuge, command, siege, and destruction. It received Syracusan exiles, joined Geloans and Messenians against Agathocles, and invited Acrotatus to lead its army. After sheltering Xenodochus, it was ruled by Phintias, commanded by Sosistratus, and delivered with forces to Pyrrhus. Hanno fortified it for the Carthaginian interest; Romans captured it, and Carthalo later retook, burned, and demolished its walls.

Agrigentum was the refuge of Xenodochus after his first defeat. Leptines later pursued the defeated Agrigentines to its walls, and the city became the place where Xenodochus's opponents demanded that he be tried.

BOOK XXII.

Agrigentum was ruled by Phintias and later commanded by Sosistratus. Pyrrhus received Agrigentum and its forces from Sosistratus, gaining eight thousand foot soldiers and eight hundred horse, along with control of thirty cities.

BOOK XXIIL

Agrigentum was fortified by Hanno, the son of Hannibal, and its people were brought over to the Carthaginian interest. The Romans later besieged and captured it after a stout resistance, while a subsequent Carthaginian force under Carthalo again took and burned the town and demolished its walls.

Agrigentum in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Agrigentum, also called Minoa after Minos's landing there, was a wealthy Greek city associated with the Agrigentines. Under Theron it conflicted with Syracuse under Thrasideus; after Thrasideus's defeat and death, its people restored democracy and sought peace from Hiero. The Agrigentines later fought the Syracusans after Deucetius was spared without their consent. Long prosperity made Agrigentum comparable in wealth to all Greek cities, with grain and valuables drawn in from its surrounding country, magnificent temples, a vast artificial pond or fishpond, tombs, population, and treasures including the Phalarian bull. Expecting to face the Carthaginian war first, it endured an eight-month Carthaginian siege despite allies. Famine forced evacuation; Amilcar entered, killed remaining people, plundered wealth and art, burned temples, and the Carthaginians occupied it through winter and sacked it again in spring.

Akragas in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Akragas is a riverside city, called the eye of Sicily, with fields guarded by Zeus, a flock-nourishing stream, a pleasant citadel, and a connection to Persephone. Its people endure long toil to gain wealth, honor, prosperity, and fame, supported by Theron’s noble ancestors. It is celebrated in song for Theron’s Olympian crown, alongside the Emmenidai and Xenokrates, a light of its people. Splendid and beautiful, it finally welcomes Midas and his victory crown.

Akragas is called the eye of Sicily and the city supported by Theron's noble ancestors. Its people are praised for enduring long toil, gaining wealth and honor, and establishing a prosperous and famous city.

Akragas is honored in the hymn celebrating Theron's Olympian victory.

VI.

Akragas is described as a riverside city for which the treasure of song is built, together with the Emmenidai and Xenokrates.

XII.

Akragas is praised as a splendid and beautiful city, the haunt of Persephone, situated by a stream that nourishes flocks and crowned by a pleasant citadel. It is presented as the place where Midas and his victory crown are welcomed.

Isthmian Ode II
