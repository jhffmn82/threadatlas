  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Salamis is a city and the origin of the Timodemidai, renowned for rearing warriors, called Aias’s city, linked with his deeds at Troy, and a witness to Aigina’s seamen helping save it in Zeus’s destructive tempest.
  • In The Library, Salamis is Asopus’s daughter and a snake-ravaged island ruled by Cychreus, who kills it; childless, Cychreus yields to Telamon.
  • In Description of Greece, Salamis lies opposite Eleusis and extends toward Megaris, containing market ruins, an ebony statue and temple of Ajax, temples of Artemis and Cychreus, and a trophy commemorating Themistocles’ naval victory for the Greeks.

Salamis in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Salamis is an island, Aias’s ancestral homeland and place of glory, praised amid the waves and admired by the world. Its blessed condition contrasts with sailors’ weary exile. In a lonely wooded place, Aias calls its sacred fields home, bids them farewell before dying, and asks the Sun to report his fall there.

Salamis in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · -0471

Salamis is an island near and fronting Athens, also a naval battlefield and strait. Its Cychrea Bay is associated with the dead. Persian ships are shattered, sunk, or routed there by Athens, Lacedaemon, Aegina, and Corinth; its beaches, reefs, waters, and nearby islet become filled with wreckage and Persian dead as soldiers and commanders are lost.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

THE PERSIANS

Salamis is the strait between Athens and the island where the Persian ships are shattered, sunk, or driven to flight by the ships of Athens, Lacedaemon, Aegina, and Corinth.

Enter a MESSENGER.

Salamis is the site of the naval battle, with beaches, reefs, and surrounding waters crowded with Persian corpses and wreckage. An islet facing Salamis becomes the place where the Persian troops sent to attack Greek fugitives are surrounded and killed.

The GHOST OF DARIUS rises.

Salamis is the place where many Persian soldiers and commanders are lost in the sea disaster. Xerxes describes men being thrown from their Tyrian ships into Salaminian waters and onto rocky shores.

Salamis in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Salamis is a city and the place of origin of the Timodemidai, renowned for rearing warriors. It is called Aias’s city, linked with his deeds at Troy, and later bears witness to Aigina’s seamen helping save it in Zeus’s destructive tempest.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Nemean Ode II

Salamis is said to be well able to rear a man of battles. Its association with Timodemos is compared with Salamis's Aias at Troy.

Isthmian Ode IV

Salamis, called Aias’s city, could bear witness to Aigina’s seamen helping deliver it during a destructive tempest of Zeus. The event is recalled as a feat of Aiginetan valor, though the ode warns against boasting.

Salamis in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Salamis lies opposite Eleusis and extends toward Megaris. It contains market ruins, an ebony statue and temple of Ajax, temples of Artemis and Cychreus, and a trophy commemorating Themistocles’ naval victory for the Greeks.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXI.

Salamis lies opposite Eleusis and extends toward Megaris; it has ruins of a market, a temple of Ajax, and a statue of Ajax made of ebony. It also contains temples of Artemis and Cychreus, and a trophy for the naval victory won by Themistocles for the Greeks.
