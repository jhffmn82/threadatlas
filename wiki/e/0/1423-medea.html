Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Medea is Jason’s wife who poisons Creon, flees to Athens, and leaves her sons at Hera Acraea’s altar, where Creon’s relatives kill them. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Medea is queen of the Colchians who prophesies Libya’s colonization, aids Jason with magic and guidance, and saves the Argo and its crew. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Medea is Jason’s abandoned foreign wife who poisons his new bride, kills their sons, and escapes with their bodies in the Sun’s chariot. In Medea, Medea is a Colchian princess, enchantress, wife, mother, avenger, child-killer, and dea ex machina who escapes in a dragon-drawn chariot. In The Argonautica, Medea is a Colchian priestess and sorceress who falls in love with Jason, aids his quest for the Golden Fleece, kills Apsyrtus, and flees with him. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Medea is a poisoner and healer who aids Jason and the Argonauts, helps bring down Pelias, burns the palace, kills most of her children, and flees. In Heroides and Amores, Medea is a Colchian sorceress who saves Jason, betrays her family, kills Absyrtus, marries Jason, and is later abandoned and exiled by him. In The Library, Medea is Aeetes’ witch daughter who aids Jason, kills Apsyrtus, Talos, Pelias, Glauce, and her children, marries Aegeus, plots against Theseus, and restores Aeetes’ kingdom.

In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Medea is a Colchian enchantress who aids and marries Jason, restores Aeson’s youth, causes Pelias’s death, kills her children, and later flees from Theseus. In The Library, Medea is a sorceress who helps Jason obtain the fleece, kills and dismembers Apsyrtus, aids revenge on Pelias, kills Glauce, Creon, Mermerus, and Pheres, and restores Aeetes’ kingdom. In Description of Greece, Medea is Corinth’s queen who makes Jason king and uses incantations to tame violent winds; accounts dispute the fate of her children. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Medea plots against Pelias, pretends to restore youth, demonstrates the trick on a ram, and persuades his daughters to boil him. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Medea is a sorceress who helps Jason obtain the fleece, restores Aeson’s youth, causes Pelias’s death, kills her children, burns the palace, and marries Aegeus.

Medea in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Medea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Medea is a woman, foreigner in Corinth, former wife of Jason, and mother of their sons. Skilled in sciences, arts, and magic, she once aided Jason in obtaining the Golden Fleece, betrayed her father’s country for him, and caused Pelias’s death through his daughters. Jason’s new marriage to Creon’s daughter leaves Medea abandoned, dishonored, and threatened with exile from Corinth alongside her children. Cut off from her father’s house and without haven, she mourns her lost country and marriage while anger and revenge overcome her reason. After Creon grants her one day, she secures Aegeus’s sworn protection and forms a plot against Jason, Creon, Creon’s daughter, and ultimately her own children, intending to wound Jason most deeply. She feigns acceptance of Jason’s marriage, asks that the sons remain in Corinth, and sends the bride poisoned gifts, a robe and golden chaplet, which kill her. Though she loves her sons, she kills them herself with a sword, fulfilling the destructive purpose associated with καταξω. Medea then escapes Jason in a chariot given by the Sun, carrying the children’s bodies beyond his reach. She says she will bury them at Juno’s shrine, establish a festival and expiatory sacrifices in the land of Sisyphus, and go to dwell with Aegeus in the land of Erectheus. She is left associated with blood, misery, exile, and a revenge achieved at terrible cost.

notes

At the end, she appears in a chariot drawn by dragons, carrying the bodies of her slaughtered sons.

Medea in Medea

Euripides · 430 BC · the work’s page · read it where Medea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Medea is a woman, princess and enchantress of Colchis, daughter of Aietes and of an old king, descended from the Sun through her father, and allied with Hecate. Skilled in unknown and evil lore, she is also a wife, mother, and foreign, landless woman. Her total devotion to Jason leads her to rescue him in the quest for the Golden Fleece, kill its guarding serpent, betray her father, leave her homeland, and help cause Pelias's death. She becomes Jason's wife and bears their two children. When Jason abandons her to marry Creon's daughter, Medea is condemned to exile from Corinth. Devastated, she curses Jason, the bride, and their house; her injuries turn her into a living Curse. She gains one remaining day from Creon, rejects Jason's assistance, and obtains Aegeus's oath of refuge in Athens. Determined on revenge against Jason, his new bride, and her father, she considers poison and fire, invokes Hecate, and chooses poisoned gifts. Though she loves her children and hesitates in grief, she sends them with the gifts, learns that the princess and king have died, and kills the children with a sword so that her enemies cannot seize them. She escapes in a winged-dragon chariot received through her divine lineage, refuses Jason access to the bodies, and travels to Pallas' plain. Within the tragic judgment, she becomes a dea ex machina while remaining subject to vengeance for the killings.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Medea is the princess and enchantress of Colchis who falls in love with Jason, helps him complete his quest, kills her own brother Absyrtus, and abandons her home to follow him. After Jason accepts a Corinthian princess and Medea is condemned to exile, her devotion turns into hatred; the introduction portrays her as a wronged woman transformed into a living Curse.

MEDEA

Medea is devastated because Jason has abandoned her to marry Creon's daughter. She curses Jason, his bride, and their house, and tells the Corinthian women that she may become dangerous if deprived of her rights in love. She persuades Creon to allow her and the children to remain for one more day.

MEDEA.

Medea believes that her defeat is not yet complete and plans revenge against Jason, the bride, and the bride's father. She considers several methods of murder, invokes Hecate for help, and urges herself to act boldly despite the danger and the possibility of having no refuge.

_Enter from the right_ JASON.

Medea denounces Jason for abandoning her after she rescued him, betrayed her father, left her homeland, and helped destroy Pelias for his sake. She refuses Jason's offers of assistance, secures Aegeus's oath of protection in Athens, and leaves the encounter resolved to carry out her plan before departing.

CHORUS.

Medea rejoices that Aegeus can give her refuge in Athens and reveals her plan to send poisoned gifts to Jason’s new bride before killing her own children. She pretends to reconcile with Jason, sends the children with the gifts, and later struggles with grief and hesitation before deciding that her children must die.

[_Enter_ MESSENGER.

Medea learns from the Messenger that the princess and the king have died and expresses satisfaction. She kills her children to prevent them from falling into the hands of her enemies, then escapes in the winged-dragon chariot and refuses Jason's pleas to touch or bury their bodies.

Medea in The Argonautica

Apollonius Rhodius · 299 to 200 BC · the work’s page · read it where Medea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Medea is Aeetes’ daughter, Chalciope’s sister, a Colchian maiden, priestess and devotee of Hecate, and a sorceress full of wiles. Hera and Athena arrange for Eros to make her love Jason so that she will aid his quest. On seeing Jason, she is seized by secret, conflicted, then passionate love, fears his death, dreams of choosing him, and struggles between shame, desire, loyalty to her family, and fear of discovery. After a sleepless night in which she considers suicide, she prepares the charm of Prometheus, meets Jason secretly at Hecate’s shrine, and gives him both the charm and instructions for overcoming the bulls and earthborn men. Her magic protects him, and she later promises to lull the guardian serpent and helps him obtain the golden fleece. When she fears Aeetes has discovered her help, Medea flees Colchis in anguish to join Jason, losing her country, parents, and home. Bound to him by oaths, she fears he may abandon her. She proposes a plan to lure her brother Apsyrtus, signals after Jason kills him, and later sits with Jason as a suppliant at Circe’s hearth while concealing the killing. She begs not to be returned to Aeetes, recalls her aid to the Argonauts, and marries Jason in Macris’s sacred cave, protected from the Colchians by Alcinous’s judgment. She travels onward with twelve handmaids, uses sorcery to subdue Talos, and is destined to marry Aeson’s son on the Elysian plain. Her course moves from love-stricken hesitation, through decisive magical aid and flight, to marriage, continued danger, and powerful intervention for Jason and the Argonauts.

Abstract written by a model

Medea is a royal maiden of Colchis, daughter of Æetes and, according to some writers, Hecate’s daughter; she is renowned as an enchantress and sorceress skilled in herbs, potions, rites, and potent magic. Aided by Juno through Venus, she falls violently in love with Jason while divided between that love and loyalty to her father and homeland. She agrees to assist his expedition if he marries her, gives him enchanted herbs and protective charms against the bulls, warriors, and dragon, helps him reach the palace and take its treasures, then betrays her family and country by fleeing with him to Greece as his wife. She restores Jason’s father Æson to youth through elaborate rites, herbs, sacrifices, and a potion. She next takes revenge on Pelias by deceiving his daughters into attacking him and completes his death through the cauldron. Afterward she goes to Corinth. When Jason marries Glauce, or Creüsa, daughter of King Creon, Medea burns the palace, kills her two sons by Jason, and evades punishment by flying away in a heavenly, winged chariot drawn by dragons. Later she marries Ægeus in Athens and seeks succession for her son Medus. She prepares a poisoned cup from Scythian wolfsbane for Theseus, but when Ægeus prevents him from drinking it, she escapes amid clouds raised by enchantments.

Medea in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where Medea first appears

Abstract written by a model

Medea is the daughter of Aeetes and a potent sorceress who practices witchcraft with enchanted plants, a magic caldron, and a serpent-drawn chariot. Promised marriage by Jason, she aids him against fire-breathing bulls, armed men, and the dragon guarding the fleece, then escapes with him. As Jason’s wife, she restores Aeson’s youth and causes Pelias’s daughters to kill him. When Jason leaves her for Creusa, she sends Creusa a poisoned robe, kills her children, burns the palace, flees to Athens, and marries Aegeus.

Chapter XI

Medea, the daughter of Aeetes and a potent sorceress, agreed to help Jason after he promised to marry her and swore an oath before the altar of Hecate. She gave him a charm that protected him from the bulls' fire and the armed men, and she taught him how to use it. She later put the dragon guarding the fleece to sleep with magic songs, spells, and a sleeping potion, then escaped with Jason and the Argonauts.

MEDEA AND AESON

Medea uses her arts to restore Aeson's youth, then deceives the daughters of Pelias into helping kill their father. After Jason puts her away to marry Creusa, she sends a poisoned robe to the bride, kills her own children, burns the palace, and flees to Athens.
