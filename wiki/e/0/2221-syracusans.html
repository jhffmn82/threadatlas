Syracusans in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Syracusans are the people and citizens of Syracuse, originally descended from the Corinthians. Their history is marked by intestine seditions, tyranny, changing commanders, warfare, and attempts to secure liberty and democratic administration. Many, disarmed under Dionysius, joined Dion, chose Dion and Megacles as absolute generals, celebrated release from slavery, defended the city, and defeated Dionysian forces. They aided the Leontines and won at sea, but rejected Dion’s proposed terms, divided politically, suffered defeat by his mercenaries, and were overrun until Dion rescued the city. Seeking relief from sedition and rule by many lords, they asked Corinth for a general and made Timoleon sovereign commander; after struggles involving Dionysius, Hicetas, the Carthaginians, and Timoleon, they adopted democratic laws under Timoleon’s control. They later admitted Agathocles and gave him full authority, fought and captured Amilcar during a Carthaginian assault, endured scarcity during a blocked harbor, and saw Agathocles murder five hundred Syracusans suspected of opposition. They were conquered by Agathocles. They also revolted against Dionysius, were dispersed, and later resisted his siege under Theodorus and others.

Syracusans in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Syracusans are the people, inhabitants, and citizens of Syracuse, portrayed chiefly through wars, civic decisions, alliances, and struggles over freedom and rule. Early on, they fought internal enemies and resisted would-be tyrants: they executed innovators, adopted Petalism after repeated attempts at tyranny, and later abolished it because it caused disorder and excluded valuable citizens. They killed their general Bilco, defeated Deucetius, accepted his submission, and sent him to Corinth with support. They defeated the Agrigentines at the river Himera, extended control over Sicilian cities, destroyed Trinacria, and dedicated spoils at Delphi. They also warred with Leontium, incorporated Leontines as citizens, used Leontium as a garrison, and later expelled its people. Their defining military success came against the Athenians. Led by Hermocrates, Sicanus, and Heraclides and aided by allies, they resisted siege, won battles on land and sea, blocked the harbor, destroyed or disabled Athenian ships, and captured thousands. After debate over mercy, they executed the Athenian generals and confederates and confined Athenian citizens in quarries. They then rewarded allies, aided them with ships, and organized government under the Dioclean laws. Later, facing Carthaginian pressure, they made peace with the Chalcidonians, tried to help Selinus, Himera, and Agrigentum, defeated part of the Carthaginian fleet at Eryx, and sought outside assistance. Political division continued: they banished Hermocrates but regretted it after his successes. Amid disasters around Agrigentum, they chose Dionysius, eventually granting him absolute power despite recognizing the danger of tyranny. They aided his war against Carthage and defended Syracuse, but lost their liberty under his rule. Armed citizens repeatedly sought to recover it, revolted or conspired, and were defeated or dispersed. They nevertheless continued to fight Carthage, helped secure naval victories, and benefited when the immediate threat to Syracuse was removed, even as Dionysius retained power over them.

CHAP. XIX.

The Syracusans fought the mercenaries who had seized parts of Syracuse and eventually defeated them in battle. They also joined Deucetius against Catana and helped restore displaced populations in Sicily.

CHAP. XXIV.

The Syracusans executed Tyndarides and other innovators after rescuers freed him from custody. They adopted Petalism to prevent powerful citizens from pursuing sovereignty, later sent fleets against the Tyrrhenians, and eventually abolished Petalism because it encouraged disorder and drove valuable citizens from public life.

CHAP. XXVIII.

The Syracusans killed Bilco, their general, believing him responsible for their defeat and suspecting him of secret communication with Deucetius. They later defeated Deucetius, accepted his submission as a suppliant, and sent him to Corinth with a promise to supply his necessities.
