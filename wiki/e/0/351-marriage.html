II

marriage in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Marriage is described through distinct terms for husband and wife; it frames Medea’s abandonment by Jason, his alliance with princes, the children’s wedding gifts, and mothers carrying torches at weddings.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

notes

Marriage is central to the notes on Medea's situation: Jason has abandoned Medea and contracted an alliance with princes, while Medea's children are associated with the bride's wedding gifts. The notes also mention the custom of mothers carrying lighted torches at their children's weddings and discuss the grammatical use of terms for marrying.

marriage in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Marriage, tied to Phædra's union with Theseus after her journey from Crete to Athens, brings bridal and household obligations but turns bitter through love for another man. Phædra condemns marital pollution and fears discovery and dishonor, while the Nurse urges concealment.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

Phædra condemns the pollution of the marriage-bed with other men and fears being discovered to have injured Theseus. The Nurse treats concealment of marital wrongdoing as a common practice among mortals.

CHORUS, PHÆDRA.

Marriage is associated with Venus, bridal chambers, dowries, household burdens, and Phædra's fear of dishonoring her family. The Chorus recalls Phædra's journey from Crete to Athens for her marriage to Theseus.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

BOOK VI

Minerva tells Nausicaa that she is likely to marry soon and should prepare clean clothes for herself and those who attend her. Nausicaa avoids mentioning her own wedding to Alcinous, though Ulysses speaks of the happiness of a husband and wife who are of one mind.
