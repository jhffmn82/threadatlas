Abstract written by a model

In Heroides and Amores, fame and public reputation are portrayed as burdens threatened by suspicion, reproach, and acknowledged infidelity, while concealment and apparent modesty preserve reputation. In Heroides and Amores, poetry and poetic fame is presented as a powerful art that wins lasting renown, overcomes barriers, earns a beloved’s favor, outlasts worldly achievements, and preserves poets’ works and memory. In The Amores, or Amours, fame is presented as an everlasting distinction conferred by poetry, offering timeless worldwide celebration and enduring reputation, including Corinna’s public poetic fame.

fame and public reputation in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where fame and public reputation first appears

Abstract written by a model

Fame and public reputation are a burden: Paris’s praise heightens Menelaus’s suspicion, while Helen fears widespread reproach if she follows Paris. Reputation is harmed when infidelity is acknowledged; Ovid instead urges Corinna to seem modest, conceal sexual conduct, and preserve the belief in her faithfulness.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Prima mea est igitur Veneri plaeuisse voluptas ;

Helen says that fame is a burden because Paris's persistent praise makes Menelaus more reasonably suspicious. She fears that Sparta, Achaia, other peoples, and Troy would fill the earth with reproaches against her if she followed Paris.

XII

Public reputation is presented as depending on whether a woman's faults are exposed or denied. Ovid urges Corinna to appear modest in public, conceal her sexual conduct, and allow him to enjoy the mistaken belief that she is faithful.

poetry and poetic fame in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where poetry and poetic fame first appears

Abstract written by a model

Poetry and poetic fame are a powerful art that seeks lasting renown worldwide. Song can overcome natural and physical barriers, including barred doors, and earns the poet favor from his beloved mistress. It outlasts material achievements, rulers, wealth, monuments, and its creators, preserving named poets’ works and promising the speaker’s own survival through verse.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

XII

The speaker claims that poetry survives longer than rocks, ploughs, kings, triumphs, gold, and monuments. He says that the works of Homer, Hesiod, Callimachus, Sophocles, Aratus, Menander, Ennius, Accius, Lucretius, Virgil, Tibullus, and Gallus will endure, and that his own poetry will survive him.

LIBER SECUNDUS

Song and poetry are portrayed as powerful enough to bring down the moon, recall the sun’s horses, silence serpents, reverse flowing water, and overcome barred doors. Ovid says that his elegies soften the mistress’s door and win him a greater reward than singing of heroic figures would.

fame in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where fame first appears

Abstract written by a model

Fame is an everlasting distinction conferred by poetry. Unlike perishable wealth, it offers lasting value and supports the Poet's reputation. The speaker seeks worldwide, timeless celebration; poets' fame endures with the world, while Corinna's poetic fame makes her public and draws rivals.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

ELEGY X.

The fame conferred by poetry is said to be everlasting, unlike vestments, gems, and gold, which will rend or spoil. The speaker offers lasting fame rather than payment for sexual favors.

ELEGY XV.

Fame is sought so that the speaker may be celebrated throughout the whole world and remembered for all time. The fame of numerous poets is said to endure as long as natural and human things continue to exist.

ELEGY XII.

Corinna has gained fame through the speaker's poems. Her fame has made her public and has attracted many rivals.
