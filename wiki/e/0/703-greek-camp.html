Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, the tents stand near the sea, contain Patroclus's funeral pyre, and are defended by the retreating Achaeans. In The Rhesus of Euripides, the Greek camp is watched by Trojan forces as Odysseus and Diomede approach it and Hector plans to attack it. In Hecuba and other plays, the Greek encampment on the shore holds Trojan women captive, and its tents and troops create uncertainty about Greek intentions.

Greek camp in The Rhesus of Euripides

Abstract written by a model

At night, the Greek camp's watch-fires reveal armies opposed to Troy amid apparent disorder; Hector suspects departure, while Odysseus and Diomede escape toward it and Hector plans to break its phalanx and burn galleys.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

RHESUS

The Greek camp is visible through rows of watch-fires in the distance. Its beacons burn through the night while apparent disorder spreads among the troops, making Hector suspect that the Greeks are preparing to leave.

_Enter_ PARIS.

The Greek camp is the destination toward which Odysseus and Diomede escape after the raid. Hector intends to break its phalanx and burn the galleys at the coming battle.

Greek encampment in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Greek encampment first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greek encampment, on the shore beside the Greek fleet, holds Trojan women captive in tents. Hecuba lures Polymestor into a tent by claiming to reveal treasure. At night, many fires and tumultuous troops moving toward Agamemnon’s tent create uncertainty about Greek intentions.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHORUS.

The Greek encampment contains the tents where the Trojan captives are held. Hecuba brings Polymestor into one of these tents under the pretense of showing him where treasure is concealed.

RHESUS.

The Greek camp is lit by numerous fires during the night, and its troops move tumultuously toward Agamemnon’s tent. Its unusual activity leads Hector to suspect a flight, but Aeneas considers that the fires may be intended to lure the Trojans into danger.
