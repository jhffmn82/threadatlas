Yucatan in Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa

Augustus Le Plongeon · 1881

Yucatan is a peninsula between the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea, extending from 17 deg. 30' to 21 deg. 50' north and 88 deg. to 91 deg. west of Greenwich. It is low and mostly flat, formed throughout of fossiferous limestone, with interior elevations above 70 feet and a narrow hill range whose highest point is not much above 500 feet. The hills yield red oxide of iron, marble, and lithographic stone. The country is described as free from earthquakes, honeycombed with caves, and marked by numerous senotes and underground currents. It has no surface rivers and, in the territories held by white inhabitants, no lakes; rare aguadas and mangrove wetlands provide exceptions to its generally water-absorbing, stony terrain. Its southern and eastern districts have red-clay patches supporting sugar cane and Yuca root, while large forests contain mahogany, cedar, and zapotillo; coastal patches include logwood and mora. Coffee, vanilla, tobacco, india-rubber, and especially good copal are abundant but uncultivated amid unsettled conditions. Hennequen is the principal crop of the white inhabitants. The population is given as about 250,000, mostly Indians and people of mixed blood, with few families of pure Caucasian race and African blood in some districts. Maya language is spoken by the majority. Yucatan also contains monuments, ruined cities, teocalis, stone buildings, pyramid mounds, temples, inscriptions, carved figures, mural paintings, and burial places including jar-like sepulchers, urns, and coffins. The later records present it as the homeland and surviving center of Maya language, traditions, and remains despite invasions and Spanish efforts to erase them. They describe ceremonies, dress, gestures, tools, beliefs, and burial practices, and repeatedly compare its architecture, customs, and monuments with those of the Mississippi Valley, Asia, Chaldea, Egypt, Nubia, and Equatorial Africa. Mayab and ancient cities are placed in or near Yucatan, whose western region is called Nohnial.

Mayab in Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa

Augustus Le Plongeon · 1881

Mayab is the Maya country, a name for Yucatan and a land of the Mayas. Its name is said to mean “tammy or sieve,” explained by stony, porous ground that absorbs surface water and yields it later, filtered and cool, in cenotes and caves. Its people are said to have taken their name from the country; MAYO_lo is described as a corruption of Mayab, and later peoples are said to have transformed Mayab from a locality into a personality associated with the mother of the gods. Mayab is portrayed through its monuments, paintings, customs, religious practices, language, writing, calendar, and astronomical knowledge. It had elaborate dress among higher classes and representations of rulers, priests, warriors, and others. It also had initiation mysteries with successive gradations, apparently culminating in a third grade, connected with rooms and symbols at Uxmal. The records repeatedly compare its color symbolism, sacred or funerary blue, religion, and ancient writing with Egypt. Finally, it is presented as a homeland from which Maya colonies and migrants departed, including an alleged colony that settled along the Nile.
