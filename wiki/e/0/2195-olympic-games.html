Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, the Olympic games are described as great games sacred to Zeus, founded in tradition by Pelops and Herakles, where competitors win wreaths, fame, and praise. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Olympic Games are described as the setting for a dispute between the Pisates and Elians, with the Pisates and Arcadians fighting during the celebration before Greek spectators. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Olympic games are described as instituted by Hercules near the Alpheus, with horse-coursing, wrestling, and other contests, and later associated with chariot displays that spectators ridiculed. In Description of Greece, the Olympian games are described as a repeatedly renewed festival of athletic and horse-racing contests at Olympia, governed by rules and contested by Elis and rival authorities. In Description of Greece, the Olympian contests are described as instituted by Idæan Hercules, with rules concerning women and athlete nudity, and with later commemorated victors including Hiero, Aratus, and Agesarchus. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Olympian games are described as established by Hercules the Theban, restored by Iphitus after a lapse, and associated with Iasius's horserace victory and wild-olive crown.

Olympic games in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · 497 BC · the work’s page · read it where Olympic games first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Olympic games are the greatest among games, sacred to Zeus and praised as a source of glorious hymn, lasting fame, and sweet tranquillity for winners. Tradition connects their founding both to Pelops and to Herakles, who established them beside the ancient tomb of Pelops after sanctifying altars to his sire. The ordinance of Herakles is held at Pisa, in its famous valley beside the Alpheos, as a fifth-year feast in the middle of Hekatombaion, at the full moon. Elean truce-bringers proclaim the games with a universal truce, and a judge presides. Contests begin with foot-racing and wrestling and include boxing, javelin, stone-throwing, chariot-racing, and the four-horse chariot race. Competitors such as Diagoras, Asopichos, and Theaios gain praise or victory there; winners receive wreaths and garlands, and their success is carried into customary hymns and ode.

The Olympian games are presented as greater than all other contests and as the source of glorious song. They honor Pelops and offer lasting fame and a sweet tranquillity to those who win.

notes

The Olympic games were held in the middle of Hekatombaion, when the moon was full. Their judge was thought to be a descendant of Oxylos or of an Aitolian who came with him; the full moon was regarded as a season of good luck for their institution by Herakles.

The Olympic Games are among the contests whose winners receive the ode's praise. Diagoras won twice at Olympia, and the poem asks Zeus to glorify the customary hymn for an Olympian winner.

The Olympic games were founded by Herakles beside the ancient tomb of Pelops. Their first contests included foot-racing, wrestling, boxing, chariot racing, javelin, and stone-throwing.

XIV.

The Olympic games are where Asopichos won the boys' short foot-race. In their famous contests at Pisa's valley, he crowned his boyish hair with garlands.

Olympian games in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Olympian games first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Olympian games are a festival of athletic and horse-racing contests at Olympia, associated with ancient traditions of their institution or renewal by figures including Idæan Hercules and Iphitus. According to the archives of Elis, Idæan Hercules set them every fifth year after racing his four brothers; other traditions connect them with Zeus, Cronos, and other early founders. The games had been discontinued after the reign of Oxylus, then Iphitus restored them with the people of Elis after instructions from the priestess at Delphi. After revival, their programme expanded from running to the double course, pentathlum, wrestling, boxing, horse-races, pancratium, boys' contests, the race in heavy armour, and races for colts. Some events were later ended, including the pentathlum for boys and, in the 84th Olympiad, the chariot and trotting races. The contests reward merit, speed, and bodily strength rather than money. Bribery, accepting bribes, lateness, and cowardice brought punishments and fines, often used for statues of Zeus. Victors receive wild-olive crowns, prizes, memorial statues or chariots, and a banquet in the Town Hall of the people of Elis. The priestess of Demeter Chamyne watches from a white-stone altar opposite the umpires, while arrangements for horse-racing starts aim at fairness. The games also preserve many individual victories and disputes over victory lists. Control of the games is contested. Pisa sought to conduct them instead of Elis in the eighth and thirty-fourth Olympiads, and Arcadians held games in the 104th; Elis excluded these games from its registers. Thus the games unfold as a repeatedly renewed festival: restored after suspension, enlarged through new contests, governed through rules and penalties, celebrated through victors and crowns, and defended by Elis against rival oversight.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

11 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK V.--ELIS.

The Olympian games had been discontinued before Iphitus’s time. Iphitus restored them with the people of Elis after receiving instructions from the priestess at Delphi.

CHAPTER V.

According to the ancient archives of Elis, Cronos was the first king of Heaven and had a temple built to him at Olympia. Idæan Hercules was said to have instituted the Olympian Games after racing his four brothers and to have set their interval at every fifth year because the brothers were five. Other traditions connected the Games with contests between Zeus and Cronos or with Zeus’s victory over Cronos.

CHAPTER VIII.

The Olympic games were said to have been established or renewed by several early figures, including Clymenus, Endymion, Pelops, Amythaon, Pelias, Neleus, Augeas, Hercules, and Iphitus. After their revival, contests were added over time, including the double course, pentathlum, wrestling, boxing, horse-races, the pancratium, boys’ events, the race in heavy armour, and races for colts.

CHAPTER XI.

The Olympic festival is associated with contests for boys and other ancient competitions represented on the throne of Zeus. The account also mentions the victory of Pantarces in boys' wrestling at the 86th Olympiad and the memorial horses of Cynisca for her victory at Olympia.

CHAPTER XV.

The Olympian games are associated with victors who are entertained in the Town Hall of the people of Elis. The victors receive crowns made from the wild olive tree, and the Hera Festival uses a race-course at Olympia.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Olympic games are presented as contests that should be won through merit, speed, and bodily strength rather than money. Athletes were punished for bribery, accepting bribes, arriving late, and cowardice, with fines often used to erect statues of Zeus.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

The Olympic contests included running, wrestling, boxing, the pancratium, the pentathlum, horse-races, and chariot-races. The unit records disputes, exclusions, changing rules, and the victories of contestants from many Greek communities.

CHAPTER VI.

The Olympic Games awarded crowns and prizes in boxing, wrestling, pancratium, running, the pentathlum, and chariot racing. The unit records the victories of Euthymus, Diagoras and his descendants, Glaucus of Carystus, and many other athletes, as well as the erection of statues and chariots commemorating them.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Olympic games are presented through records of victories in running, wrestling, boxing, the pancratium, the pentathlum, horse-racing, and chariot-racing. The unit also records disputes about Olympic victory lists, including the victories attributed to the sons of Phidolas.

CHAPTER XVII.

The Olympian games were watched by the priestess of Demeter Chamyne from an altar of white stone opposite the umpires. The games included athletic contests and horse races, and the horse-racing starts were organized so that all competitors could begin fairly.

CHAPTER XXI.

Pisa challenged Elis's control of the Olympian games by inviting Phido of Argos to oversee them in the eighth Olympiad and by holding games under Pantaleon in the thirty-fourth Olympiad. Games conducted by Pisa, and later by the Arcadians in the 104th Olympiad, were not included by Elis in its registers.

Olympian contests in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Olympian contests first appears

Abstract written by a model

Olympian contests are instituted by Idæan Hercules, who gives them their name. Women of Elis who compete or cross the Alpheus on forbidden days face being hurled from Mount Typæum; Callipatira is spared after disguising herself for her son, and athletes are required to compete naked. Hiero, Aratus, and Agesarchus are later commemorated as victors.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

Women of Elis who competed in the Olympian contests or crossed the Alpheus on forbidden days were traditionally to be hurled from Mount Typæum. Callipatira, also called Pherenice by some, was the only woman said to have been detected; she had disguised herself as an athlete to bring her son to Olympia. She was spared because of her family’s victories, but a law was then made requiring athletes to compete naked.

CHAPTER XII.

Hiero the son of Dinomenes was commemorated for victories in the Olympian contests. Aratus was proclaimed victor at Olympia in the chariot-race, and the inscription for Agesarchus states that he defeated men in boxing at the Olympian games.

Olympian games in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Abstract written by a model

Established by Hercules the Theban, the Olympian games lapsed, then Iphitus restored them with racing prizes; Iasius won the horserace, receiving a wild-olive crown.

CHAPTER XXVI.

The Olympian games had been discontinued for a long time before Iphitus restored them. When they were restored, prizes were offered only for racing.

CHAPTER XLVIII.

The Olympian games were established by Hercules the Theban. Their horserace was won by Iasius, and the prize at Olympia was a crown of wild olive.
