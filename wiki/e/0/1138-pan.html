Pan

deity · 7 works · 62 quoted facts · 20 connections
Also called: Chemmin, God, God Pan, God of the flocks, Pan Nomius, Pan, the shepherd-god, Pans, Pan’s herd of goats, a representative of all the gods, boy, child, god, god of all nature, god of the fields
and 6 morelord, rustic melody, son, the god, the god of woods and fields, the personification of Nature
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Pan accompanies Osiris, is highly revered by the Egyptians, and has a city named after him in Thebides called Chemmin.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Pan is a shepherd-god and son of Hermes who hunts, plays reed pipes, sings, and dances with nymphs in wooded and mountainous places.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Pan is a god who pursues Syrinx, creates the syrinx from reeds, and is variously associated with Egyptian nature worship, Echo, and the explanation of an echo.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Pan is a half-goat god of flocks who plays joined reeds, boasts to the Nymphs, challenges Apollo, and loses to him.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Pan is an Arcadian deity associated with mountain hunting, pipes, temples, prayer, punishment, oracles, Demeter's discovery, and his appearance to Philippides.
  • In Description of Greece, Pan is a deity who meets Philippides, promises aid to Athene at Marathon, is honoured by the Athenians, and is worshipped near Marathon and at Erasinus.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Pan is the god of woods, fields, flocks, shepherds, nature, and music who pursues Syrinx, invents the syrinx, loses a contest to Apollo, and later symbolizes the universe, Nature, all gods, and heathenism.

Hesiod · -0699~

Pan is a shepherd-god and dear son of Hermes, marked by goat’s feet, two horns, beard, and lynx-pelt. He roams wooded glades and mountain peaks, keenly hunts wild beasts, then at evening plays reed pipes, sings, and dances with nymphs. His merry noise and song delight the immortals’ hearts.

Pan in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Pan is a god or divinity, possibly associated with Egyptian worship of nature, yet possibly one of twelve Pans native to Greece and worshipped at Mendes. He loves and pursues Syrinx as she returns from the hill of Lycæus; when he seizes reeds rather than her, their sound charms him and he joins them with wax to form his first instrument, the Syrinx or Pandæan pipe, perhaps from reeds by the River Ladon. In poets’ embellished accounts he also falls in love with Echo, while another explanation presents Pan as a person seeking the cause of that phenomenon.

Pan in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Pan is a deity native to Arcadia, associated with hunting its mountains and playing pipes. He has temples at Heræa and near the sanctuary of Despœna, as well as an altar near Tegea; his temple has a portico, a small statue, and a never-ceasing fire. Considered among the most powerful gods, he answers prayers, punishes the wicked, and formerly gave oracular responses through the Nymph Erato. While hunting, he alone found Demeter on Mount Elaion and told Zeus. He later appeared to and interviewed Philippides.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXVII.

Pan has a temple reached by a staircase, with a portico and a statue that is not very large. He is said to answer prayers and punish the wicked, and in ancient times he reportedly gave oracular responses through the Nymph Erato as his interpreter. A never-ceasing fire burns in his temple.

CHAPTER XLII.

Pan traversed Arcadia while hunting in the mountains and saw Demeter dressed in black on Mount Elaion. He informed Zeus of her whereabouts.

Pan in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Pan is a god of woods, fields, flocks, and shepherds, living in Arcadia and grottos and associated with nature, music, and the syrinx. He wears a pine-leaf garland, wanders mountains and valleys, hunts, leads nymphs in dances, and inspires sudden "panic terror." After pursuing Syrinx, he receives only reeds when she is transformed by water nymphs, then invents and names the syrinx for her. Worshipped by Midas, he loses a music contest against Apollo. Later, he symbolizes the universe, Nature, all gods, and heathenism.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter III

Pan pursues Syrinx after meeting her, but embraces only reeds when the water nymphs transform her. He makes an instrument from reeds of unequal lengths and calls it Syrinx in her honor.

Chapter IV

Pan was the god of the fields and became worshipped by Midas. He compared his music with Apollo's and challenged Apollo to a contest, which Apollo won.

Chapter XV

Pan is the god of woods and fields, flocks and shepherds. He dwells in grottos, wanders through mountains and valleys, hunts, leads nymphs in dances, and plays the syrinx, which he invented. Sudden fright without a visible cause was attributed to him and called panic terror; he later became a symbol of the universe, Nature, all the gods, and heathenism.
