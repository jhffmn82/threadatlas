Syracuse in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Syracuse is a major Greek city in Sicily, repeatedly the seat and base of rulers, armies, factions, and commonwealth. Its history begins under Dionysius the tyrant, whose naval news reached the city, and then under Dionysius the younger, whose fear of Dion led Dion to seek the city’s liberty. Dion entered after citizens and nearby inhabitants joined him: Syracuse exchanged slavery for liberty, though Dionysius kept the Island’s castle. Conflict continued through faction, poverty, unpaid soldiers, disorder, sack, and fire, until Dion defeated Nypsius’s forces, restored defenses, and repaired the city. Internal seditions and many tyrants led the Syracusans to seek Corinthian help. The city was divided among Dionysius, Hicetas, Timoleon, and the Carthaginians, before Timoleon gained it all. He destroyed forts and palaces linked to tyranny, instituted democratic laws, revised laws, enrolled freed inhabitants and Corinthian colonists as citizens, and offered houses and land to settlers throughout Greece. He governed for eight years and was honored after death. Agathocles then rose from Syracuse, gained complete authority through armed massacre, and ruled it as tyrant. The city served as his base during campaigns, received exiles and spoils, and endured Carthaginian attacks on its harbor. After defeats, Agathocles repaired its walls, gathered grain and fruit, and left garrisons; when he crossed to Africa, Syracuse faced expulsion of inhabitants, siege by Amilcar, cut provisions, destroyed crops, naval losses, and scarcity. Its defenders resisted, captured Amilcar, and, after Agathocles’s sea victory, received provisions and changed from scarcity to abundance. Agathocles also ordered mass killings there, but after his death Syracuse regained democratic government, destroyed his statues, and confiscated his estate. Later Syracuse appealed to Pyrrhus during a Carthaginian siege; he entered, reconciled factions, and used its ships, engines, weapons, and provisions. It remained with Hiero after peace with Roman consuls and supplied corn to the Romans. After Hieronymus’s death, it ordered the killing of his relatives. When Syracuse was taken, its citizens sought mercy from Marcellus, who promised to spare freemen’s lives while permitting soldiers to take property.

Syracuse in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Syracuse is a Greek city, called the greatest among the Grecians, containing Ortygia, Epipolae, a great haven, and forts. It is associated with Arethusa and Cyane, and its inhabitants observed annual ceremonies for Ceres and Proserpine. Its political life moved between civic rule and domination: Gelon returned from Himera as benefactor, king, and deliverer; Hiero ruled; citizens expelled Thrasybulus and restored democracy, followed by three hundred years of peace and prosperity until Dionysius. The city also endured disputes over mercenaries, land, faction, Petalism, and tyranny, while its assembly at times protected suppliants such as Deucetius and decided questions of war, prisoners, and government. Syracuse directed campaigns, received refugees and displaced inhabitants, and became a center of resistance after Agrigentum fell. Its most prominent danger was siege: Athenians attacked by land and sea after taking Epipolae, but Syracuse, aided by Gylippus, Corinth, and Lacedaemon, destroyed their works, won on land and sea, celebrated safety, and debated mercy toward prisoners. Under Dionysius, fear and division enabled absolute authority; he strengthened defenses, separated the fortified island, controlled weapons, recruited foreign troops, and made the city a center for arms, ships, and engines. Carthaginian attacks damaged its harbor and later besieged it with forts, yet its citizens fought at sea, captured supplies, and helped destroy the opposing fleet. Syracuse remained outside Carthaginian control and became a base for Dionysius's expeditions into Italy.

Syracuse was asked to aid Selinus and began gathering forces after making peace with the Chalcidonians. Hannibal later planned to sail against Syracuse with selected troops, believing it lacked sufficient strength to defend itself, but the intended attack did not occur in this unit.

CHAP. X.

Syracuse received the bodies of the Syracusans killed at Himera, and its people unanimously supported their burial. The Syracusans then killed Hermocrates and many of his accomplices during his attempted return.

CHAP. 3(111.

Syracuse received refugees from Sicily after the fall of Agrigentum and became the center of resistance to the Carthaginians. Dionysius used the city's fear and political divisions to obtain absolute authority over it.

CHAP. XVI.

Syracuse received the inhabitants removed from Camarina and became the scene of a revolt against Dionysius. Syracusan horsemen entered the arsenal and plundered Dionysius's palace, but Dionysius returned, forced entry through the gates, and reestablished his control.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK XIV.

Dionysius divided the fortified island of Syracuse from the city, built towers and guardhouses, and enclosed the arsenal at the small harbor of Laccius. The city later became the center of a revolt against his rule.

CHAP. III.

Syracuse was placed under stronger tyrannical control when Dionysius seized weapons from the citizens and recruited foreign troops. Its inhabitants of Naxos, Catana, and Leontium were defeated, enslaved, expelled, or transferred under Dionysius's expansion.

CHAP. VII.

Syracuse became the center of Dionysius's extensive military preparations, with workmen producing arms, ships, and artillery engines throughout the city. Its harbor was attacked at night by a Carthaginian force, which destroyed or sank almost all the ships there.

Pindar · -0497

Syracuse is a great, famous city, associated with Ares, armed men, horses, and the island Ortygia, from which it springs and on which part of it was built. It is the home of Agesias and of Hieron, its ruler and king. From Thebes, the speaker sends or imagines bringing victory song, health, honor, and continuing good fortune to Syracuse, Hieron, and Ortygia.

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Syracuse, the only Sicilian Greek city remaining, was besieged by Carthaginians; it invited Pyrrhus of Epirus, who raised the siege; it was ruled by Hiero son of Dinomenes then Hiero son of Hierocles, after whose assassination Dinomenes tried to kill Hippocrates.

Syracuse invited Pyrrhus of Epirus into Sicily when the Carthaginians were besieging it. Pyrrhus crossed from Tarentum and compelled the Carthaginians to raise the siege.

CHAPTER XII.

Syracuse was ruled by Hiero the son of Dinomenes and later by Hiero the son of Hierocles. After Hiero the son of Hierocles was assassinated, Dinomenes tried to kill Hippocrates, who had come to Syracuse from Erbessus and was attempting to persuade the people.
