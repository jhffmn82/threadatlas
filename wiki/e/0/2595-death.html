Death in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

Death is an inexorable power that profanes what is hallowed and lays dusky hands on all, overwhelming poets and dragging even pious worshippers from temples to tombs; yet poetry remains alive after the speaker’s fire, while Envy rests and merit preserves reward.

ELEGY XV.

Death does not destroy poetry, which remains alive after the speaker’s closing fire has consumed him. After death, Envy is at rest and each person’s merit protects his reward.

ELEGY IX.

Death profanes everything hallowed and lays its dusky hands on all. It overwhelms even Orpheus, Homer, and Tibullus, and drags the pious worshipper from the temples to the tomb.

death in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Death is portrayed as the feared possible end of anxious love, imagined in shipwreck and mourning. It is also prayed for as relief from the lover’s pain. Though it profanes sacred things, seizes worshippers and poets, and reduces Tibullus to an urn, it cannot wholly destroy poetry or the fame kept alive by song: after the final fire, a large part of the speaker endures.

death in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Death is an unavoidable and uncertain fate for mortals, described as a debt none can escape and as a terrible ill. Across these scenes, it appears as sacrifice, condemnation, separation, and honorable self-offering. Polyxena prefers death to dishonorable slavery, while Hecuba mourns and cannot die in her place. Orestes faces death after condemnation and later faces Taurian sacrifice with Pylades; he holds that mourning cannot stop it. Alcestis dies as a substitute, temporarily altering the appointed course of death, and Admetus imagines reunion after his own death. Iphigenia willingly embraces death as honor, memorial, glory, and a substitute for marriage and children, though Clytemnestra grieves. Death remains inevitable, yet its meaning is contested by those who face it and those left behind.

6 sections; each heading opens the text

HECUBA. CHORUS.

Polyxena considers death preferable to living as a slave without honor. Hecuba begs to die with her daughter, but Ulysses says that Polyxena's death alone is required.

ORESTES.

Death is presented as the expected outcome for Orestes because he cannot eat and has been condemned by the city. Electra says that she will choose to live or die with him.

ALCESTIS.

Death is treated as a power that cannot ordinarily be escaped, though Apollo temporarily alters its appointed course through a substitute. Alcestis and Admetus both recognize that mortality separates them, while Admetus imagines reunion after his own death.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Hercules says that death is a debt all mortals must pay and that no one knows whether he will live until the next day. Pheres and Admetus's quarrel also contrasts the claims of old age, youth, self-preservation, and the sacrifice made by Alcestis.

Iphigenia treats death as a willing and honorable act rather than an ignoble one. She says that her death will become her memorial, wedding, children, and glory, while Clytemnestra understands it as a loss requiring mourning.

CHOR. Thou hast told wondrous things concerning him who has appeared,

Death is imminent for Orestes and potentially Pylades because of the sacrificial rites. Orestes argues that mourning cannot prevent death, while Iphigenia describes the fire, rocky chasm, and funeral honors intended for the one who is sacrificed.

death in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Death is portrayed as a silent, hidden destination for the condemned: a deep house and eternal bridal chamber for the dead. For Antigone it is a grave fate imposed while she lives; for Aias it is a welcome passage to painless rest. Its aftermath brings grief, danger, uncertainty, and conflict over burial. Electra contrasts desired death with the misery of being unable to die.

Narrative, section by section

CHORUS

Antigone describes death as a silent strand, a deep house, and an eternal bridal chamber where Persephone has housed the dead. She is sent toward death while still alive, and the Chorus treats her fate as a grave consequence of mortal and divine powers.

death and mortality in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Death and mortality are the unavoidable common end of all mortals, portrayed as both punishment and release from life’s burdens. Creon uses death to bar Antigone’s marriage and punish her burial; Antigone accepts it amid misery. Oedipus awaits his appointed death, Polyneices fears it, and the Chorus sees it ending inherited suffering.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

[Enter ANTIGONE and ISMENE with THESEUS]

The Chorus declares that excessive length of life brings more burdens and that death finally releases everyone from life’s troubles. Oedipus accepts that his death is appointed, while Polyneices recognizes that his expedition may lead him to death.

[Enter GUARD bringing ANTIGONE]

Antigone accepts that she must die and says that death is a gain for someone whose life is full of misery. Creon makes death the penalty for her burial, while the Chorus presents the suffering of the Labdacidae as a continuing punishment across generations.
