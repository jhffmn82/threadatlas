Called Jupiter’s daughter and said to be born in Crete near the Triton, she guards Bacchus, or Dionysius, at Nysa and leads women in his army. Minerva in The Odyssey

Homer · -0632~/-0599~

Minerva is an immortal goddess, Jove’s daughter, who dwells on Olympus and is a protective, changeable divine ally of Ulysses and his family. She favors Ulysses from the Trojan War onward, assists in major earlier deeds, and repeatedly uses disguises, a golden wand, mist, winds, sleep, beauty, courage, and battle aid to advance his return and restore his household. At the beginning, concerned by Ulysses’ suffering on Calypso’s island and Neptune’s persecution, she presses for help and goes to Ithaca as Mentes and Mentor to strengthen Telemachus. She directs him to call an assembly, confront the suitors, and seek news of his father; she arranges and protects his voyage, later recalls him from Lacedaemon, and warns him of the suitors’ ambush. Meanwhile she aids Ulysses at sea, protects Nausicaa, guides him among the Phaeacians, and helps secure his passage home. Back in Ithaca, she conceals and disguises Ulysses, coordinates plans with him and Telemachus, directs him to Eumaeus, and prepares the downfall of the suitors. She also comforts and enhances Penelope, influences the bow contest, and gives Ulysses strength and protection during the fight, while making the suitors’ weapons ineffective. After the suitors are destroyed, she restores Laertes’ strength, directs his attack, and finally intervenes in the civil conflict to halt bloodshed and establish peace between the contending parties.

Athena in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Athena is a bright-eyed, grey-eyed, queenly, host-leading, battle-loving goddess: daughter of Zeus, holder of a dark aegis and a host-scaring weapon, and equal to Zeus in strength and wise understanding. Destined as Metis’s very wise daughter, she is born from Zeus’s head already armed for war and resides with him on Olympus. She delights in Ares, wars, and battle-craft, yet confronts him when he fights Heracles: she encourages Heracles and Iolaus, conveys Zeus’s grant of power against Cycnus, instructs Heracles how to wound Ares, rides with them, warns Ares, and turns aside his spear. With Hephaestus she teaches men glorious gifts. She disputes fairness with Aphrodite, is judged among three goddesses by Alexandrus, and polishes Peleus’s spear. In the struggle around Troy, she helps Odysseus gain Achilles’ arms, directs Epeius in building the wooden horse, receives the Trojans’ dedication of it, and is angered by Aias’s treatment of her image and Cassandra; she plans destruction for the departing Greeks and causes Agamemnon and Menelaus to quarrel. Finally, offended by damage to her temple possessions and by the Frogs’ noise, she refuses aid to both Mice and Frogs and tells the gods to watch their battle.

Minerva in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Minerva is a goddess, daughter of aegis-bearing Jove and child of Zeus, marked by her priceless, tasselled aegis and stout spear. A marshaller of hosts and goddess of plunder, she is chiefly aligned with the Achaeans and Danaans in the conflict against Troy, usually aiding through counsel, courage, disguise, and selective intervention rather than open fighting. She serves Juno by preventing the Achaeans’ departure, restores order through Ulysses, urges wavering fighters onward, and descends at Jove’s command to induce the breaking of oaths while protecting Menelaus from a mortal wound. Minerva is a goddess, divine female warrior, daughter of Jupiter, and a warlike, yellow-haired virgin also called Tritonia and Pallas. She springs from Jupiter’s head and is said to have been born near Trito. She animates Man and Prometheus’s workmanship, and with Prometheus represents formation and animation of man. She is patroness and tutelary divinity of Athens, associated with and owning the olive tree, and is credited by some with making linen cloths and nets. Angered that Aglauros revealed her mysteries, she commands the hated Envy to make Aglauros jealous of Herse. As Cadmus’s favorer, she directs him to sow dragon’s teeth, producing armed earth-born warriors. She punishes impiety, changes Medusa’s hair to serpents, gives Perseus a reflecting shield, and accompanies him until leaving Seriphus. After traveling to Mount Helicon and visiting the Muses, she becomes Arachne’s rival. Disguised as an old woman, she warns Arachne, reveals herself, weaves a web of Athens and divine contests, then tears Arachne’s excellent web, strikes her, and transforms her into a spider. She also plays the flute but throws it aside in disgust after seeing its effect on her appearance.

Athene in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Athene is a goddess, widely worshipped in Athens and Attica and patron deity of Athens. In Libyan tradition she is associated with Poseidon and Tritonian Marsh as daughter, is represented with grey eyes, and is also portrayed springing from the head of Zeus. Her defining contest is with Poseidon for the soil of Attica: she displays and possesses the olive of Athene, which served as proof of her claim. Her most sacred statue stood on the Acropolis, formerly called the Polis; Athenians said it had fallen from heaven. Its temple held the sacred olive, ancient statues and offerings, and Callimachus’s golden lamp, which burned for a year when filled with oil. Her images and sanctuaries extend across many places. At Piraeus she appears armed with a spear beside Zeus; at Cnidus she is called Athene of the Fair Voyage. She has statues in the temple of Hephaestus, at Corinth, Titane, and elsewhere, including brazen works by Phidias and the Lemnian Athene. She is shown with Theseus and Hercules, her shield bears the battle of Theseus and the Amazons, and she is portrayed striking Marsyas after he took up flutes she had discarded. She is also called Athene the worker, Athene Area, Goddess of Horses, the Bridler, Injurious Athene, and Sthenias. Her cult includes temples, altars, and rites from Megara, Sparta, Phrixa, and Pontinus to Trœzen, Hermione, and Buporthmus. On the island once called Sphæria, Æthra built her a temple after a dream, and maidens of Trœzen dedicated maiden-girdles to Injurious Athene before marriage. As the Bridler, she aided Bellerophon, giving him Pegasus after breaking in and bridling him.

Athene in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Athene is a goddess worshipped under several local names and forms, especially at Aliphera, where inhabitants say she was born and reared and where she has a temple, bronze statue, and festival. Accounts also connect her with the river Triton, though the text disputes whether this is the local river near Alalcomenae or one in Libya. She invents contrivances, gathers flowers with Proserpine in an alternative identification, and is called Onga by Thebans, Hippia by Manthurii, and Alea by other Greeks and Peloponnesians. In action, she drove Enceladus's chariot in the battle of gods and giants, tried as Melas to prevent Teuthis from leaving Aulis, was wounded by him, appeased Hercules, defended Elatea, cursed Cylo's party after the destruction of her suppliants, and aided Tegea through Cepheus with a promise and Medusa's lock. Her worship is marked by temples, shrines, altars, festivals, and many statues: standing beside Zeus, in marketplaces and theatres, in groves, and as images of ivory, gold, bronze, stone, and a wounded thigh. These images preserve both her protection and the local stories attached to her.

Athene stands beside Olympian Zeus in his temple, and a separate statue of her stands in the open air in the market-place. Her statue in the temple called Pan-Achæis is made of ivory and gold.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Athene is worshipped especially at Aliphera, where the inhabitants say that she was born and reared. Aliphera has a temple and a notable bronze statue of Athene by Hypatodorus, and its public festival is thought to be dedicated to her.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Athene tried to stop Teuthis from marching home from Aulis while appearing in the semblance of Melas the son of Ops. Teuthis wounded her thigh with his spear, and later the people of Teuthis made a statue of her with a wound in the thigh to propitiate her after oracular responses from Dodona.

CHAPTER XLVII.

Athene is called Hippia by the Manthurii because they say she drove the chariot of Enceladus in the battle between the gods and giants; other Greeks and Peloponnesians call her Alea. Her altar at Tegea is said to have been made by Melampus and bears representations of Rhea, the Nymph Œnoe with Zeus as a baby, eight Nymphs, the Muses, and Mnemosyne.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Athene had a temple in the plain near Alalcomenae and was associated with the local river called Triton. The text says that some claimed she was brought up near the river Triton, though the author argues this should refer to the Triton in Libya rather than the local stream.

CHAPTER XIII.

Athene was included among the statues dedicated by the Phocians from spoil taken from the Thessalians. In the group depicting the contest between Hercules and Apollo, she tried to appease Hercules.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

An ancient bronze statue of Athene stood in the theatre at the extreme right of Elatea. The Elateans said that Athene fought for them against the barbarians under Taxilus.

Athena in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Athena is a goddess, Olympian deity, daughter of Zeus, and Trito-born figure called Pallas and Tritonis. She possesses the stormy Aegis and, when Zeus grants them, weapons no other god can lift. Much of her course makes her an ally and divine supporter of the Achaeans: she sends Penthesileia a deceitful dream in her father’s likeness, drawing her into battle against Achilles and toward ruin for herself and Troy; favors Odysseus for his sacrifices; and prevents Aias from attacking Odysseus and the Argives by maddening him, redirecting his violence, and later removing his delusion. She gives the Achaeans glory and advantage in battle, opposes Ares on their behalf, and is restrained with him by Zeus’s thunder before leaving for Athens. She reconciles Philoctetes to Odysseus and Diomede and sends his ship a favorable breeze. She helps the Greeks devastate Troy, directs Epeius in a dream to build the Wooden Horse, aids its making, blinds Laocoon for opposing it, and rejoices when it enters Troy. She then sends serpents that kill Laocoon’s sons. Her support turns to wrath after Cassandra is violated in her temple by Oileus’ son. Seeking vengeance, she asks Zeus for his weapons, raises a storm against the returning Argive fleet, destroys many ships, and strikes Aias’s ship with a thunderbolt, while remaining concerned for Odysseus.

Minerva in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Minerva, also called Pallas Athene, is a goddess of wisdom, useful and ornamental arts, and defensive war. Daughter of Jupiter without a mother, she springs fully armed from Jupiter’s head; the owl is her favorite bird and the olive is sacred to her. She presides over agriculture, navigation, and women’s spinning, weaving, and needle-work. She wins Athens from Neptune by producing the more useful olive, but punishes challenges: she changes Medusa’s hair to serpents and transforms Arachne into a spider. She aids Perseus with her shield, receives Medusa’s head for her Aegis, invents thunderbolts for Jupiter, and gives Bellerophon a golden bridle. Hostile to Paris and the Trojans after the beauty contest, she is linked to the Wooden Horse. At Athens she protects Orestes and appoints the Areopagus court. As Ulysses’s patron, she calms waves, sends favorable winds and dreams, guides and conceals him, and helps him return and reveal himself.
