glory and reputation in war in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

A topic concerning honor and fame in war: Macaria gains lasting glory through willing death for her brothers, while generals’ credit is challenged against soldiers’ suffering, and the Chorus warns that victory won unjustly yields fading, shameful renown.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

DEMOPHOON, IOLAUS, CHORUS.

Macaria seeks honorable fame by dying willingly for her brothers rather than living as an abandoned and dishonored refugee. The Chorus says that her virtue will be praised in the grave, and Iolaus declares that her glory will surpass that of other women.

PELEUS, MENELAUS, ANDROMACHE, MOLOSSUS, CHORUS.

Peleus criticizes generals and rulers who receive glory while ordinary soldiers endure the toil and danger of battle. The Chorus says that victory gained through injustice brings shame and that unlawful fame withers over time.

honor and noble reputation in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Honor and noble reputation is pursued by Orestes through a free or noble death and embodied by Macaria, whose willing death for her brothers, chosen over cowardly survival or abandonment, earns remembrance, honorable burial, virtue, and glorious reputation.

CHOR. This land is ever willing to aid in a just cause those in difficulty;

Macaria chooses death because she considers it more honorable than surviving as a coward or being abandoned after the fall of the city. She asks to be remembered and buried honorably, and the Chorus declares that her virtue will win a glorious reputation among men.

glory in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Glory is divine-given fame and honor, granted by Zeus and other divine favor, and bestowed as a prize for victory, brave deeds, civic achievement, proper rule, and skillful song. It is called the second lot after happiness and the highest reward of success in games or war. At first it belongs to Alkimedon’s house through crowns from the wreathed games and is asked to continue there without envy or discontent. It is then stored in ample measure for Olympian winners and placed by sweet strains on Agesidamos’ golden-olive crown. The odes connect lasting good report to truthful speech, generosity, and loving-kindness, while cruelty, hidden wealth, mockery, and lust for gain oppose honor and lead to hated or absent fame. Finally, glory from athletic and heroic achievement is preserved and spread by poetry: song becomes a beacon over earth and sea, giving Phylakidas, Aigina’s heroes, and others enduring remembrance.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

VIII.

Fame and honor are presented as gifts granted differently to different people through divine favor and as the proper reward for noble deeds. The ode asks that honor continue to come to Alkimedon’s house, whose clan has received six crowns from the wreathed games, and that its glory be shared without envy or discontent.

X.

The poem describes ample glory as stored for Olympian winners. Pindar promises to place such glory in sweet strains upon Agesidamos' crown of golden olive.

I.

The ode urges Hieron to seek good report through truthful speech, generous action, and proper rule. It teaches that loving-kindness, like that of Craesus, can remain memorable, while cruelty, like that of Phalaris, is remembered with hatred; happiness is called the chief prize and glory the next.

Nemean Ode IX

Honor and glory arise from brave deeds, divine song, military courage, civic achievement, and athletic victory. The ode says that glory won together with great wealth gives a mortal no higher mountain-top to reach, while lust for gain can secretly steal the spirit of honor.

Isthmian Ode I

Fame is described as a valuable reward for victory in games or war, greater than the rewards sought by ordinary workers merely to escape hunger. The text warns that a person who hides wealth and mocks others may die without having known good fame.

Isthmian Ode III

Glory and fame arise from brave deeds and skillful song. The ode presents poetic praise as a beacon whose light can shine over the earth and sea and preserve excellence for all time.

Isthmian Ode IV

Glory comes through success in games, fair fame, heroic deeds, and their telling in song. Phylakidas receives glory at Isthmos and Nemea, while Aigina’s heroes gain enduring glory through poetry and worship.
