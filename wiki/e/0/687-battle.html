Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, the battle is the fierce close combat between the Achaeans and Trojans centered on the ships, with its course reversing before the Trojans finally flee. In The Rhesus of Euripides, Troy's coming battle is the planned dawn attack in which Hector expects Troy to defeat the Greeks, break their rampart, and burn their galleys. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, the battle for the Grecian ships is the struggle in which the Trojans threaten the Greek ships, Patroclus drives them away, and the Greeks fight to recover Patroclus's body.

battle in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where battle first appears

Abstract written by a model

This is a battle and close combat between the Achaeans, Argives, Danaans, and Trojans, centered on the ships after the Trojans break through the wall and gates. It matters because both sides see its outcome as deciding whether the Achaeans survive or the Trojans burn the fleet. It begins as fierce close-quarters fighting, with dense formations and spears, swords, bows, axes, and hatchets; men die, bodies are defended or stripped of armor, and fighting centers around Alcathous. The struggle rages most fiercely by the wall and ships: the left wing favors the Argives, while the Trojans press the ships. Neptune’s cry strengthens the Achaeans, and after Hector is removed they gain confidence, though casualties fall on both sides. The course then reverses when Neptune withdraws and Apollo intervenes: Apollo’s aegis panics the Achaeans and the Trojans drive them back into a narrow space by the ships. Patroclus restores Achaean momentum and forces the Trojans back. Finally, the combat becomes scattered; chieftains kill one another, the Trojans lose formation and flee before a broad Danaan assault.
