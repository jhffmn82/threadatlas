Lycaon

person · 4 works · 32 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Arcadian tyrant, Lycaon the son of Pelasgus, father, father-in-law, master, Λυκάονα, Λυκάων
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Lycaon, Callisto’s father, receives her and her babe, entertains Zeus while feigning ignorance, and serves the cut-up babe at his banquet.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Lycaon is an Arcadian king and tyrant who tests Jupiter by serving him human flesh and is transformed into a wolf.
  • In The Library, Lycaon, son of Pelasgus and king of the Arcadians, fathers fifty sons and is destroyed by Zeus with a thunderbolt after his sons prepare an impious banquet.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Lycaon, son of Pelasgus, founds Lycosura, names Zeus Lycæus, establishes the Lycæa, fathers many children, and is said to become a wolf after sacrificing a baby.

THE ASTRONOMY

Lycaon was Callisto's father. He received Callisto and her babe after goat-herds gave them up to him, and, according to Hesiod, entertained Zeus while pretending not to know what had happened and served him the cut-up babe.

Lycaon in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Lycaon is an Arcadian king and tyrant associated with a polluted banquet, remarkable cruelty, impiety, and a plot against Jupiter. When Jupiter comes as a guest, Lycaon mocks the people’s prayers and tests whether Jupiter is a God or mortal by planning to kill him. He cuts the throat of a Molossian hostage, cooks the body, and serves human flesh at a feast. Jupiter punishes him by destroying his house and transforming him into a wolf. The records also preserve conflicting reports: Pausanias and Suidas call him a virtuous prince, benefactor, and promoter of improvement, and the story is possibly based on Cain.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Lycaon is associated with a polluted banquet and is described as remarkable for his cruelty. He has formed a plot against Jupiter, and Jupiter states that he has suffered dire punishment.

opening

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, mocks the people's prayers and tests whether Jupiter is a God by planning to kill him in his sleep. He kills a hostage from the nation of the Molossians, cooks the body, and serves it at a feast; Jupiter then destroys his house, and Lycaon becomes a wolf.

notes .. notes

Lycaon is described as the figure who set human flesh before Jupiter and was punished by being transformed into a wolf. The text suggests that his story may preserve elements of the account of Cain, while also reporting that some writers distinguished two Lycaons.

Lycaon in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lycaon, son of Pelasgus, founded Lycosura on Mount Lycæus, named Zeus Lycæus, and established the Lycæa. He fathered many sons, including Nyctimus and youngest Œnotrus, and his only daughter Callisto. After sacrificing a baby at Zeus Lycæus’s altar, he was said to become a wolf.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II.

Lycaon, the son of Pelasgus, founded Lycosura on Mount Lycæus, called Zeus Lycæus, and established the Lycæa. He sacrificed a baby on the altar of Zeus Lycæus and was said to become a wolf afterward.

CHAPTER III.

Lycaon was the father of Nyctimus, Œnotrus, and many other sons, as well as his only daughter, Callisto.
