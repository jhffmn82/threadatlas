Works and Days in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Works and Days is a didactic poem, the central poem of the didactic group, whose moral purpose is to show men how best to live in a difficult world through justice, industry, prudence, and care. Across four main sections, it begins with moral conduct and justice, then gives guidance on agriculture and trading by sea as ways to escape want and misery. It next offers domestic and everyday precepts, and ends with notices of favourable and unfavourable days. Its name derives from agriculture and days of the month. It depends on Homer in diction, dialect, and style and is earlier than Theogony.

The Hesiodic Poems

The Works and Days consists of sections on moral conduct and justice, agriculture and seafaring, domestic and everyday behavior, and favorable or unfavorable days. Its real purpose is presented as moral rather than technical: it teaches how to live as well as possible in a difficult world through justice, industry, prudence, and care. It is described as the oldest and most original of the Hesiodic poems, probably dating from the middle of the ninth century B.C.

BIBLIOGRAPHY

The Works and Days is represented by one papyrus and numerous medieval manuscripts, which Rudolf Rzach divides into six families issuing from a common original. Papyrus evidence produced new lines at 169 a-d and improved readings at 278.

THE DIVINATION BY BIRDS

Verse 828 of Works and Days is identified as the verse after which some place The Divination by Birds.

CHAPTER XXXI.

The Works and Days is attributed by some people at Helicon as the only work written by Hesiod, with its opening address to the Muses removed so that it begins with the section about Strife. A lead copy of the poem was shown near Hippocrene, though it was almost entirely rotten with age.
