Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Orestes takes vengeance for Agamemnon’s murder together with Pylades. In The Odyssey, Orestes returns from Athens, kills Aegisthus in revenge for Agamemnon, and performs funeral rites for his mother and Aegisthus. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Lakonian Orestes is saved after Agamemnon’s murder, raised near Parnassos, and later kills Clytaemnestra and Aigisthos. In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Orestes avenges Agamemnon by killing Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, then is pursued by the Furies and acquitted at Athens. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Orestes falsely reports his own death, reveals himself to Electra, kills Clytemnestra, and confronts Aegisthus. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Orestes avenges Agamemnon by killing Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, suffers madness and persecution, and escapes Tauris with Iphigenia and Diana’s image. In Hecuba and other plays, Orestes plots and engineers the death of Hermione’s husband, Achilles’ son, after murdering his own mother. In The Electra of Euripides, Orestes returns from exile to avenge Agamemnon, kills Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, and is sent to Athens for trial.

In Iphigenia in Tauris, Orestes travels to Tauris with Pylades to seize Artemis’s image, recognizes Iphigenia, and escapes with her. In Heroides and Amores, Orestes avenges Agamemnon by killing Aegisthus, though he is condemned for taking vengeance on his mother, and Hermione urges him to reclaim her. In The Library, Orestes avenges Agamemnon, is pursued by the Furies, is acquitted at the Areopagus, escapes from the Taurians with Iphigenia, and later marries Hermione and dies from a snakebite. In Description of Greece, Orestes is purified and tried for shedding his mother’s blood, escapes punishment, and later gains Argos, Arcadian territory, and Sparta. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Orestes is described as Agamemnon’s son and as a Roman envoy who settles disputes, after surviving madness, purification, and trial. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Orestes avenges Agamemnon by killing Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, is pursued by the Erinnyes, travels to Tauris, and is acquitted at Athens.

Orestes in The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies

Aeschylus · 457 BC · the work’s page · read it where Orestes first appears

Abstract written by a model

Orestes is the son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, brother of Electra, an Argive, and an orphaned child. While Agamemnon is away, he is kept in Phocis under Strophius’s loyal protection because of danger; after Agamemnon’s murder, he becomes an exiled returnee expected to come back. He returns from banishment with Pylades to Agamemnon’s tomb, offers shorn locks of hair, prays for help, and observes the mourners before revealing himself to Electra. Bound by Apollo’s oracle and regarding vengeance as a sacred obligation, he resolves to avenge Agamemnon by killing Clytemnestra and Aegisthus. Clytemnestra’s dream compares him to a serpent, which he takes as foretelling her death. With Pylades’ aid, he enters the palace disguised as a traveller, falsely reports his own death, kills Aegisthus and Clytemnestra with a sword, and displays Agamemnon’s robe, used to entangle Agamemnon. The act makes him a matricide. He sees the Furies as his mother’s hell-hounds, suffers madness and visions, and flees while pursued by them. Protected and directed by Apollo, he clings to Apollo’s altar, is purified, wanders through many lands, and becomes a suppliant at Athena’s shrine and statue. Before Athena and Athenian judges, he admits the killing but says Apollo compelled it. Athena’s deciding vote frees him from blood-guilt. Restored to Argos, he swears that no Argive chieftain will lead an army against Athens, then leaves with Apollo.

Orestes in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · 449 to 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Orestes first appears

Abstract written by a model

Orestes is the son and heir of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, brother of Electra and Chrysothemis. Secretly taken to Phocis in childhood and preserved by the Old Man, he returns to Mycenae at full age with Pylades and the Old Man. Apollo commands him to avenge Agamemnon’s murder, so he plans revenge through deception. A false report says he won Delphian contests but died in a chariot crash, was burned, and was brought home in an urn; this apparent death devastates Electra, though tomb offerings and a lock of hair briefly suggest his return. Alive, Orestes enters as a Phocian messenger carrying the urn, claims to report his own death, and reveals himself to Electra by his voice and Agamemnon’s signet. He then enters the palace, kills Clytemnestra, and confronts Aegisthus.

Orestes returns to Mycenae with Pylades to avenge Agamemnon's murder, acting on Apollo's command. He plans to pretend that he has died in a chariot accident while secretly preparing to attack his enemies.

_Enter_ CLYTEMNESTRA.

Orestes is reported to have won the foot-race at the Delphian games before entering a chariot race. He is said to have crashed after striking a stone, becoming entangled in the reins and being dragged by the horses until he died. His body was burned, and his ashes were placed in a bronze urn for burial in his homeland.

Orestes is believed by Chrysothemis to have returned because of the offerings and lock of hair at the tomb. Electra states that he is dead and that salvation can no longer come from him.

Orestes in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Orestes first appears

Abstract written by a model

Orestes is Agamemnon and Clytemnestra’s son, Electra and Iphigenia’s brother, and Pylades’ devoted friend and companion. At Apollo’s command, and with Electra and Pylades involved, he kills Aegisthus and Clytemnestra to avenge Agamemnon. This matricide defines his subsequent life: he is polluted, excluded or exiled from Argos and Mycenae, pursued by the Furies or Erinnyes, and afflicted by illness, sleeplessness, hunger, terror, weakness, and heaven-inflicted madness. Electra tends him, while Pylades steadfastly supports him. In Argos, Orestes defends the killing as vengeance for his father, but the Argive assembly condemns him and Electra to death by stoning. Facing death, they consider self-slaughter; with Pylades, Orestes also plans revenge on Menelaus, Helen, and Hermione. He enters Helen’s chamber with Pylades, threatens Hermione and the palace, and seeks survival. Apollo then directs him to leave the land, undergo trial at Athens for his mother’s blood, marry Hermione, and eventually rule Argos. He is also described as living for a year on the Parrhasian plain and as having a trial at the Areopagus. Still pursued during his wanderings, Orestes goes with Pylades to Tauri under Phoebus’s oracle to seize Diana’s image and obtain relief. Madness returns, he is captured, and he and Pylades are marked for sacrifice. Prepared to die, Orestes asks Pylades to preserve his memory and care for Electra. Iphigenia’s letter reveals that she is his sister; they recognize one another and reunite. Orestes escapes with Iphigenia and the sacred image, resisting pursuit, and Minerva orders them to take both Iphigenia and the image to Athens.

ORESTES.

Orestes killed Clytæmnestra after Apollo commanded him to do so, with Electra and Pylades sharing in the deed. He has since been afflicted with madness, weakness, sleeplessness, hunger, and terror at visions of the Eumenides; Electra watches over and comforts him.

MENELAUS, ORESTES, CHORUS.

Orestes identifies himself to Menelaus as Agamemnon's son and describes his disordered body, madness, visions, social exclusion, and impending sentence to death by stoning. He defends killing Clytæmnestra as vengeance for Agamemnon and appeals to Menelaus to save his life and Electra's.

PYLADES, ORESTES, CHORUS.

Orestes is guarded in the palace and faces trial by the Argive citizens for killing his mother. He explains that he acted to avenge Agamemnon, but the assembly condemns him and Electra to die. He suffers from heaven-inflicted madness and plans to die by self-slaughter with Electra, while Pylades supports and guides him.

ELECTRA, ORESTES, PYLADES, CHORUS.

Orestes is condemned to die with Electra and considers killing himself with a sword rather than suffering a disgraceful death. He agrees with Pylades to murder Helen, seize Hermione as a hostage, and take revenge on Menelaus. He recalls that he slew his mother and prays to Agamemnon and Zeus for success.

PHRYGIAN, CHORUS.

Orestes enters Helen's chamber with Pylades, kills Clytemnestra, and attempts to kill Helen. He threatens to kill Hermione and burn the palace unless Menelaus helps secure his survival, but Apollo orders him to leave the land for a year, stand trial at Athens for shedding his mother's blood, marry Hermione, and eventually rule Argos.

notes

Orestes is described as having avenged his father, Agamemnon. The notes also refer to his infirmities and to Pylades’ voluntary attachment to his misfortunes.

IPHIGENIA IN TAURIS.

Orestes comes to Tauri with Pylades to take Diana's image, following Phœbus's oracle. He suffers an attack of madness near the sea, imagines that the Furies are pursuing him, attacks cattle, and is eventually captured with Pylades.

CHOR. Thou hast told wondrous things concerning him who has appeared,

Orestes arrives in Tauris with Pylades, bound for sacrifice. He identifies himself as an Argive from Mycenae but conceals his name, reports that Agamemnon, Clytemnestra, and Electra are dead or believed dead, and says that he is fleeing unwillingly and willingly. He refuses to let Pylades be sacrificed in his place and accepts that Iphigenia intends to sacrifice him.

CHOR. I deplore thee now destined to the gory streams of the lustral

Orestes accepts that he is destined to die in the planned sacrifice and urges Pylades to leave him rather than be killed with him. He asks Pylades to preserve his memory, care for his sister, and tell Argos that he died by an Argive woman's hand. He recognizes Iphigenia as his sister when her letter names Orestes and when she confirms memories of their ancestral home.

IPH. O dearest one, no more, for thou art dearest. I hold thee, Orestes,

Orestes embraces Iphigenia and tells her that he is an exile from Argos because the Erinnyes pursue him after he killed his mother. He explains his trial at the hill of Mars and Phoebus’s command that he seize Diana’s image and take it to Athens.

Orestes is identified as the son of Agamemnon and the brother of Iphigenia, whom she has consecrated to the Goddess at the Tauric altars. He escapes with Iphigenia and the sacred image, fights with the men sent to stop them, and attempts to sail away. Minerva orders him to take Iphigenia and the image to Athens.

notes

Orestes is associated with wanderings, pollution, and the need for purification after his earlier actions. In the play he reaches Tauris, is recognized by Iphigenia, and escapes with her.

Abstract written by a model

Orestes is a Greek, the first brought to the Taurian altar, and the firstborn son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, brother of Iphigenia and Electra, native to Mycenae and an exile from Argolis. He kills Clytemnestra to avenge Agamemnon, but is cursed, pursued by Furies, and driven into madness. Orestes in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Orestes first appears

Abstract written by a model

Orestes, son of Agamemnon, was accused of shedding his mother’s blood, housed and fed in a tent at Troezen until gradually purified, with Hippocrene water linked to his purgations. Tried at the Areopagus, he escaped punishment and erected an altar of Athene. After Cylarabes died childless, he gained Argos, Arcadian territory, and Sparta. Spartan tradition credits him with bringing the Orthian Artemis cult; his tomb was at Sparta.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVIII.

Orestes, the son of Agamemnon, acquired Argos after Cylarabes died childless. He had also gained much Arcadian territory and the kingdom of Sparta, whose people accepted him as king because they preferred the descendants of Tyndareus’ daughters to Menelaus’ sons by a bondmaid.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Orestes was received in a building at Troezen called his tent after being accused of shedding his mother’s blood, because the people would not house him until he was purified. He was gradually cleared and fed there, and his descendants continued to hold feasts at the site; traditions also connected his purifications with the local Hippocrene water.

Orestes in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Orestes first appears

Abstract written by a model

Orestes is described both as Agamemnon’s son and as a Roman envoy based at Corinth to settle disputes between the Lacedæmonians and Achæans. After murdering his mother Clytæmnestra, he went mad at Maniæ, damaged a finger, was healed at Ace, cut his hair, and faced pursuit by the Furies before an Areopagus trial. Following Apollo’s oracle, he migrated from Mycenæ to Arcadia; later he summoned Achæan leaders and urged restraint toward Lacedæmonian refugees.

CHAPTER XIV.

Orestes arrived in Greece among the Roman envoys and summoned Diæus and the principal people of the Achæan cities. He announced the Roman Senate’s position and tried to stop the Achæans from assaulting Lacedæmonians who had taken refuge at his headquarters.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Orestes was said to have gone mad at Maniæ after murdering his mother and to have gnawed off one of his fingers there. He was healed at Ace, cut off his hair at the Shearing-place, and was pursued by the Furies of his mother Clytæmnestra before the trial before the Areopagus.
