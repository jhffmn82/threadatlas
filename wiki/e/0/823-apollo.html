Apollo in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Apollo is a god, son of Latona and prophet of Zeus, associated with prophecy, the tripod, the bow, music, light, and divine command. He killed Python, leads a choir, gives heavenly lyre music, and habitually carries Apollo’s bow. As oracle-giver and prophet, he foretells Oedipus’s killing of Laius and gives Laius an oracle. His most consequential actions concern Orestes: he commands the murder of Clytæmnestra, gives Orestes a bow to repel the Furies, and leaves Orestes suffering consequences that prompt questions about the justice of his command. Apollo then preserves Helen, stops conflict, orders Orestes’ trial at Athens, testifies for and saves him at the Areopagus, directs marriages and rule in Argos, and finally sends him to seize Diana’s image, promising release. In another course of service, Zeus punishes Apollo, who becomes Admetus’s servant, shepherd, guardian, and protector at Pherae. Because Admetus is pious, Apollo obtains from the Fates a way for him to escape death through another’s death and foretells Hercules’ recovery of Alcestis from Death. He is also father of Asclepius.

6 sections; each heading opens the text

ORESTES.

Apollo commanded Orestes to kill Clytæmnestra, and Orestes says that Apollo offered consolation in words but not relief from the consequences. Apollo also gave Orestes a horn bow and said that it would repel the Furies. Apollo's oracle at the tripod of Themis led Orestes to commit the murder of his mother. Electra and the chorus question the justice of the command because it has brought madness and suffering.

Apollo in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Apollo is a deathless, far-shooting god, also called Phoebus, lord, and Far-shooter. He is the son of Zeus and Leto, brother and foster companion of Artemis, and father of Chaeron. He bears a bright curved or silver bow, a golden blade or sword, and a hollow lyre with golden keys. The lyre and bow are especially dear to him, and he plays among the gods of Olympus with radiant appearance. He alone is permitted to know Zeus’s counsel, declares Zeus’s unfailing will, practices divination, and teaches the three prophetic sisters. Through Apollo and the Muses come singers and lyre-players. Born on Delos after Leto’s long labor, Apollo makes Delos his principal and favored dwelling and reigns over it. There stand his temple and oracle; the Ionians honor him at their gathering, and the girls of Delos praise him before their songs. He is also associated with Lycia, Maeonia, and Miletus, has wooded groves in many places, and waits for Artemis at vine-clad Claros. His holy houses are at Pytho and in Delphi’s rich land. Seeking a place for an oracle, Apollo travels through many regions, establishes a temple and oracle at Crisa, kills the dragoness by its spring, and renames the place Pytho after she rots there. He punishes Telphusa by covering her streams with rocks. Diverting a Cretan ship, he brings the Cretans to Crisa to serve the sanctuary, instructs them, and promises abundant offerings. Apollo’s conflict with Hermes begins when Hermes steals Apollo’s cattle from Pieria. Apollo follows their reversed tracks, questions an old man at Onchestus, finds Hermes in Maia’s cave, carries him away, and brings the dispute before Zeus and the immortals. Delighted by Hermes’ lyre, Apollo exchanges his whip for it, becomes Hermes’ friend, and grants him a golden staff and prophetic privileges. He kills Meleager while Meleager fights the Curetes, slays Hyacinthus unwittingly with a discus, kills Amphilochus at Soli, and is represented as destroying the Cyclopes. He loves Hymenaeus and would not leave Magnes’ house; another account says he loved Ileus. A swan and a minstrel sing of him, and he is invoked to favor song.

Apollo in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Apollo is an immortal god, son of Jove/Zeus and Leto, lord of the silver bow and Far-Darter. He acts chiefly through divine force in the conflict between Achaeans and Trojans, serving Jove while repeatedly aiding Troy, Ilius, Hector, and Aeneas. When Agamemnon dishonors his priest Chryses, Apollo hears the priest’s prayer and sends a deadly pestilence among the Achaeans; it ends only after Chryseis is returned and sacrifice is made at Chryse. He rescues Aeneas from Diomed, hides him in darkness, takes him to Pergamus for healing by Latona and Diana, and creates a likeness of him to sustain the battle. He helps shape fighting through counsel, disguises, healing, and direct intervention: raising Hector, restoring his strength, carrying Jove’s aegis to panic the Achaeans, advancing the Trojans, and destroying the Achaean wall. He heals Glaucus and transports Sarpedon’s cleansed body to Lycia. He prevents Patroclus from taking Troy and helps make Hector’s attack on Patroclus possible. Later he incites Hector and Aeneas, strengthens Aeneas against Achilles, warns Hector against single combat, protects Hector in darkness, and defends Ilius by drawing Achilles away from its people. Yet his protection has a limit: after sustaining Hector during the pursuit, Apollo leaves when Hector’s doom is weighed, and Hector can no longer escape.

Apollo in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Apollo is presented chiefly as a god: an ennobled deity, supposed son of Hyperion and also called a son of Jupiter, Pythian God, God of Delphi, Delian God, Phœbus, and the Sun. He is an ever-youthful archer, healer, oracle, musician, and world-governing power, born on Delos and associated with the Sun, healing herbs, prophecy, victory, arrows, the harp, bow, lyre, and plectrum. His worship at Delphi is said not yet to have been known in Deucalion’s time; one explanation alternatively presents him as a god, prince, or priest who destroyed Python or a robber. After subduing Python, Apollo proudly mocks Cupid, but Cupid makes him love Daphne. He pursues and praises her; when she becomes a laurel, he claims the tree for his hair, lyre, and quiver and grants it lasting honor. He gives oracles at Patara for six months yearly and is linked with healing, though herbs cannot cure his love. As Phaëton’s claimed father, he is the Sun governing the world. After Phaëton’s death, he appears eclipsed and grief-stricken, withholds services from the world, and challenges Jupiter or another God to drive the chariot of light. He also loves Coronis: after hearing the raven’s report, he kills her, regrets it, rescues their child from the flames, and punishes the raven by barring it from white birds. Later, while in Elis and the Messenian fields, he tends cattle in shepherd’s clothing, occupied with love and pipe music, and Mercury steals stray cattle. Apollo is associated with plague and is said to have killed Niobe’s sons with arrows. Finally, challenged by Marsyas in music, he wins, fastens Marsyas to a tree, flays him, suspends his skin in Celenæ, and delivers his body to Olympus for burial.

Phœbus, also called Apollo and Hyperion, is a god: the bow-bearing, Delian son of Latona, twin brother of Diana, and a luminous heavenly body or sun whose chariot carries light to the universe. He is connected with Daphne’s transformation into a laurel and, before the laurel existed, wears garlands from any tree. Apollo in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Apollo is a god, a Delian god, son of Latona and Neptune’s nephew, associated with the lyre and bow. He is a skilled lyre player whose gem- and ivory-adorned lyre, plectrum, and purple robe are noted; he defeats Pan in Tmolus’s judgment. His loves bring grief and transformation: he loves Cyparissus, consoles him after the stag’s death, and makes him a cypress; he loves and is deeply loved by Hyacinthus, whose death from a rebounding discus Apollo laments, causing a flower to arise from his blood. He transforms Dædalion into a hawk and, in some accounts, takes the form of a wild boar. In mortal form with Neptune, he builds Troy’s walls for Laomedon. Later, concealed in a cloud, he directs Paris’s arrow to kill Achilles. At Delos, Anius serves him and his oracle guides the Trojans. He loves the Sibyl, offers her a vast lifespan and youth on conditions she rejects, and she ages. Finally, consulted at Delphi during Rome’s pestilence, his oracle directs the Romans to seek his son rather than Apollo himself.

Apollo in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Apollo is a deity represented, worshipped, and honoured across many places through temples, sanctuaries, statues, epithets, an oracle, and local traditions. A brazen statue outside the Parthenon, said to be by Phidias, calls him Apollo Averter of Locusts because he removed locusts that devastated the land. At Megara he was said to help Alcathous build the wall and to leave his harp on a stone which sounds like a harp when struck; the city also had several temples and forms of Apollo. He appears in Corinth, Tenea, Sicyon, Hermione, Didymi, Coccygium, Sparta, Amyclæ, Gythium, Epidelium, Zarax, Pyrrhichus, Las, and near Corone. His titles include Lycian or Wolf-god, Pythæan, Apollo of the Borders, Corydus, Carnean, Amazonius, Clarian, Pythius, Decataphorus, Archegetes, Protecting, Carinus, Latoan, and Averter of Locusts. At Sicyon he was credited with curing the daughters of Prœtus and destroying wolves. At Amyclæ women annually wove him a coat, and his large ancient statue stood on a base associated with Hyacinthus, whom he loved; he was worshipped there with Dionysus. Near Corone, his highly honoured and reputedly most ancient temple held a wooden statue and a brazen dedication said to be from the Argonauts, and the Messenians believed Corydus healed diseases. Other records present him as leading the Muses in song, victorious in racing and boxing at Olympia, taking Marpessa, and having amour with Creusa. His oracle of the Ridgeway was still functioning in the author’s time.

CHAPTER XXIV.

A brazen statue of Apollo outside the Parthenon is said to be by Phidias and is called Apollo Averter of Locusts. The name refers to the god’s removal of locusts when they destroyed the land, although the manner in which he did so is not known.

CHAPTER XLII.

Apollo is said by the Megarians to have helped Alcathous build the wall of Megara and to have placed his harp on a stone beside the altar of the Prodromi gods. The stone produces a sound like a harp when struck with a pebble. Megara also contains temples and statues of Apollo, including Apollo Pythius, Apollo Decataphorus, Apollo Archegetes, Protecting Apollo, Apollo Carinus, and Latoan Apollo.

BOOK II.--CORINTH.

Apollo was represented by a statue near the well of Pirene and by a statue called the Clarian in the Corinthian market-place. A temple and brazen statue of Apollo stood on the road from Corinth toward Sicyon, and Tenea held Apollo in the highest honour.

CHAPTER VI.

Apollo was worshipped at Sicyon in temples and sanctuaries, including the temple in the market-place and the temple of the Lycian, or Wolf-god, Apollo. The Sicyonians said that Apollo cured the daughters of Prœtus and destroyed wolves by directing that bark and flesh be placed before them.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Hermione has three temples and statues of Apollo: one without a title, one called Pythæan Apollo, and one called Apollo of the Borders. At Didymi there is also a temple of Apollo, and a temple on Coccygium was said to be Apollo’s.

CHAPTER XV.

Near the temple of Hilaira and Phœbe, the women of Sparta annually wove a coat for Apollo at Amyclæ, at a place called Coat. Apollo is also central to the Amyclæan sanctuary, where Bathycles made a throne decorated with numerous mythological scenes.

CHAPTER XIX.

Apollo is a god whose identity is expressed through worship, oracles, protection, conflict, love stories, and many temples, statues, and offerings. He is associated with oxen, appears in forms ranging from old wooden and cedarwood images to a square Hermæ, seated figures, bronze works, and a gold statue, and bears names including Pythian Apollo, Apollo the Helper, Averter of Ill, Ismenius, and Sitalcas. He aids communities in pestilence, especially the Phigalians and Athenians, and receives offerings after victories and deliverances. His loves include Bolina, who escapes into the sea, Daphne, and Acacallis; his love for Daphne is linked to jealousy of Leucippus and to the laurel crown. Temples, sanctuaries, sacred places, and oracles devoted to him endure across the records, though Delphi is repeatedly plundered and its statues removed. The account closes with his hippodrome near Delphi, Pythian horse-races, and the consequences of impiety against his sacred territory.

Apollo in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Apollo, also called Phoebus, is a god: son of Jupiter and Latona, brother of Diana, born on Delos, and associated with archery, prophecy, music, poetry, medicine, song, the lyre, and the sun. Apollo, or Phoebus Apollo, was the son of Jupiter and Latona and the brother of Diana. He was the god of archery, prophecy, music, and the sun, and he delighted the gods by playing the lyre.

PYTHON

Apollo slew Python and founded the Pythian games and his oracle at Delphi. He pursued Daphne after Cupid caused him to fall in love with her, and he later claimed the laurel as his tree. He was also described as the god of music, poetry, medicine, song, and the lyre.

Chapter IV

Apollo defeated Pan in the music contest and received Tmolus's judgment of victory. When Midas questioned the decision, Apollo changed Midas's ears into hairy, movable ears like those of an ass.

Chapter V

Apollo is passionately fond of Hyacinthus and joins him in sports and outdoor activities. During a game of quoits, Apollo's discus strikes and fatally wounds Hyacinthus; Apollo mourns him and changes his blood into a flower marked with his regret.

Chapter VIII

Apollo was loved by Clytie but made no return of her affection. Clytie continually gazed at the sun during its daily course because of her love for him.

Chapter IX

Apollo is the son of Latona and brother of Diana. In response to Niobe's insult, he helps kill her sons with arrows, though he would have spared Ilioneus if the arrow had not already left the string.

Chapter XVI

Apollo's son Aesculapius was killed by Jupiter's thunderbolt, and Apollo retaliated against the Cyclopes who had made it. As punishment, Apollo served Admetus for one year and pastured his flocks beside the Amphrysus.

THE ILIAD

Apollo sends pestilence into the Grecian camp after Chryses prays to him. Later, Jupiter sends Apollo to heal and encourage Hector, and Apollo strikes the helmet and lance from Patroclus before Patroclus is mortally wounded.

MYTH OF OSIRIS AND ISIS

Apollo became the sole divine source assigned to the prophetic oracle at Delphi after the power had earlier been attributed to Earth, Neptune, Themis, and others. His oracle was delivered through the inspired Pythia and interpreted by priests.

Chapter XXVIII

Apollo appears in the unit as one of the deities represented in celebrated ancient sculpture. The Apollo Belvedere is described as a victorious Apollo who has just shot the Python.

Apollo in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Apollo is a god, called Phoebus and the Pythian god, associated with Delphi, prophecy, hunting, and the lightnings of his Sire. Wise beyond concealment, he directs Thebes to purge Laius’s murder, is invoked to heal its plague, and pursues the murderer as an avenging power. Through his oracle, he gives Oedipus prophecies of defiling his mother’s bed, killing his father, suffering miseries, and eventually finding respite in a distant land. Oedipus holds Apollo responsible for bringing his sufferings to pass, though he says his own hand performed the blinding. Apollo also foretells Oedipus’s final resting-place, its blessing and curse, and signs of his final hour.

OEDIPUS THE KING

Apollo, called Phoebus and the Pythian god, gives the command that Thebes must purge the pollution caused by Laius’s murder. The Chorus asks Apollo to heal the plague-stricken city and reveal the murderer.

CHORUS.

Apollo is associated with the lightnings of his Sire and pursues the doer of the murder as the avenging Phoebus. The Chorus calls Apollo wise and says that nothing is hidden from him.

CREON.

Apollo sent Oedipus back from Delphi without the information he sought but gave him prophecies that he would defile his mother’s bed, raise abhorrent offspring, and kill the father from whose loins he sprang. The Chorus invokes Apollo and laments that faith in him has declined.

Apollo previously told Oedipus of his future miseries and foretold a respite after many years in a distant land. Oedipus says Apollo promised that he would become a blessing to the land where he dwells and a curse to the land that cast him out.
