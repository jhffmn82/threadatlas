  • In Heroides and Amores, the Trojans are a wealthy and populous Asian people who fight the Argives and prepare to welcome Helen with gifts, ceremonies, and honor.

  • In The Iliad, the Trojans defend Troy against the Achaeans, suffer reverses, assault the Greek ships, and flee into Ilius after Achilles returns; they mourn, bury, and honor Hector after Achilles kills him.

  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, the Trojans resist the Greek landing, defend Troy, admit the Wooden Horse, and after the city's destruction follow Aeneas into exile and war in Italy.

the Trojans in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

The Trojans are the surviving people of Troy, a Trojan race descended from Dardanus and led by Aeneas. After Troy falls, they endure the siege’s final defeat, flee with their people, gods, arms, and treasure, and become exiles destined under Jupiter’s decree to reach Italy. Their voyage lasts seven years and is marked by storms, failed settlements, plague, hostile creatures, religious guidance, and repeated preparations and departures. In Carthage, shipwrecked survivors request shelter from Dido, receive protection, and attend her feast; later they leave quickly at Aeneas’s command, prompting Dido’s curse of future war. They honor their dead and sustain communal life through funerals, sacrifices, games for Anchises, and a Sicilian settlement for those who do not continue. Guided by Helenus’s prophecy, they eventually reach the Tiber, recognize a promised sign, build a fortified camp, and peacefully seek admission to Latium. Though Latinus initially welcomes them, they become a foreign people opposed by local forces. They defend their camp against the Rutulians with tactics learned at Troy, suffer losses, seek Aeneas’s return, and receive Tuscan reinforcement. They fight alongside allies, mourn Pallas and their own dead, and continue the war despite proposals for settlement. In the final conflict, they prepare for a treaty and sacrifice, but fight again when it is broken. After being driven back, they recover under Aeneas, defeat opposing forces, and attack the city. They then stand by for Aeneas’s duel with Turnus. Jove decrees that they will merge with the Latins, sharing a common settlement and rites while retaining their customs under the peace settlement.

Trojans in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

The Trojans are the warrior people and army of Troy and Ilius, living there with their wives, little ones, Priam, and his people. Led chiefly by Hector and joined by allies from many places, including the Dardanians, they speak diverse languages and defend Troy for their families in war with the Achaeans over Helen. They hate Alexandrus for the danger he has caused, yet after Pandarus breaks the oaths by attacking Menelaus, they renew the war. They suffer reverses early but are repeatedly rallied by Hector, Apollo, Mars, Aeneas, and their allies. Jove’s promised aid gives them a major advantage: they drive the Achaeans through the trench, camp by the ships with watchfires and sentinels, breach the wall, and assault the fleet, seeking to burn it. Their strength fluctuates through fierce fighting at the ships, around fallen warriors, and over Patroclus’s body. They are at times routed, yet press to take Patroclus to Ilius and confine the Achaeans at their ships. Their advance ends when Achilles returns. Patroclus first drives them back, though Apollo helps Hector kill him; then Achilles defeats and pursues them across the plain and in Xanthus. They flee in panic into Ilius under Apollo’s protection. Hector remains outside as Troy’s chief defender and is killed by Achilles, leaving the city endangered. The Trojans mourn him throughout the city, then receive his body, burn it, gather his bones in a golden urn, raise a burial mound, and hold a feast in Priam’s house.

Trojans in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

The Trojans are the people, warriors, and defenders of Troy, led by Priam and fighting the Argives, Danaans, Achaeans, and Greeks in the war around their city. Their central purpose is to defend Troy, their homes, families, wives, lives, and country. They repeatedly depend on champions and allies: they welcome Penthesileia and fight with her Amazons, welcome Memnon and his Aethiopians, and later welcome Eurypylus. They also receive aid from Ares and courage from Phoebus, yet suffer recurring losses when their champions fall. At first, fear of Achilles confines them within their fortress, but Penthesileia's arrival brings hope and victories until Achilles kills her. They flee and mourn, then honor her and her Amazons with burial. They similarly rejoice at Memnon's arrival, fight with the Aethiopians, and mourn and retreat after his death. Achilles' death gives them great hope: they rejoice, make offerings at his pyre, and expect relief or flight by their enemies. Yet surviving Achaean champions remain dangerous. Under Eurypylus, they drive the Danaans toward the ships, but later recoil before Neoptolemus. After Eurypylus falls, they retreat behind Troy's walls. They continue to defend gates and towers, sometimes rallying fiercely and sometimes retreating in disorder, while burning their dead and debating whether to fight outside the city. Zeus briefly protects Troy in mist, and they keep watch in fear. Their end comes with the Wooden Horse. They torture Sinon but believe his story, ignore Laocoon's warning and other portents, refuse to burn the Horse from fear of the Gods' wrath, and drag it into Troy. They feast, become drunk, and sleep while the Danaan army returns. Fighting desperately with seized weapons, they are killed or enslaved. They are punished for Helen's abduction, violated guest-right and oaths, and profaned sacrifice; many die by Argives, fire, falling buildings, suicide, or despair, with no Gods saving them.

Trojans in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

The Trojans are a people and army first led by Hector in resistance to the Greek landing. They oppose the Greeks, receive support from Jupiter, drive them back to their ships, breach the Grecian rampart, and set a ship on fire before Patroclus forces a retreat. After Achilles’s assault, they flee into Troy, mourn and bury Hector, then mistakenly admit the Wooden Horse; the Greeks return at night and destroy the city. The surviving Trojans become exiles led by Aeneas, seeking a new home. They leave Thrace, Crete, and other dangers behind, endure hunger, storms, and attacks, and eventually reach Italy. At the Tiber’s mouth they recognize the promised land and seek friendship with Latinus, but Juno’s opposition brings war. Ordered by Aeneas to remain fortified during Turnus’s attack, they later resume fighting after he returns with allies.
