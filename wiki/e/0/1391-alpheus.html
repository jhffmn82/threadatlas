Alpheus

river · 4 works · 33 quoted facts · 17 connections
Also called: Alpheian, River Alpheus, deep-eddying Alpheus, deep-eddying river Alpheus, river Alpheus, the Alpheus, the river, the stream of Elis
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Alpheus is the deep-eddying river where Hermes casts his sandals and quenches the fire's embers.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Alpheus is the river that pursues Arethusa and changes into water to mingle with her.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Alpheus is the river whose course repeatedly disappears and reappears, eventually flowing into the sea and reportedly reemerging at Ortygia.
  • In Description of Greece, Alpheus is the large river associated with Olympia, its tributaries, and traditions of pursuing Arethusa and Artemis.

Alpheus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Alpheus is a river, also described as a stream of Elis, rising in Arcadia, running through the Eleans’ territory, disappearing underground and reappearing, and entering the sea near Cyllene. In the poetic story, he sees Arethusa bathing, loves and relentlessly pursues her across regions; after she becomes a stream, he changes from mortal form into water to mingle with her.

Alpheus sees Arethusa bathing and pursues her relentlessly across fields, mountains, and several regions. After Arethusa becomes a stream, he changes from mortal form into water so that he can mingle with her.

Alpheus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Alpheus is a large, pleasant river rising in Arcadia, not Elis. It has seven notable tributaries, including Helisson, Brentheates, Gortynius, Buphagus, Ladon, Erymanthus, and Cladeus, and later receives Diagon and Lucyanias as it flows through Pylos in Elis. It matters especially at Olympia: seers use it to renew the great altar, it is considered particularly friendly to Olympian Zeus, and it has altars with Artemis and alone. Traditions describe it as a hunter loving Arethusa, flowing under the sea to Ortygia, and unsuccessfully pursuing Artemis at Letrini.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

The Alpheus was a large and pleasant river whose source was in Arcadia rather than Elis. It received seven notable tributaries, including the Helisson, Brentheates, Gortynius, Buphagus, Ladon, Erymanthus, and the Cladeus. Traditions said that Alpheus was once a hunter who loved Arethusa and that the river flowed under the sea to join the spring at Ortygia, near Syracuse.

CHAPTER XI.

The Alpheus is used by the seers of Olympia when they knead sacrificial debris to renew the great altar on the nineteenth day of Elaphius. It is regarded as especially friendly to Olympian Zeus, and altars at Olympia are dedicated jointly to Alpheus and Artemis and separately to Alpheus.

CHAPTER XXI.

The Alpheus flows through the district of Pylos in Elis, which the people of Elis identify with the Pylos mentioned in Homer. It also receives the rivers Diagon and Lucyanias, and a local story says that Alpheus pursued Artemis at Letrini but failed to distinguish her from the mud-covered Nymphs.

Alpheus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Alpheus is a river whose course is described through repeated meetings, disappearances, and reappearances. It is said to rise at Carnasium, where Malus and Scyrus join it, and also to rise at Phylace, receiving a stream at the Meeting of the Waters. Near Asea it joins Eurotas, runs with it for about 20 stades, vanishes into a cavity, and emerges at Pegæ in Megalopolis; another account has it lost in the plain of Tegea, reappearing at Asea, then lost again and emerging at Wells. It receives Buphagus, Helisson, and Gatheatas, flows by Pisa and Olympia, passes beyond Cyllene into the sea, and is said to reappear at Ortygia in Syracuse, where it mixes with Arethusa.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXVI.

The Alpheus lies beside Heræa, and travelers cross it when going from Heræa to Aliphera. The Buphagus falls into the Alpheus.

CHAPTER XXIX.

The traveler crosses the Alpheus after passing Trapezus and again after visiting Basilis. The Helisson eventually flows into the Alpheus.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

The Alpheus is about 15 stades from Maniæ. The Gatheatas flows into it at a place reached from Maniæ, while the Carnion first flows into the Gatheatas.

CHAPTER XXXV.

The Alpheus has its rise at Carnasium, where the Malus and the Scyrus mingle their waters with it in one stream. A road from Megalopolis reaches the Alpheus after 30 stades and then follows its riverside.

CHAPTER XLIV.

The Alpheus flows near Asea, a little away from the road, and has a source near a roofless temple of the Mother of the Gods. It joins the Eurotas, flows with it for about 20 stades, disappears into a cavity, and reemerges at Pegæ in Megalopolis.

CHAPTER LIV.

The Alpheus rises at Phylace, receives another stream at the Meeting of the Waters, and disappears and reappears several times. After passing through Tegea, Asea, the districts of Pisa and Olympia, and beyond Cyllene, it is said to reappear at Ortygia in Syracuse and mix with the Arethusa.
