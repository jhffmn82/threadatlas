  • In The Amores, or Amours, Achilles is the Thessalian warrior said to be inflamed by the captive Briseis.
  • In Heroides and Amores, Achilles is the captain and lover who takes Briseis, withdraws from battle, refuses appeals to restore her, and is urged by Briseis to return.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Achilles is the son of Peleus and Thetis who joins Agamemnon in the Trojan War and kills Memnon.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Achilles is the greatest Greek warrior who kills Hector, dies at Troy from Paris’s shaft, and appears as a ghost demanding Polyxena’s sacrifice.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Achilles is the warrior who opposes the Greeks’ deceptive plan to sacrifice Iphigenia and later demands Polyxena as a tribute at his tomb.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Achilles is Neoptolemus’s father whose death and armor shape his son’s role in aiding Philoctetes.
  • In The Rhesus of Euripides, Achilles is mentioned as a powerful Greek warrior whose horses are an extraordinary breed associated with Poseidon and Peleus.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Achilles is the son of Peleus and Thetis who becomes the strongest warrior, kills major Trojan champions, dies by an arrow, and resides in the islands of the blest.
  • In The Odyssey, Achilles is the fallen Achaean champion who appears among the dead, asks after Neoptolemus and Peleus, and laments exchanging earthly life for the underworld.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Achilles is the swift-footed warrior who sacks cities, kills Penthesileia, Memnon, and Thersites, and appears after death to demand honor and warn against continuing the voyage.
  • In The Iliad, Achilles is the foremost Achaean warrior who withdraws from the Trojan War, returns to avenge Patroclus, kills Hector, and returns Hector’s body to Priam.
  • In The Library, Achilles is the son of Peleus and Thetis who leads the Myrmidons, fights at Troy, kills Hector and other enemies, and is killed by Alexander and Apollo.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Achilles is the Greek bulwark who defeats Cygnus, kills Hector and Memnon, dies from an arrow to his heel, and remains associated with Polyxena’s sacrifice after death.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Achilles’s ghost rises before the Greeks, reproaches them for forgetting his valor, and demands Polyxena’s sacrifice to honor his tomb.
  • In Description of Greece, Achilles is a hero remembered for fighting Memnon and honored by temples, statues, cults, and a cenotaph.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Achilles is the Greek champion who kills Penthesileia and Memnon, dies after Apollo strikes his ankle, and continues to influence events after death.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Achilles is the warrior and father of Neoptolemus whose armor, horses, and lance pass to his son after his death.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Achilles is the invulnerable Greek champion who withdraws over Briseis, returns after Patroclus’s death, kills Hector and Memnon, and is slain by Paris’s poisoned arrow.

Achilles in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Achilles is Peleus’s famed son, a Greek chief and the greatest Greek warrior. Angry with the chiefs, he refuses to assist the Argive host. In the Trojan War he kills Hector and drags Hector’s body around Troy’s walls. He is father of Neoptolemus and is also invoked as father of Molossus by family lineage. He dies for Greece at Troy, killed by Paris’s shaft, though others blame the Phrygians, Helen, or Menelaus. After death, his ghost appears at his tomb demanding Polyxena’s sacrifice; later he dwells on an Euxine-washed island.

Homer · -0749/-0724

Achilles is a great, mighty mortal hero and Achaean warrior, son of Peleus and Thetis, descendant of Aeacus, king and commander of the Myrmidons. He is the Achaeans’ foremost fighter, possesses immortal horses and exceptional weapons, and is protected and honored by gods, yet is doomed to a short life and to die at Troy after Hector. The central course of his story begins when Agamemnon dishonours him by taking Briseis, his awarded prize. Furious but restrained from attacking Agamemnon, Achilles withdraws from the Trojan War, keeps his Myrmidons from battle, rejects gifts and reconciliation, and asks through Thetis that Jove favor the Trojans. While the Achaeans suffer, he remains at his ships with Patroclus, playing his lyre and refusing to fight. He allows Patroclus to wear his armour and lead the Myrmidons, praying for his victory and safe return, but Patroclus is killed and Hector takes Achilles’ armour. Grief replaces his anger toward Agamemnon. Though he knows his own death will follow Hector’s, Achilles reconciles with Agamemnon, receives armour made by Vulcan, and returns to battle seeking revenge. He kills many Trojans, drives them toward Ilius, and finally kills Hector in single combat. His grief and vengeance continue after the killing: he dishonours Hector’s corpse, drags it behind his chariot, and honors Patroclus with mourning, sacrifice, burial, and funeral games. At Jove’s command, conveyed through Thetis, he accepts Priam’s ransom, cleans and returns Hector’s body, comforts Priam, and restrains war for Hector’s funeral rites. The record ends with Achilles still mourning Patroclus while yielding Hector to his father.

Achilles in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Achilles is a hero and Greek warrior, son of Peleus and Thetis, earlier named Ligyron and raised by Chiron on the inwards of lions and wild swine and the marrow of bears. At nine, he was declared necessary for Troy’s capture. To conceal him, Thetis disguised him as a maiden and entrusted him to Lycomedes; there he had a child with Deidamia. Once discovered, he traveled to Troy with Phoenix and Patroclus, becoming admiral and leader of the Myrmidons’ fifty ships. He was linked to Iphigenia as her supposed bridegroom, killed Tenes despite Thetis’s warning, and, after Protesilaus died, landed with the Myrmidons. He killed Cycnus and Troilus, captured Lycaon, seized cities including Lesbos and Lyrnessus, and fought around Troy for years. Anger over Briseis led him to refuse battle until Patroclus fought and was killed. Achilles then received armor from Hephaestus, returned to war, killed many Trojans including Hector, Penthesilia, and Memnon, and dragged Hector’s body to the ships. He was killed at the Scaean gate when Alexander and Apollo shot him in the ankle. Buried with Patroclus in the White Isle, with their bones mixed, he was later said to consort with Medea in the Isles of the Blest. His grave became the place of Polyxena’s sacrifice, and his arms were awarded to Ulysses after his death.

Achilles is the son of Peleus and Thetis, foretold to surpass his father in power and valor. A fierce Greek warrior and bulwark in the Trojan War, he is vulnerable only at his heel. Achilles is a Greek, Achaean, and Danaan warrior-hero: Peleus' son, Thetis' son, Aeacus' son, a king among the Myrmidons, and the foremost champion feared by the Trojans. He possesses divinely fashioned armour, a long keen sword, and a spear wrought by Chiron. His defining importance is his overwhelming power in battle and the grief, honor, and continuing influence that follow his death. Before the recorded events, he has killed Hector, Troilus, Cycnus, Mynes, and others and spoiled Lyrnessus and Thebes. He joins Aias against the Trojans and Amazons, kills Penthesileia, then sorrowfully loves her in death; after Thersites mocks this pity, Achilles kills him and later reconciles with Diomedes. When Memnon kills his dear friend Antilochus, Achilles avenges Antilochus by killing Memnon. After Antilochus' burial he again devastates the Trojan army, but Apollo strikes his ankle with a concealed shaft. Achilles fights on, collapses, and dies; Aias defends his body. The Achaeans burn him with offerings, preserve his bones, raise a barrow, mourn him, and hold funeral games. He is mourned by the Myrmidons, Argives, Aias, Phoenix, Agamemnon, and Briseis, who loves him as her defence. His arms become the subject of a dispute, and his death leads the Achaeans to seek his son Neoptolemus. Poseidon declares that he will become a god on an island in the Euxine Sea. Achilles in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Achilles is a renowned hero, the most illustrious Greek warrior, and son of Thetis and Peleus. Thetis faithfully protects his interests: she hides him among Lycomedes’ daughters until Ulysses discovers him through weapons, leading Achilles to join the Greek expedition; she later obtains armor from Vulcan for him. Dipped in the Styx, he is invulnerable except at his heel. In the Trojan War, he possesses Briseis, but withdraws when Agamemnon takes her and refuses the Greeks’ embassy. He lends his armor to his friend Patroclus, who leads the Myrmidons and dies; overwhelmed by remorse, Achilles reconciles with Agamemnon, returns as an irresistible champion, defeats and abuses Hector’s body, then gives it back to Priam. He also kills Penthesilea, regrets it, kills Thersites, and later kills Memnon after a long doubtful contest. He loves Polyxena after seeing her at Troy. Paris kills him with a poisoned arrow in his heel; afterward his ghost demands Polyxena’s sacrifice.
