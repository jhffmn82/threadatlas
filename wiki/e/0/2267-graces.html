Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, the Graces are three goddesses of grace and beauty who give pleasant things, wisdom, beauty, and fame, assist victory song, and preside over divine feasts and dances. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Graces are a deity worshipped under differing numbers and names, with traditions concerning their parentage and names.

Graces in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · 497 BC · the work’s page · read it where Graces first appears

Abstract written by a model

Graces are three goddesses of grace or beauty: Aglaia, Euphrosyne, and Thalia. Renowned as queens of Orchomenos and guardians of the Minyai’s ancient race, they give men pleasant things, wisdom, beauty, and fame. They are required by the gods in ruling feasts and dances beside Pythian Apollo, assist victory song, and provide delicious dew for the Psalychidai’s song and fame. Their beauty can make an untrue tale believable.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

notes

The Charites or Graces are goddesses of Grace or Beauty, often represented as three. Pindar uses them to explain why people may believe an untrue tale because of the beauty of its presentation.

XIV.

The Graces are invoked as the renowned queens of Orchomenos and guardians of the Minyai's ancient race. They are said to give men pleasant things, wisdom, beauty, and fame, and to share authority over heavenly feasts and dances beside Pythian Apollo.

Graces in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Graces first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Graces are a deity worshipped under differing numbers and names. Eteocles and later custom established three; Lacedæmonians had two. Traditions made them daughters of Zeus and Eurynome, or Ægle and Sun, and named them Charis, Pasithea, Euphrosyne, Aglaia, and Thalia.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXV.

The Graces were worshipped in differing numbers and under differing names: three according to Eteocles and later custom, two according to the Lacedæmonians, and with various names in Athenian and poetic traditions. Homer called one Charis and another Pasithea, while Hesiod named three as Euphrosyne, Aglaia, and Thalia.
