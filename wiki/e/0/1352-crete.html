Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Crete is settled by Pelasgi, Achaeans, and Dorians, the Three-fold People, who divide the land into three parts. In The Odyssey, Crete contains Cnossus and is presented as the birthplace of the disguised stranger and the home of his father Castor son of Hylax; the stranger claims to have commanded Cretan men and ships. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Crete is Ergoteles's homeland through his origin at Knosos, and the Cretans are said to have kept aloof from the Panhellenic games. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Crete is the home of Phædra before her marriage to Theseus, and she says she will not disgrace the house of Crete by acting basely for the sake of her life. In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Crete is the ancestral homeland and origin of Phaedra, who recalls it and refuses to shame the old proud Cretan castle; the Chorus refers to its strand and her voyage from home. In The Argonautica, Crete is an island rising above other islands, with Dicte’s haven and Argo’s harbour; guarded by Talos, it receives the Argonauts after his fall, and they build Athena’s shrine before sailing on. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Crete is an island and convenient port whose peoples, rulers, myths, religious rites, and mysteries are described, including the reigns of Ammon and Minos and the stories of Theseus, Ariadne, Daedalus, and Icarus. In The Aeneid, Crete is mistakenly identified by Anchises as Aeneas’s destined homeland; Aeneas establishes Pergamus there, but plague and crop failure force the Trojans to leave when the household gods reveal it is not their destined abode.

In Heroides and Amores, Crete is a land of a hundred cities known for nurturing the infant Jupiter or Jove; Ariadne cannot return after betraying her father and homeland, and Crete later alone remains fertile when Ceres abandons other fields. In The Amores, or Amours, Crete alone remains fertile when Ceres lingers in the woods with Iasius. In The Library, Crete is ruled by Idomeneus until Leucus seduces ten of its cities from their allegiance, makes himself lord of the island, and expels Idomeneus. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Crete is an isle, also called Gortynian, to which Jupiter carries Europa; Teutamus and some Pelasgians settle there, it becomes the kingdom of Jupiter Asterius, and Minos reigns over it and prepares war against Athens. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Crete is the birthplace of Jupiter, the realm ruled by Minos, and the setting of his palace, the Labyrinth, Phæstus, and the Phæstian land; it also contains the Gnossian kingdom and produces Iphis’s transformation. In The Library, Crete is the place to which Zeus, in the form of a tame bull, carries Europa; Zeus fathers Minos, Sarpedon, and Rhadamanthys with her there, and Minos rules and passes laws over it. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Crete is marked by civil war during Philopœmen’s service, competing traditions about Cydon, Catreus, and Gortys, and associations with Dædalus at Minos’ court and the work of Chirisophus. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Crete is Minos’s kingdom and Jove’s cradle; it is initially named mother of Trojans by an oracle, receives Athenian tribute, and is protected by Minos from Scylla.

Crete in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Crete first appears

Abstract written by a model

Crete is an island, described as a convenient port for expeditions throughout the world. Its earliest inhabitants are said to have been the Eteocretæ or Eteocretans; later peoples included the Idæi Dactyli, Curetes, Titans, Pelasgi, Dorienses, and others. Ammon, driven from his kingdom, fled there, married Geta, daughter of the ruling Curetes, gained sovereignty, and was later supported in a victorious war against the Titans. Minos subsequently ruled with a powerful navy and dominion of the sea; with Rhadamanthus he united its peoples. Under Minos, Athenians brought tribute, Theseus killed the Minotaur and fled with Ariadne, and Daedalus found refuge before escaping with Icarus. Crete is also linked to the Curetes or Corybantes, Jupiter’s concealment, discoveries, gods, religious rites, and mysteries.

CHAP. V.

Crete was ruled by Minos and was the place where Daedalus found refuge and favor after fleeing Athens. Daedalus and Icarus escaped from Crete, and the island was also associated with the worship of the Curetes or Corybantes and the concealment of Jupiter from Saturn.

Crete in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Crete first appears

Abstract written by a model

Crete is a place marked by civil war during Philopœmen’s service, competing traditions about Cydon, Catreus, and Gortys, and associations with Dædalus at Minos’ court and the work of Chirisophus. Tegeans describe migrants naming Cydonia and Gortys; Cretans give different parentage.

CHAPTER LIII.

The people of Tegea say that Cydon, Archedius, and Gortys migrated to Crete and gave their names to Cydonia and Gortys, as well as Catreus. The Cretans reject this account, instead giving Cydon as the son of Acacallis and Hermes, Catreus as the son of Minos, and Gortys as the son of Rhadamanthus; Crete is also associated with the stay of Dædalus at Minos’ court and with the work of Chirisophus.
