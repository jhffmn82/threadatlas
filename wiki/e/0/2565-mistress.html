Abstract written by a model

In Heroides and Amores, the beloved mistress is the woman whose resistance to the poet is softened by his song and poetry, leading her to grant him her beauty, favor, and return. In Heroides and Amores, the beloved girl is the speaker’s mistress who secretly kisses a rival before reconciling with the speaker through exceptionally pleasing kisses. In Heroides and Amores, the mistress is the lover’s beloved inside a guarded house; later, she is frightened and wounded by his violence but silently accuses him through her tears and expression. In The Amores, or Amours, the mistress is the speaker’s beautiful but demanding and unfaithful beloved, whose relationship with him is marked by desire, gifts, broken vows, and separation.

mistress in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where mistress first appears

Abstract written by a model

The mistress is a woman whose relationship with the speaker is shaped by desire, beauty, money, and broken vows. She once pleased him by being disinterested, but now requires gifts as a return for love and freely asks payment for sexual favors, which he says has blemished her disposition. Habitually treating and dyeing her hair despite his warnings, she nearly loses it, grieves over her former locks, and may regain natural hair. Though far away after promising everlasting companionship, she must prove her promises by coming to him. She repeatedly swears falsely to the Gods and the speaker and laughs unpunished, yet remains beautiful: tall, graceful, fair and rosy, with long hair, tiny feet, and star-like expressive eyes. The records end with her separated from him, lying alone on a solitary couch during the rites of Ceres.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

ELEGY X.

The mistress once pleased the speaker because she was disinterested, but her demand for presents has blemished her disposition in his estimation. He asks her to stop demanding payment and says that he will then give her what he refuses while she asks for it.

ELEGY XIV.

The mistress repeatedly treated her hair despite the speaker's warnings to leave it alone. After nearly losing it, she becomes distressed and ashamed, looks at her former locks placed in her bosom, and is told that her natural hair may eventually return.

ELEGY XVI.

The mistress is far away from the speaker, although she had sworn to be his companion. The speaker says that her promises are empty unless she now proves them by coming to him in her little chaise. He asks the hills and winding paths to become smooth wherever she travels.

ELEGY III.

The mistress has sworn falsely to the Gods and to the speaker, but her flowing hair, complexion, stature, feet, and expressive eyes remain as beautiful as before. She laughs unpunished at the Gods and at the speaker, who asks her to show more regard for his eyes.

ELEGY X.

The mistress is separated from the speaker during the yearly season of the rites of Ceres. She lies apart on a solitary couch.
