  • In Iphigenia in Tauris, Clytemnestra is Agamemnon’s wife and the mother of Iphigenia and Orestes; she murders Agamemnon and is later killed by Orestes.
  • In The Electra of Euripides, Clytemnestra is queen, Agamemnon’s widow, and the mother of Electra, Orestes, and Iphigenia; she helps Aegisthus murder Agamemnon and is killed by Orestes with Electra’s assistance.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Clytemnestra is Agamemnon’s wife and the mother of Iphigenia, Orestes, and Electra; she protests Iphigenia’s sacrifice, kills Agamemnon, and is killed by Orestes.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Clytemnestra rules with Aegisthus, helps murder Agamemnon, fears Orestes’ return, and is killed by him after believing a false report of his death.
  • In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Clytemnestra rules Argos, kills Agamemnon with Aegisthus, and is later killed by Orestes, becoming a ghost seeking vengeance.
  • In The Odyssey, Clytemnestra helps murder Agamemnon and kills Cassandra beside him, for which Agamemnon condemns her as cruel and shameless.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Clytaemnestra deserts Agamemnon for Aegisthus and murders him with Aegisthus.
  • In The Library, Clytaemnestra becomes Agamemnon’s wife, sends Iphigenia to Aulis under a false pretext, helps kill Agamemnon and Cassandra, and is eventually killed by Orestes.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Clytemnestra is Agamemnon’s unfaithful wife who plots with Aegisthus to murder him and is later killed by Orestes.

Clytemnestra in The Electra of Euripides

Clytemnestra is queen of Argos and Mycenae, first Agamemnon’s wife and later his widow, and mother of Electra, Orestes, and Iphigenia. She is also Castor’s and Polydeuces’ twin sister and becomes Aegisthus’ wife. With Aegisthus, she murders Agamemnon. She defends the killing by citing Agamemnon’s sacrifice of Iphigenia, his bringing another woman into their house, and his exclusion of her from his love. Clytemnestra arrives to perform rites, asks for mercy by invoking her motherhood, and is killed by Orestes with Electra’s assistance in revenge for Agamemnon’s murder.

Clytemnestra in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Clytemnestra is a woman defined by her family ties and by a cycle of loss, murder, and retaliation. Daughter of Leda and Tyndarus, she was married to Tantalus before becoming Agamemnon’s wife. She is mother of Iphigenia, Orestes, and Electra. When Agamemnon summons Iphigenia to Aulis under the pretense of a marriage to Achilles, Clytemnestra arrives with Iphigenia and Orestes, carrying wedding gifts. On learning that Iphigenia is intended for sacrifice, she confronts Agamemnon, reproaches him for sacrificing their daughter for Helen and the Greek cause, and appeals to Achilles for help. She grieves when Iphigenia faces death, though a Messenger and Agamemnon later report that the Gods spared her and send Clytemnestra home with Orestes. Later, Clytemnestra kills Agamemnon in his bath after entangling or covering him in an inextricable robe. She secretly takes Ægisthus as her husband. Her killing of Agamemnon becomes the central wrong answered by Orestes, who kills his mother in retaliation and at Apollo’s command. Her death is disputed: she warns Orestes against the act, Tyndarus condemns both killings, and an assembly debates whether Orestes was justified. Helen sends offerings to her tomb.

Clytemnestra is queen of Argos, wife of the absent Agamemnon, and mother of Orestes, Electra, and Iphigenia. While Agamemnon is away for ten years, she rules Argos, kindles altars across the city, and learns of Troy’s capture through a chain of beacon fires. She publicly celebrates, warns that the victors must respect Troy’s gods, and presents herself as a faithful wife who has preserved the household despite loneliness, fear, rumors, and sleeplessness. When Agamemnon returns, she welcomes him with elaborate praise and persuades him to enter on purple robes. Her welcome conceals a plotted killing shared with Aegisthus, her lover, political ally, and later spouse. She also orders Cassandra into the palace. Clytemnestra then admits that she deceived Agamemnon, entangled him in a robe or covering net, struck him three times, and killed him. She calls the murder requital for Iphigenia and for wrongs against her household, exults in it, and intends to rule with Aegisthus, while restraining further immediate bloodshed between him and the Chorus. Her rule is shadowed by accusation, curse, and fear. Electra condemns her as lacking a mother’s heart, and Clytemnestra sends libations to Agamemnon’s grave after a prophetic dream in which she bears a serpent that draws blood from her breast. She is identified as having killed Agamemnon and sought Orestes’ destruction. When Orestes returns disguised and false news reports his death, she is devastated, recognizes the house’s curse returning, appeals to him as the son she nursed, and is killed by him after Aegisthus. After death, she becomes the ghost of the slain mother, seeking vengeance for her own killing. She urges the Furies to pursue Orestes, displays her wounds, and invokes the offerings she gave them. Her murder of Agamemnon and Orestes’ retaliatory killing of her remain the central bloodstain requiring judgment and atonement.
