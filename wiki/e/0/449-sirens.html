Sirens

group · 5 works · 25 quoted facts · 6 connections
Also called: Sea-nymphs, Sirens three, The Sirens, clear-voiced Sirens, daughters of Acheloüs, maidens’ voice, the Sirens, the two Sirens, Σειρήνων, Σειρῆνες

Abstract written by a model

In The Odyssey, the Sirens are dangerous enchantresses whose enchanting, prophetic song lures sailors to their deaths, while Odysseus's crew avoids them by stopping their ears with wax. In The Argonautica, Sirens are bird- and maiden-shaped daughters of Achelous through Terpsichore who beguile and destroy sailors with sweet song until Orpheus overcomes it and Cypris rescues Butes. In The Library, Sirens are bird-shaped women, daughters of Achelous and Melpomene, who use lyre, song, and flute to persuade passing mariners to linger and die when a ship passes them. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Sirens are daughters of Acheloüs and one of the Muses who are transformed into birds while searching for Proserpine, retaining maiden faces and human voices. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Sirens are sea-nymphs whose song charms mariners to destruction, and Circe advises Ulysses to stop his men's ears with wax and have himself bound to the mast.

the Sirens in The Odyssey

Homer · about 632 to 599 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Sirens first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sirens are dangerous enchantresses on their island, sitting in a green field of flowers and singing an enchanting, prophetic song to passing sailors. Their song promises wisdom about Troy and the world, but those who approach are enchanted, never return to their families, and die among rotting bones. Odysseus's crew avoids them by stopping their ears with wax.

Sirens in The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Sirens first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sirens are bird-shaped women from the thighs downward, daughters of Achelous and Melpomene, comprising Pisinoe, Aglaope, and Thelxiepia. Through lyre, song, and flute, they persuade passing mariners to linger; according to prophecy, they die when a ship passes them.

The Sirens are Pisinoe, Aglaope, and Thelxiepia, daughters of Achelous and Melpomene. One plays the lyre, one sings, and one plays the flute; they persuade passing sailors to linger. They have bird forms from the thighs and die after a ship passes them.

Sirens in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Sirens first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sirens are daughters of Acheloüs and one of the Muses. Seeking Proserpine throughout the world, they ask the Gods to transform them into birds; afterward they have feathers and bird feet while retaining maiden faces and human voices.
