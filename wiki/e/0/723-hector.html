  • In The Amores, or Amours, Hector goes to battle immediately after embracing his wife Andromache.
  • In Heroides and Amores, Hector is praised as Paris’s brother, Andromache’s husband, a warrior, and Trojan strength, then lamented as a hero dragged by horses.
  • In The Aeneid, Hector is Troy’s dead defender who appears as a bloody ghost to Aeneas, warns of Troy’s fall, and gives him the Trojan gods.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Hector is identified as Priam and Hecuba’s especially valiant son in the Trojan War.
  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Hector is remembered as Troy’s mighty defender, and his battle-worn shield becomes Astyanax’s burial bier after Hector’s death.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Hector commands Troy’s army and defenses, directs the night watch, receives Rhesus, orders care for the wounded and burial of the dead, and is killed by Achilles.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Hector fights successfully with his spear until his death accompanies Troy’s destruction.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Hector exchanges gifts with Ajax; his girdle is used to drag him to death, while his sword is used by Ajax to kill himself.
  • In The Rhesus of Euripides, Hector commands the Trojan forces, sends Dolon as a spy, receives Rhesus, and responds angrily when Rhesus is killed through a security failure.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Hector kills Protesilaus when the Greeks attempt to land at Ilium.
  • In The Iliad, Hector is Troy’s foremost warrior, commander, guardian, and defender; he kills Patroclus, faces Achilles, and is killed and mourned after his body is ransomed and buried.
  • In The Library, Hector kills Protesilaus, fights Ajax, breaches the Greek defenses, burns ships, kills Patroclus, and is slain by Achilles before Priam ransoms his body.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Hector defends Troy, kills Protesilaüs, attacks the Greek fleet, fights Ajax, dies facing Achilles, and has his body dragged around Troy before his ashes are honored.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Hector fights for Chios, sacrifices to the Pan-Ionic league, receives a tripod for his prowess, and is honored by a tomb and painting.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Hector is Troy’s former champion and defender, is defeated and killed by Achilles, and his death brings fear and grief to Troy.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Hector supports Troy’s throne, resists the Greeks, helps kill Patroclus, and is killed by Achilles before receiving a solemn funeral.

Hector in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Hector is a mortal Trojan prince, son of Priam and Hecuba, husband of Andromache, father of Astyanax, brother of Paris and other Trojan kin, and Troy’s foremost warrior, commander, guardian, and chief defender. He is valued for courage, counsel, devotion to his people, and determination to protect Ilius, though he knows Troy is fated to fall. He rebukes Paris, organizes the city’s defense, urges prayers and offerings, visits Andromache and their child, and returns to battle rather than avoid his duty. Throughout the war he leads Trojan and allied forces at the front, rallies councils and troops, challenges Ajax to single combat, breaches the Achaean wall, drives toward the ships, and threatens them with fire. Supported at times by Jove and Apollo, he withstands wounds and repeatedly renews the assault. He orders Dolon to spy on the Achaeans, rejects an omen in favor of trusting Jove, and at key moments accepts or refuses Polydamas’s advice. His greatest battlefield success is killing Patroclus with Apollo’s aid, stripping Patroclus of Achilles’ armour, and wearing it in triumph; this makes him Achilles’ central enemy. Apollo warns Hector not to face Achilles alone and at times saves him, but after Patroclus’s death the conflict closes around them. Hector remains outside the gates despite his parents’ pleas, runs three times around the city, and finally faces Achilles after Minerva deceives him in the likeness of Deiphobus. Achilles wounds him in the throat and kills him. Hector asks for his body to be returned, but it is stripped, dragged behind Achilles’ chariot, and dishonoured. Venus and Apollo preserve it from dogs, sun, and decay. Priam ransoms the body; Troy mourns its glory and protector, burns it, buries the bones, and raises a barrow.
