Homeric Hymns

Abstract written by a model

The Homeric Hymns are a traditionally Homer-ascribed collection of thirty-three poems, regarded as the Epic School’s last considerable work and generally later than the Cyclic poems. Influenced in diction and style by Homer and Hesiod, they move through longer narrative hymns concerning Demeter, Apollo, Hermes, Aphrodite, Dionysus, and Pan, alongside shorter hymns. Their chief importance is tempered by a distinctly bad text, with defects, conjectural problems, and many lacunae; no papyrus fragment had emerged. Hugh G. Evelyn-White used Allen and Sikes and T.W. Allen’s articles as chief sources.

PREFACE

In preparing the text of the Homeric Hymns, Hugh G. Evelyn-White's chief source was the edition of Allen and Sikes from 1904, supplemented by T.W. Allen's articles in the Journal of Hellenic Studies.

The Ionic School

The Homeric Hymns are a collection of thirty-three poems regarded as the last considerable work of the Epic School and generally later than the Cyclic poems. They combine Homeric and Hesiodic influences and include longer narrative works about Demeter, Apollo, Hermes, Aphrodite, Dionysus, and Pan, as well as shorter hymns.

BIBLIOGRAPHY

The Homeric Hymns are described as having a distinctly bad textual condition, with many defects, conjectural problems, and numerous lacunae. No papyrus fragment of the Hymns had emerged, although a papyrus contained a paraphrase closely parallel to the Hymn to Demeter.
