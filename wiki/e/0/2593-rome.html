Rome in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Rome is a future city and imperial state of the Romans, destined through the Trojan people and Aeneas’s lineage, with Romulus as its founder. Its imperial name, fame, and Ausonian scepter depend on Aeneas fulfilling that destiny and on Ascanius’s line. From humble places beside the Tiber, including the future site of its hall, it rises into a city whose towers, conquests, and shadow extend over earth and ocean. It is to rule the world and the Roman empire through conquest, peace, war, and laws, while retaining immortal majesty and glory. Its foretold history includes founding legends, conflict with the Sabines, defenses against Porsena and the Gauls, triumph at Actium, and domination over many nations.

Rome is presented as the future city founded by Romulus, descendant of the Trojan line. Jupiter says that Rome will rule the world, impose laws, and receive lasting glory.

Rome in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Rome is a city containing the Senate of Rome and portrayed as a center of government, warfare, diplomacy, public judgment, and civic change. It is said to have been built in the second year of the seventh Olympiad. Early in the record, a sedition over consuls versus military tribunes produces anarchy until six military tribunes are chosen; Marcus Petilius is later named among its consuls. Rome creates two censors, receives the Appia water supply, and begins Appius Claudius's road to Capua. Its Senate receives appeals, ambassadors, and embassies from many peoples and rulers, investigates Pleminius, orders redress for the Locrians, declares them free, negotiates settlements, imposes conditions, and declares war against Perseus. Rome also receives leaders, grain, honored dead, and public mourners, while captured rebels are brought there for condemnation. Its military position is repeatedly tested: an army's defeat causes distress and loss of confidence; Cimbrian successes and slave rebellions strain its manpower; and authorities direct campaigns under Lucullus, Servilius, and Aquilius. Threats then come closer through Pompoedius, slave conspiracies, a revolt by one hundred and fifty slaves, and later civil violence, proscriptions, killings, and calamities involving Marius and Sylla. Alongside its power, the record praises Senate-led generosity and public service, but also says luxury and extravagance came to outweigh military service and sober conduct. After following humane generals, Rome is called mistress of the world.

Rome in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Rome is a noble city and the setting of recorded memorable actions. It was marked by commotion during conflict over magistracy and law, including a struggle between the Decemviri and soldiers, who seized the Aventine mount before political concessions restored order. It had four military tribunes exercising consular authority. After ambassadors were killed, Rome declared war on the Fidenates and fought indecisively. Following defeat at the Allia, it was weakly defended, and the Gauls entered and destroyed nearly the whole city while survivors held provisions and valuables in the castle and Capitol.

Rome provided the author with many materials for his undertaking. Because he had lived there for a long time, he collected from Roman ancient records accounts of the memorable actions of the Roman empire.

Rome was the scene of the conflict between the Decemviri and the soldiers who opposed the abuse of power. The soldiers entered Rome and seized the Aventine mount, after which the dispute was settled by political concessions and the city returned to order.

Rome declared war on the Fidenates after they killed Roman ambassadors. It appointed Manius Aemilius dictator, with Aulus Cornelius as master of the horse, and fought the Fidenates to an indecisive engagement.

CHAP. XIII.

Rome was left weakly defended after the Roman defeat at the Allia. The Gauls entered and destroyed nearly the entire city, while the surviving Romans concentrated provisions and valuables in the castle and Capitol.

Rome in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Rome is a city of Romulus, founded when its walls are established at the festival of Pales. Rising beside the Tiber in Latian air, it begins with gates opened to the Sabines through Juno’s intervention, then sealed by a heated fountain near Janus until the Romans arm themselves. Its traditions include Numa’s laws, the Arician grove, Etrurian divination, and Romulus’s spear-tree as a sign of greatness. Cippus remains outside Rome rather than accept kingship and make its people slaves. A dire pestilence kills many until Æsculapius arrives at the Island of the Tiber and restores the city’s health. Rome is prophesied to change, become mistress of the boundless earth, extend power through conquered earth, and honor Julius Cæsar as a deity.

EXPLANATION.

Rome was associated with the traditions of Numa’s laws, the Arician grove, and the divination practices transmitted from the Etrurians. The miraculous spear-tree of Romulus was regarded as a sign of Rome’s greatness, though the explanation considers it unlikely that a single cornel tree survived in the crowded city for nearly seven hundred years without being renewed.

Rome suffers a dire pestilence that kills many people and resists both ordinary efforts and physicians’ skill. After Æsculapius arrives at the Island of the Tiber, the mourning ends and the city is restored to health.

Rome is the city in which Julius Cæsar is made a deity and from which his worship is viewed. The Roman power is also described as extending throughout the conquered earth, where the Poet expects his work to be read.

Rome in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Rome begins as land near the Tiber, inhabited by Evander and Arcadians who accompanied him, later becoming part of Rome. It becomes a center of Roman authority: it allies with the Athenians, sends forces against Philip, and receives Achæans ordered there for trial, many of whom are treated as condemned and imprisoned in Etrurian towns. Appeals, embassies, disputes, fines, and arbitration involving the Oropians, Ætolians, Lacedæmonians, Achæans, and Athenians are brought before Rome and its Senate. Rome then sends envoys, Mummius, and advisers to settle disputes and direct war against the Achæans; it receives Corinthian offerings, restores institutions, remits fines, and governs Greece through a prætor. Finally, wars involving Rome include Antonine's victories over Germans and Sauromatae.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

Rome formed an alliance with the Athenians because their other allies were weak. It intervened militarily against Philip by sending forces to assist or protect the Ætolians and Athenians.

CHAPTER X.

A Roman Senator ordered Achæans accused by Callicrates to stand trial at Rome. More than 1,000 went there, although the Romans treated them as already condemned and imprisoned them in towns in Etruria.

CHAPTER XI.

Rome was the place where the Oropians appealed to the Senate and where the Ætolians who inhabited Pleuron were allowed to send a private embassy. The Romans and the Senate made decisions concerning the Achæan League and the fine imposed on the Athenians.

CHAPTER XII.

Rome was the destination of embassies from the Lacedæmonians and the Achæans concerning the dispute. The Roman Senate was said to have directed the Lacedæmonians to try all but capital cases before the Achæan League, and later announced that ambassadors would arbitrate the dispute.

CHAPTER XIV.

Rome sent envoys to Greece to settle the disputes between the Lacedæmonians and the Achæans. The Achæans also sent Thearidas and other prominent men as ambassadors to Rome, though they turned back after meeting other envoys on the road.

CHAPTER XVI.

Rome sent Mummius and advisers to settle disputes and direct the war against the Achæans. It received the most valuable Corinthian offerings, later showed mercy to Greece by restoring institutions and remitting fines, and continued to send a prætor to govern the region.

CHAPTER XLIII.

Part of what later became Rome was inhabited by Evander and the Arcadians who accompanied him. The son Antonine fought against the Germans and subdued the Sauromatae in wars involving Rome.

Rome in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Rome rises from the Trojan state and is compared, in its fallen condition, to Niobe. At the Tiber, its future Forum, Capitol, and other landmarks are first a wooded rural site seen by Aeneas and Evander. Romulus and Remus found it beside the river after revenge on Amulius. It grows into a proud city and power sovereign of the world, with temples of Janus and public safety tied to Vesta’s sacred fire. After the conquest of Greece, it becomes a chief center for collecting and producing Greek sculpture.
