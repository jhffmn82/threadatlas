  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Venus is the goddess of love who punishes Hippolytus's rejection by causing Phædra's passion and bringing about his death.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Aphrodite is the goddess of desire and mother and queen of the Cadmean race who defends her prerogative and intervenes in the Danaids' acquittal.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Aphrodite is the foam-born goddess of love whose desires, favors, and anger direct gods and mortals.
  • In The Iliad, Venus is the goddess who protects Alexandrus and Aeneas, aids the Trojans, lends Juno her girdle, and is wounded while intervening in battle.
  • In The Library, Aphrodite is the goddess who punishes Smyrna, loves Anchises, bears Aeneas and Lyrus, rewards Paris, rescues him, and is wounded while aiding Aeneas.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Venus is the goddess of love who defends her worship, aids lovers, mourns Adonis, supports the Trojans, and protects Rome and Julius Cæsar.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Venus is the sea-associated goddess, wife of Vulcan and mother of Cupid, who seeks revenge, pities transformed mortals, and extends her influence through Cupid.
  • In The Library, Aphrodite is the daughter of Zeus and Dione who punishes Clio, makes Dawn perpetually love, and gives Melanion the golden apples used against Atalanta.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Aphrodite is worshipped in many temples and images and acts as a deity who grants aid, matches, inventions, and forgetfulness.
  • In Description of Greece, Aphrodite is a deity honored in varied local temples and forms who encourages Jason's wooing of Medea and represents the bonds of love.
  • In Description of Greece, Celestial Aphrodite is an ancient celestial goddess worshipped at Cythera's holy temple, where wooden and stone statues represent her.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Aphrodite is the goddess and protector of Aeneas who mourns Paris, rescues her son, safeguards his family, and preserves Helen from Menelaus's vengeance.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Venus is the goddess of love and beauty who inspires unions, punishes neglect, and intervenes in the fortunes of lovers and heroes.

Venus in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

Venus is a goddess of love, an unwarlike deity associated with Love and mother of Cupid, Æneas, and the tender Loves. She is united to Vulcan, gives kisses to Mars, may be swarthy yet pleasing, and commands the South winds. Her favor can aid a damsel, though she gives deceived lovers a deaf hearing; she is uncertain, since defeat and reversal occur in love as in war. She deeply mourns Tibullus, turning from his funeral pile and, according to some reports, weeping. By the end, she is addressed as the mother of the tender Loves and asked to seek a new Poet as the speaker ends his Elegies.

ELEGY VII.

Dipsas says that Venus now favors the damsel because Mars has gone, and that Venus holds sway in the City of Æneas. She also claims that Venus gives a deaf hearing to lovers who have been deceived.

ELEGY IX.

Venus is described as uncertain, like Mars, because in both love and war the defeated may rise again and those thought incapable of falling may be overthrown.

ELEGY IX.

Venus grieves Tibullus's death almost as deeply as she grieved when the boar tore the youth's groin. She turns away from the funeral pyre, and some say that she sheds tears.

ELEGY X.

Venus is addressed as the mother of the tender Loves. The poet tells her to seek a new Poet because he is ending his Elegies.

Venus in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Venus is a sea-born goddess of love and beauty, mother of Aeneas and Cupid, and beloved of Mars; she is also involved with Mars in an adulterous affair. She wins Paris's judgment among three goddesses through her beauty and her promise that he will possess Helen, later sponsoring his marriage to her on a painted ship. Helen both doubts Venus's praise and is pleased by it. Venus rules in the city of Aeneas, favors a young woman after Mars departs, and is invoked by Dipsas to excuse deception and false oaths in love. Though linked to love's camp, she and Cupid are described as unwarlike and unsuited to soldiers' pay. Speakers invoke her to grant or permit love, distinguish her powers from Minerva's, and favor poetic love; in a procession she nods and gives a favorable sign for a new mistress to endure being loved.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

XVI

Venus promises Paris that he will possess Helen, whom she describes as more beautiful than her mother. She wins Paris's judgment by offering him love and later appears as the sponsor of his marriage to Helen on the painted ship.

XVII

Venus is said to have promised Paris that he would become the husband of Tyndareus's daughter. Helen doubts that Venus's praise of her beauty should be trusted and says that such praise might make others envious, though she admits that she is pleased by it.

P. OVIDI NASONIS AMORES / LIBER PRIMUS

Venus is invoked as the deity who may hear the speaker's prayers that the beloved woman either love him or at least allow herself to be loved. She is also contrasted with Minerva in the speaker's argument that gods should not exchange their characteristic powers.

VIII

Venus is said to rule in the city of Aeneas and to favor the young woman after Mars has departed. Dipsas invokes Venus to justify deception in love, claiming that Venus turns a deaf ear to false oaths and permits such tricks in play.

IX

Venus is associated with the age suited to warfare and with the camp of love. Neither Venus nor her son is suited to earning the pay of soldiers, because they are unwarlike gods.

LIBER TERTIUS

Venus is invoked during the procession of the gods as the goddess whom the poet and Cupid's powerful children applaud. The poet asks her to favor his efforts and to give his new mistress the disposition to endure being loved. Venus nods and gives a favorable sign.

Venus in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Venus is a goddess, Queen of Love, wife of Vulcan, associated with Paphos and Cythera, and above all the divine mother, protector, and advocate of Aeneas and the Trojan line. When Aeneas and the Trojans suffer and cannot reach Italy, she appeals to Jupiter, who confirms their fated future. She repeatedly guides and shields Aeneas: disguised as a huntress in Libya, she tells him of Dido and Carthage, directs him toward the city, and conceals him and Achates in cloud. To protect him further, she sends Cupid in Ascanius’s form to make Dido love Aeneas, while placing Ascanius in sleep in her groves. During Troy’s destruction, she reveals the gods involved and orders Aeneas back to Anchises, Creusa, and Ascanius. She remains alert to threats that could turn the Trojans from Italy, seeks Neptune’s protection for their voyage, and asks Vulcan to forge Aeneas’s armor, which she delivers with instructions to face the Latian enemy and Turnus. During the later fighting she appeals to Jupiter for the Trojans and Ascanius, and finally heals Aeneas’s wound with dittany, ambrosial substances, and medicinal herbs, restoring his strength.

Hesiod · -0699~

Aphrodite is an immortal, foam-born goddess of love, born from Heaven’s severed members and sea foam, accompanied by Eros and Desire. Golden, beautiful, and crowned with gold and violets, she governs love and sexual desire among gods, mortals, birds, and creatures of land and sea; her portion includes whisperings, smiles, deceits, love, and graciousness. All are subject to her will except Athene, Artemis, and Hestia, whose hearts she cannot bend. Brought by the western wind to Cyprus, she is clothed and adorned there, welcomed by the gods, and becomes associated with Paphos, Salamis, and Cyprus as goddess and queen who gives kindly gifts. Her power can punish: angered when Tyndareus forgot her in sacrifice, she caused his daughters to desert husbands and cast them into evil report. Yet Zeus makes her subject to desire for the mortal Anchises. She joins with him, bears Aeneas, reveals her divinity, is shamed among the deathless gods, and instructs Anchises to conceal their union. She also shapes Alexandrus’s course by promising Helen, suggesting shipbuilding, bringing them together, and ordering Aeneas to sail with Alexandrus. Throughout, she is portrayed as radiant and smiling, but as a force whose desire, favor, and anger direct both gods and mortals.

She is called Celestial Aphrodite on a square statue and appears in images by Praxiteles, Canachus, Hermogenes of Cythera, and others: seated in gold and ivory, wooden at Argos, stone near the sea, armed on Acrocorinth and at Sparta, and fettered in Sparta’s upper shrine. Her places of worship span Corinth, Acrocorinth, Sicyon, Argos, Hermione, Trœzen, Sparta, Epidaurus Limera, and Cænepolis. Corinthian tradition says she received Acrocorinth from the Sun; Polynices and associated Argives dedicated her Argive image. Her cult includes restricted rites at Sicyon, marriage-related sacrifices by maidens and widows at Hermione, and temples named Peeping, Bridal, of the Height, Marine, and of the Harbour. She encourages Jason in wooing Medea, while her fetters signify bonds of love between men and women. The records close with her notable temple at Epidaurus Limera and another near the sea at Cænepolis.

CHAPTER VI.

Aphrodite had a sanctuary at Sicyon containing a gold-and-ivory seated image by Canachus. Only a maiden sacristan and a maiden bath-carrier performed rites inside, while others worshipped the goddess from the porch.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Aphrodite was honored at Trœzen in a temple called Peeping Aphrodite, from which Phædra was said to have watched Hippolytus while he trained. Theseus built a temple of Bridal Aphrodite when he married Helen, and temples of Aphrodite of the Height stood above the temple of Isis. Hermione contained temples of Marine Aphrodite and Aphrodite of the Harbour, as well as another Aphrodite temple where maidens and widows intending to marry were required to sacrifice.

CHAPTER XV.

Sparta had an old temple and wooden statue of Aphrodite in full armor, with an upper shrine to Aphrodite the Shapely. The goddess was seated with a veil and fetters on her feet, which Tyndareus was said to have added as symbols of the bonds of love between men and women. Another statue honored Youth-prolonging Aphrodite, and Bathycles’ throne represented Aphrodite near Amyclæan Apollo.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Aphrodite has a temple at Epidaurus Limera, where the temple is one of the town’s notable sights. Another temple of Aphrodite stands at Cænepolis near the sea.

Aphrodite in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Aphrodite is a deity worshipped through temples, sacred enclosures, and many statues across places including Patræ, Ægium, Golgi, Orchomenus, Psophis, near Tegea, and near Mount Eryx in Sicily. At Patræ she has harbour and seaside sanctuaries; its women are especially devoted to her, and her images include one fished up by fishermen and a wooden figure with stone fingers, toes, and head. Her sanctuary at Psophis is ruined, while the temple near Mount Eryx is described as ancient and wealthy. She can act directly: after Selemnus lost Argyra, she transformed him into a river and later gave him forgetfulness of Argyra. She obtains good matches for the daughters of Pandareus from Zeus and receives prayers from widows at Naupactus seeking a second husband. Called Inventive, she is associated with most inventions in word or deed. Her representations take varied forms and names: Celestial, Common, Pandemian, Heart-Turner, Black, and an untitled image; some are wood, some stone, marble, or mixed materials, and makers include Damophon, Euclides, and Praxiteles. Her temples can also preserve memory, as at Actium’s seafight.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXI.

Aphrodite has temples at Patræ, including a sacred enclosure near the harbour and a temple in the grove near the sea. One of her statues was fished up by fishermen, while another is made of wood except for its stone fingers, toes, and head. The women of Patræ are described as especially devoted to her.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Aphrodite transformed the love-stricken Selemnus into a river after he was deprived of Argyra. She eventually granted him forgetfulness of Argyra.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Psophis has a temple of Aphrodite Erycina that is now in ruins and was reportedly built by the sons of Psophis. The account connects this sanctuary with the ancient and wealthy temple of Aphrodite Erycina near Mount Eryx in Sicily.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Aphrodite has a statue in the temple made of wood except for her hands, head, and toes, which are made of stone. She is surnamed Inventive because, in the account given here, most inventions come from her in word or deed.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Aphrodite had three statues at her ruined temple. One was called the Celestial, another the Common, and the third had no title.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Thebans have three ancient wooden statues of Aphrodite, said to be votive offerings of Harmonia made from the wood of the gunwales of Cadmus’ ships. They call them the Celestial Aphrodite, the Pandemian, and the Heart-Turner, representing pure love, vulgar sensual love, and lawless passion respectively.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Praxiteles made a stone statue of Aphrodite and another of Phryne. Thespia also has a temple of Black Aphrodite.

Venus in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Venus, also called Aphrodite, is a goddess of love and beauty, said to be daughter of Jupiter and Dione in one account or to have sprung from sea foam. Wife of Vulcan and mother of Cupid and Aeneas, she possesses the love-inspiring Cestus and is associated with swans, doves, the rose, and myrtle. Worshipped at Cyprus and served by Hero, she inspires love, favors unions, and punishes cruelty or neglect toward lovers and herself. She brings Galatea to life for Pygmalion and blesses their marriage; loves Adonis, warns him, and transforms his blood into an anemone after his death. Jealous of Psyche's worship and beauty, she assigns harsh trials, but ultimately accepts Psyche's marriage to Cupid. She aids Hippomenes with golden apples, then causes him and Atalanta to offend Cybele when they fail to honor her. After winning the golden apple as fairest goddess, she promises Helen to Paris, protects him, persuades Helen to elope, favors the Trojans, and enlists Mars on their side. She also consoles Ariadne and hears Hero's prayer to calm the storm.

6 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter I

Venus, or Aphrodite, was the goddess of love and beauty and was said to be the daughter of Jupiter and Dione or to have sprung from sea foam. She became the wife of Vulcan, possessed the love-inspiring Cestus, and was associated with swans, doves, the rose, and the myrtle.

Chapter V

Venus hears Pygmalion's prayer and brings his ivory statue to life, then blesses their marriage. She falls deeply in love with Adonis, warns him against dangerous animals, and later mourns his death by changing his blood into the anemone.

CUPID AND PSYCHE

Venus becomes angry because Psyche receives worship that she believes belongs only to her. She persecutes Psyche with a series of difficult tasks but eventually consents to Psyche's marriage to Cupid after Cupid pleads with Jupiter.

Chapter VIII

Venus was the goddess whom Hero served as a priestess. In the account derived from Schiller's ballad, Hero prayed to Venus to calm the storm after Leander had entered the dangerous sea.

MEDEA AND AESON

Venus hears Hippomenes's prayer and gives him three golden apples from her tree in Cyprus. When Atalanta and Hippomenes fail to honor her after their victory, Venus causes them to offend Cybele.

Chapter XX

Venus promises Paris the fairest of women as his wife if he awards her the golden apple. Paris chooses her, and she protects him as he sails to Greece and persuades Helen to elope with him.
