Altis

Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Altis is the sacred grove dedicated to Zeus by Herakles, who fenced and bounded it, and named after the Hill of Kronos. In Description of Greece, Altis is the sacred grove of Zeus at Olympia and a densely furnished sanctuary and ceremonial precinct.

Altis in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Altis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Altis is the sacred grove of Zeus at Olympia, also a sanctuary precinct and Olympic sanctuary. Its name is described as a slightly altered form of the Greek word for grove, and Pindar used it in an ode for a victor at Olympia. It is above all a densely furnished sacred and ceremonial place: it has a processional road and entrance, numerous altars, temples, city treasuries, the Town Hall of the people of Elis, the Hippodamium, and facilities associated with the Olympian games. Its altars belong to many named gods and figures, while its temples include the Temple of the Mother. Throughout it stand statues, pillars, monuments, and votive offerings from athletes, communities, and individuals, including statues of Olympic victors, statues of Zeus made from athletic fines, the largest brazen Zeus, and the pillar of Oenomaus. Particular objects include Phormis’s bronze horse, the Mendæan offerings, and a brazen trophy beneath plane-trees, dedicated by the people of Elis from Lacedæmonian spoils. Altis also preserves conflict: during the Lacedæmonian invasion, the people of Elis defended themselves from its temples and high buildings, and later a trophy marked victory over the Laconians. Its described course moves through entrances, altars, temples, walls, monuments, and game-related places, ending with its many statues, offerings, treasuries, and facilities.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

8 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VIII.

The sacred grove of Zeus at Olympia was called Altis, a slightly altered form of the Greek word for grove. Pindar also used the name Altis in an ode composed for a victor at Olympia.

CHAPTER XV.

Altis contains numerous altars, including those of Aphrodite, the Seasons, Artemis, Zeus, Apollo, Dionysus, the Fates, Hermes, Poseidon, Hera, Castor and Pollux, Ares, Athene, Pan, and Aphrodite. It also contains the Town Hall of the people of Elis and is entered by a road used for processions.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Altis contains votive offerings, temples, statues of Zeus made from athletic fines, the pillar called the pillar of Oenomaus, and the Temple of the Mother. During a battle between the Lacedaemonians and Elis, the people of Elis defended themselves from the temples and other high buildings inside Altis.

CHAPTER XXII.

Altis contains numerous statues and votive offerings, including the largest brazen statue of Zeus, the statues of the boys from the Sicilian Strait, and several other offerings on its walls and near its temples. An altar near the entrance to the course is used by trumpeters and heralds when they proclaim the games.

CHAPTER XXVI.

It contained Phormis’s bronze horse, which stallions repeatedly entered or rushed toward in attempts to mount it. Under its plane trees stood a bronze trophy dedicated by the people of Elis from Lacedæmonian spoils, and it also contained the offerings of the Mendæans and other monuments.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

Altis contained the statues and monuments described by Pausanias, including those of athletes, horsemen, and seers. A fight took place there during the Lacedæmonian invasion of Elis, and Dædalus later erected a trophy for the Eleans after their victory over the Laconians.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Altis contains the statues and pillars described throughout the unit. Milo was said to have carried his own statue there, Pyrrhus had a statue erected there by Thrasybulus of Elis, and Dinosthenes set up a pillar there recording a distance to another pillar at Lacedæmon.

CHAPTER XVII.

Altis contains numerous statues, votive offerings, city treasuries, temples, the Hippodamium, and facilities associated with the Olympian games. The unit describes its processional entrance, the surrounding monuments, and objects dedicated by communities and individuals.
