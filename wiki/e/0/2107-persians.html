Persians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Persians are a people of Persia and of the Persian empire, repeatedly portrayed as a major military and political force. They fought Egyptians, at times ruled Egypt, Phoenicia, and Cyprus, and campaigned widely by land and sea. Against Evagoras they assembled roughly three hundred thousand horse and foot and more than three hundred galleys, gained the naval advantage, besieged Salamis, and ended the war with tribute from Evagoras. They invaded Egypt more than once: one invasion under Pharnabazus, aided by Iphicrates, withdrew after Egyptian attacks; under Artaxerxes they lost men in bogs near the Great Lough, captured Pelusium, obtained Egyptian cities’ submission through Mentor’s stratagems, plundered Egypt, and demolished city walls. They also faced revolts among coastal peoples, governors, and commanders, recovered Phoenicia after Sidon’s destruction, received the fleeing Tachos, and fought alongside Ariarathes against Egyptians. Their conflict with Alexander dominates the later record. Philip began war against them and sent forces into Asia. The Persians gathered forces from many regions, rejected Memnon’s advice, and were defeated at the Granicus; they then defended Miletus and resisted at Halicarnassus. Under Darius they fielded vast, diverse armies at Issus and Arbela. Their chariots and cavalry initially pressured the Macedonians, but Darius’s flight brought dispersal, flight, severe losses, and more than ninety thousand deaths in one account. They defended passes toward Persepolis, while Darius fled with thirty thousand Persians and mercenary Greeks. After these defeats, Alexander adopted selected Persian dress and court customs, appointed Persians to offices, chose a thousand as body squires, and mixed Persian archers, darters, and slingers into his army. Persians also preferred Peucestes as governor and continued to gather forces during disturbances in Greece.

Persians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Persians are an Asian people described as barbarians, inheritors of the Medes’ empire after Cyrus conquered Astyages. They ruled Egypt for one hundred and thirty-five years, despite revolts attributed to harsh governors and impiety toward Egyptian gods. After Cambyses burned temples, they carried Egyptian treasures to Asia and used Egyptian workmen on palaces at Persepolis, Susa, and elsewhere in Media. Their earlier history was recorded from Persian public records, according to the account of Ctesias. Their military record begins with a failed invasion of Scythia under Cyrus, whose army was routed by the Scythian queen. Under Xerxes, they campaigned against Greeks: an unexpected attack caused fear, confusion, and heavy losses, though they later surrounded and destroyed the remaining defenders with missiles. The extraordinary attack by five hundred defenders terrified and discouraged them. They lost many men at Thermopylae, ships to storms and fighting, and two hundred galleys at Salamis, where their fleet was defeated in narrow straits. Although the king and many troops escaped, their army was later defeated at Plataea; Artabazus led about forty thousand men back to Asia. They continued to intervene in Greek affairs through garrisons, money, alliances, and offers to Athens, and suffered further defeats and territorial losses in Caria, Lycia, Cyprus, and at Eurymedon. They recovered Egypt after an initial setback, supplied the victorious fleet at Cnidus, fought Agesilaus unsuccessfully near Sipylus, and remained engaged against Lacedaemonians and other Grecians. Their forces could be lightly armed or inexperienced, but royalist Persians recovered after Cyrus’s death and defeated his army. After peace, Artaxerxes prepared forces for Cyprus.

The Persians offered the Athenians territory, restored walls and temples, and the enjoyment of their former laws and liberties if they would ally with Persia. Their army was defeated at Plataea, although Artabazus led about forty thousand men safely back into Asia.

The Persians were defeated by the Greeks in the sea fight in which Mandona lost her children. Themistocles later reached Persia, learned the Persian language, and was acquitted before the Persian senate.

CHAP. XIII.

The Persians lost cities in Caria and Lycia, a naval battle at Cyprus, and a land battle at Eurymedon. They lost ships, soldiers, prisoners, and treasure, and afterward began building more ships because of their fear of the Athenians.

The Persians fought to recover Egypt after the revolt. Their forces were initially defeated by the Egyptians and Athenians, but a later Persian army raised the siege of the White Wall and compelled the Egyptians to submit.

The Persians led the barbarian contingents in their respective companies and regiments. Their forces included many lightly armed or inexperienced soldiers, but a Persian common soldier mortally wounded Cyrus, and the royalist Persians recovered after his death and defeated his army.

CHAP. vm.

The Persians opposed Agesilaus in Asia. Their forces under Tissaphernes were defeated near Sipylus, and their possessions were plundered as far as Sardis.

CHAP. IX,

The Persians supplied the fleet commanded by Conon and Pharnabazus. Their fleet defeated the Lacedaemonian fleet at Cnidus and helped undermine Lacedaemonian control of the sea.

CHAP. XIII.

The Persians were involved in the long war against the Lacedaemonians and other Grecians. After peace was made, Artaxerxes prepared Persian forces for the war in Cyprus.

Persians in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Persians invade Attica, land at Marathon, and bring Parian marble to Rhamnus to prepare a victory trophy before conquering Athens. They are defeated at Marathon, driven into marshy ground and Marathon’s muddy lake, killed while boarding ships, and lose ships in retreat. Some also land at the Acropolis and kill Athenians behind wooden palisades. Spoil taken from them is used for Phidias’s brazen statue of Athene.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIV.

The Persians are depicted in the Painted Chamber being defeated at Marathon, driven into the marsh, and killed while attempting to board their ships. They also landed at the Acropolis and killed Athenians who had fortified it with wooden palisades.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

The Persians who landed at Marathon were defeated, and spoil taken from them was used to make Phidias's brazen statue of Athene. Philippides reported that the Lacedæmonians delayed marching against the Persians because they would not campaign until the moon was full.

CHAPTER XXXI.

The Persians landed at Marathon but were defeated and lost some ships while retreating. Many fugitives fell into Marathon’s muddy lake, and those who landed at Rhamnus brought Parian marble for a victory trophy before they had conquered Athens.

Persians in Musical Instruments

Carl Engel · 1875-08

Persians had instruments resembling Assyrians’ and Hebrews’ before the Christian era, and later possessed an advanced musical culture with chang forms, small intervals, and diverse instruments before Arab conquest.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

THE PERSIANS AND ARABS.

The Persians possessed musical instruments and a musical culture that were more advanced than those of the Arabs when the Arabs conquered Persia. They used various forms of the chang, smaller musical intervals than semitones, and instruments including the lute, tamboura, kanoon, santir, rebab, kemangeh, nay, and surnay.
