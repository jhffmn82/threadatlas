Achaeans in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Achaeans first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Achaeans are a Greek army, also called Argives and Danaans, led chiefly by Agamemnon son of Atreus. They besiege Troy for nine years before the tenth, wage war against the Trojans over Helen, and possess a fleet, ships, tents, chariots, and a shore-side camp protected by a wall, gates, trench, and stakes. Their story begins with the sack of Thebe, division of spoil, and Apollo’s deadly pestilence. They return Chryseis, purify themselves, and sacrifice, but a quarrel among their leaders causes Achilles to refuse fighting. Though they initially rush toward their ships when return is proposed, they are recalled to the war, gather in force, make and trust oaths with the Trojans, and repeatedly fight, gain ground, and suffer reverses. As Trojan pressure grows, the Achaeans build defenses around their ships, maintain watches, inspect their guards, and send Ulysses and Diomed to gather intelligence. Their leaders are wounded, they are driven through wall and trench toward the ships, and their army is repeatedly frightened, routed, or forced into desperate defense. Encouragement from figures including Discord, Neptune, Ajax, Nestor, and Idomeneus helps them resist. Patroclus leads the Myrmidons to recover ground, but dies; the Achaeans fight fiercely over bodies including Sarpedon’s and Patroclus’s, eventually recover Patroclus, and mourn him. Achilles’ return changes the battle: the Achaeans arm, press the Trojans back toward Troy, nearly take its gates, and defeat Hector. They strip and carry Hector’s body to their ships. They then mourn and bury Patroclus, hold funeral contests, and remain encamped beside the ships. At the end, Priam secretly crosses their camp for Hector’s body, and Achilles promises they will not attack Troy during the agreed mourning and funeral period.

Danaans in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Danaans first appears

Abstract written by a model

Danaans are the Greek forces assembled for the Trojan War: the Achaeans, Argives, and Greek army besieging Troy. They are warriors with ships and tents, organized by regional contingents, chiefs, territories, and ships. Their host crosses the plain toward the city in a movement heavy enough to make the earth groan. They fight Trojans and gods fighting mortals, serve Mars, and repeatedly defend their ships and positions. After Agamemnon speaks, they applaud; rallied by him, they advance silently and in order, kill Trojan fighters, and suffer losses. They stand against Trojan attacks but retreat when Mars is with the Trojans. At sunset they slaughter oxen, eat at their tents, obtain wine with bronze, iron, hides, whole heifers, and captives, feast all night, and make wine offerings to Jove before sleeping. Their fortunes worsen as Hector and the Trojans inflict heavy losses; Agamemnon prays for their survival, and Jove grants that his people will live. An embassy seeks Achilles’ help and offers gifts, but he rejects the value of their fighting and tells them to find another way to save their ships and people. They try to hold the wall and battlements, yet flee toward their ships after Hector enters the gates. With the wall broken and the ships threatened, Neptune restores their courage. They form a dense line around the two Ajaxes, exchange armour so their strongest fighters have the best equipment, check Hector, and then attack fiercely. They kill Trojan chieftains, pursue fleeing Trojans, and gain the upper hand. Finally, they defend Patroclus’s body in close formation: driven back at first, they rally around Ajax and fight throughout the day.

Abstract written by a model

The Argives are another name for the Achaeans, the Greek army also called Danaans, assembled against the Trojans. They possess the Achaean ships, which serve as their camp, refuge, and the chief point of danger in the war. Led and counselled by figures including Agamemnon, Ulysses, Nestor, Ajax, and Achilles, they fight to take the Trojan city. They are first stirred by Agamemnon’s proposal to sail home, but Ulysses and Nestor recall them to assembly and persuade them to continue. Agamemnon urges them to attack bravely; they advance with a shout, yet suffer severe losses while Mars aids their enemies. Juno rebukes their loss of courage and restores their spirit. As Jove’s favor turns against them, they retreat toward their ships, rally briefly, and are repeatedly driven back by Hector and the Trojans. Their danger becomes extreme: destruction at the ships is threatened, leaders must remain watchful and united, and the Trojans guard against escape by sea. Though panic occurs, they regroup around Eurypylus and Ajax, fight in order within the wall, defend it against the Lycians, and fiercely protect their ships. Hector nevertheless breaks the gates. Later, Achilles summons the Achaean heroes; they witness his reconciliation with Agamemnon and prepare to fight. After Hector’s death, Achilles calls the Argives his friends, princes, and counsellors and proposes an attack on the city. They also participate in and judge the chariot races. At the end, their ships and camps remain near Achilles as Priam is told to cross the Achaeans’ camp and go to their ships to ransom Hector’s body.

They form a large naval host at Aulis, equipped with ships, arms, shields, horses, and chariots, intending to recover Helen, avenge the abduction of Greek wives, reach Troy, and sack it. Detained by a calm and awaiting favorable winds at Aulis and Euripus, they are told by Calchas that Iphigenia must be sacrificed to secure the voyage. The army demands the sacrifice, can pressure Agamemnon and threaten his family, and turns against Achilles when he tries to save her. Iphigenia accepts death for their victory, rule over barbarians, and the distinction that Greeks are free while barbarians are enslaved. They sail and wage the Trojan War against the Trojans, suffering deaths as well as inflicting destruction. They besiege, capture, and destroy Troy by military force. After conquest, they hold Trojan women as captives and booty, while their campaign is also presented as bringing ruin to both Greeks and Phrygians because of Helen’s beauty. After Troy falls, the Greeks remain with their ships anchored on the Chersonese. Achilles’ appearance prevents their departure, and they debate whether his tomb should receive a victim. They decree and carry out Polyxena’s sacrifice to honor Achilles and preserve honor for brave dead; the whole army attends. Though portrayed as capable of disorder and harshness, they respond to Polyxena’s courage, allow her to die freely, and honor her body with leaves and funeral offerings. Agamemnon sends men to report her death and burial. The Greeks are friendly to Polymestor but regard Polydore as an enemy, complicating aid to Hecuba; yet Agamemnon says Greeks abhor killing guests. They ultimately prepare to sail home. Elsewhere, Greek captives Orestes and Pylades face sacrifice in Tauri, while Iphigenia longs for news from the Greek land.

the Greeks in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where the Greeks first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greeks are the Greek or Argive army and host assembled at Troy under leaders including Menelaus, Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax, Tydeus’ son, Ulysses, and Diomede. They wage the Trojan War against Troy over Helen, endure many deaths before its walls, and are said by Menelaus to become valiant through the war. During the conflict they occupy a camp and fleet near Troy, kindle fires that alarm Trojan guards, and send Ulysses and Diomede into the Trojan camp, where they kill Rhesus. They ultimately deceive Troy with the wooden horse, storm and sack it with spear and fire, kill its defenders, take spoils, and become victorious. They capture, divide by lot, and enslave Trojan women, including Hecuba; kill Astyanax because they fear he may rebuild Ilion; and prepare to burn the city. After the fall, the army remains encamped in the Thracian Chersonesus while unfavorable conditions delay its return to Greece. By general consent it resolves to sacrifice Polyxena at Achilles’ tomb, carries out the killing, and then honors her corpse with foliage, a pyre, and ornaments. It treats Polymestor as an ally despite Hecuba’s dead son being regarded as an enemy, and Agamemnon declines to aid Polymestor after his blinding. The host assigns captives and prepares its homeward voyage, but Minerva and Neptune plan storms and destruction at sea; later, the returning fleet is wrecked by Nauplius’s deceptive beacon at Caphareus. Greeks accompanying Menelaus also fight Egyptians aboard a ship and prevail.

Greeks in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Greeks first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greeks are a collection of cities and peoples repeatedly struggling over freedom, self-government, foreign service, and common action. They are temporarily freed from garrisons and govern by their own laws under the Peace of Antalcidas, but renewed interventions and the attack on Mantinea bring war again. They preserve or seek ancient Greek liberties, while also serving abroad as mercenaries and auxiliaries: in Egyptian conflicts they fight on both sides, resist at Pelusium, fight at Bubastis, and some later serve Carthaginians against Agathocles, with disastrous results. Philip’s military success initially frightens the Greeks, then wins voluntary submission when he promises war against the Persians for their benefit and revenge for Persian impiety. Greek cities honor him with gold crowns, make him general of all Greece, and promise forces for an Asian expedition, though his death stops him from leading it. Greeks later aid Darius with money and foreign soldiers, while Athenians remain loyal to Alexander. They endure severe Persian cruelty, including enslaved and mutilated captives, and after Thebes is destroyed they send ambassadors to Alexander and serve in his campaigns. They distinguish human honors from divine worship and, in Callisthenes’s argument, would resist being compelled to adore Alexander as a god. After Alexander’s death they revolt, defeat Leonatus in Thessaly, but lose to Antipater and Craterus. Their confederacy dissolves as cities make separate peace; Athenians and Aetolians continue, and eventual submission follows except for the Aetolians. Later Demetrius frees several cities while retaining Corinth’s citadel at the citizens’ request, and Flamininus promises freedom, removal of garrisons, and rule by their own laws. Throughout, Greek unity is partial and unstable, but liberty, local laws, and resistance to domination remain central.

Greeks in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Greeks first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greeks are presented as a people of Greece and Asia whose antiquities, myths, laws, customs, eloquence, and political community are examined after those of foreign peoples. They are said to lack knowledge of other nations’ antiquity and, unlike the Chaldeans, to differ in philosophical learning; they also travel to Egypt to learn laws and customs. Egyptian priests accuse them of transferring Egyptian gods, heroes, rites, and institutions into Greek traditions, while Greek accounts identify figures such as Dionysius and Osiris differently. They trade with and are welcomed in Egypt, and their learning influences Psammetichus’s family. Their expansion unfolds through settlement and conquest: they are the last settlers mentioned in Sicily, build coastal cities, spread Greek language and ways of living, and, after Troy’s destruction, become powerful enough to expel Carians from most of the Cyclades. Their central story is resistance to Persia. At Thermopylae, they choose liberty and honour over life and slavery, remain unanimous in holding their post, attack the Persian camp, and sacrifice themselves for Greece’s common safety. Their example inspires other Greeks. They then fight at Artemisium and Salamis, where their fleet defeats the Persians in the straits despite losing forty ships. A confederate army swears to defend liberty, defeats Persia at Plataea, and dedicates spoils at Delphi. A fleet aids Asian inhabitants, defeats Persia at Mycale, and accepts Ionians and Eolians as confederates; formerly Persian-held cities are freed and some seek Athenian protection. Greeks are also shown organizing confederacies against other threats, sharing rights and privileges within the Greek community, and governing cities by their own laws. They value mercy toward surrendered enemies, but eventually accept Artaxerxes’s peace terms, despite distress over the Asian cities, because they cannot resist Persia.

The Greeks are the forces assembled against Troy. When the Trojans refuse, the Greeks land, kill many, confine the Trojans within Troy, and begin a siege. They decide Astyanax’s death in an assembly according to some accounts. Afterward they quarrel over departure and sacrifice to Athena, leave in separate groups, and endure storms, shipwrecks, and Nauplius’s false lights. Some sail to Mimas, and some settle in Italy.

Greeks in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Greeks first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greeks are a broad collective appearing across shared religious practice, warfare, settlement, memory, and representation. In early times they honored unhewn stones as gods. Before sailing from Aulis, they consulted Calchas about sacrificing Iphigenia; adverse winds detained them until Artemis substituted a doe, after which favorable winds came. After the capture of Ilium, they shared spoil including a chest with a statue of Dionysus, prepared to sail, and administered an oath to Ajax over Cassandra’s violation; they also spared Laodice out of regard for Antenor’s family. They commemorate common struggles through joint tombs, trophies, and dedications. Those killed against the Medes had a common tomb near Platæa, and after that battle the Greeks jointly offered a golden tripod. Greeks who fought the Persian King dedicated a brazen Zeus at Olympia and an Apollo at Delphi after Artemisium and Salamis. They later suffered a fatal defeat at Chæronea, while Epaminondas was credited with winning their freedom. Their unity is repeatedly tested. They were initially not banded together, enabling the Achæans to grow powerful, while Philip established garrisons and raided Greek communities; Flaminius sought the Achæans as Roman allies and Philip was required to remove seized garrisons. In the invasion by the Galati, Greeks defended Thermopylæ, fought and buried their dead and Galatian dead, were evacuated when threatened with encirclement, then assembled at Delphi and defeated the retreating enemy. The records also show Greeks as settlers and traders: Cretans under Rhacius reached Claros, Greeks helped found several communities, and they came to Sardinia under Aristaeus, where later conflict nearly destroyed them. They preserve prophetic traditions, including Herophile, and are portrayed collectively through named figures in scenes after Ilium.

The Greeks said that the first Herophile was the daughter of Zeus and Lamia and the first woman to chant oracles. They also preserved traditions about the Herophiles and other prophetically inspired figures.

Greek traders called Sardinia Ichnusa, and later Greeks came to the island under Aristæus. CHAPTER XIX.

The Greeks were the opponents of the Galati in the major invasion of Greece, whose greatest struggle occurred at Delphi. Brennus regarded Greece as weak at that period and sought to exploit its wealth and temple treasures.

The Greeks assembled a strong force at Thermopylæ and advanced silently and in good order against the Galati. Their infantry maintained its line, while their light-armed troops used darts, arrows, and slings; after the battle they buried their dead and stripped the bodies of the barbarians.

The Greeks gathered at Delphi to fight Brennus and the Galati. They resisted the barbarians throughout the battle and then attacked them during their retreat, with Athenians, Bœotians, Phocians, Ætolians, Thessalians, and Malienses contributing to the defeat.

Abstract written by a model

The Achaeans are the Greek/Argive army and host of warriors that besieges Troy from outside its walls for a long period. Argives in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where Argives first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Argives are the Greek and Achaean army opposing, besieging, and ultimately conquering Troy. Seeking vengeance connected with Helen's abduction, they fight a long, costly war marked by repeated losses, mourning, burial of their dead, and determination to continue. At first, a Trojan charge with Penthesileia drives them back and kills many. They strip fallen enemies, honor their own dead, celebrate Achilles, and endure further losses to Memnon while grieving Antilochus. They fiercely defend Achilles' corpse, mourn Achilles beside the ships, and take part in or watch his funeral contests. The dispute over Achilles' arms divides them: Odysseus' victory shocks them, and Aias' madness endangers both ships and lives. Under Eurypylus, the Argives suffer severe defeats and flee toward their ships; some remain behind to bury Machaon and Nireus. Athena protects and encourages them to hold the rampart. Their fortunes improve when they trust Neoptolemus as their chief hope: they follow him eagerly, kill many Trojans, drive them into Troy, and assault the city. Zeus temporarily stops their attack by protecting Troy with storm and mist, after which they withdraw, bury their dead, praise Achilles' son, and keep watch. They resume fighting despite heavy losses under Deiphobus, attack Troy's gates and walls with engines and a shield-wall under Odysseus, but are again driven back when Aeneas breaks their ranks. Finally, after sailing from Tenedos at Sinon's signal, they enter Troy, kill Trojans, burn homes and temples, kill Priam and Astyanax, take captives, and spare Antenor's household while heeding Calchas's warning about Aeneas. They ravage, destroy, and plunder the city, enslave Trojan women, and celebrate victory. Before departing, they sacrifice Polyxeina to appease Achilles. As conquerors, they leave with captives, wealth, trophies, and spoils, seeking Argive home-return, though Calchas warns that destruction lies ahead.

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where Greeks first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Greeks are a people described with the Romans as belonging to the Aryan family of mankind and, according to the stated history, descending from Northern India. Also called Hellas in the period described, they were divided among many small kingdoms, but common undertakings such as the Calydonian Hunt fostered a sense of one nation. Their writers supplied most stories of ancient mythology, and they peopled nature with invisible beings. Greeks joined Jason’s expedition, including Hercules, Theseus, Orpheus, and Nestor; they endured its dangers and returned with Jason and Medea. Later, they assembled a vast fleet and army at Aulis under Agamemnon to recover Helen and capture Troy. They suffered pestilence and defeats after Achilles withdrew, recovered Patroclus’s body, avenged him, and held his funeral rites and games. They ultimately rescued Achilles’s body, obtained the Palladium, entered Troy through the Wooden Horse, carried away captives, and began returning to Greece.
