The Amores, or Amours in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

The document is a sequence of elegies called the Amours, reduced by its author from five books to three. It follows a poet-narrator, often named Naso or Ovid, whose intended poetry of arms is redirected by Cupid into love elegy: Cupid alters his meter, wounds him with love, and makes him Love’s poet and servant. The narrator repeatedly centers his desire for Corinna, though he also pursues or imagines other women, addresses servants, husbands, rivals, friends, gods, and personified figures such as Elegy and Tragedy. The poems unfold through changing episodes of erotic pursuit, frustration, jealousy, reconciliation, and self-reflection. The lover celebrates encounters with Corinna, seeks access to her house, sends letters and gifts, flirts with her at banquets and races, and longs for her during separation. Yet doors remain barred, messages are refused, guardians such as Bagous obstruct him, rivers delay him, and her husband’s presence or surveillance becomes an obstacle and sometimes an erotic stimulus. He urges deception and concealment in adulterous affairs, advises husbands both to guard and not to guard their wives, and repeatedly struggles with Corinna’s suspected or witnessed infidelity. The narrator’s conduct is often conflicted: he repents after beating his mistress, is enraged by advice urging women to seek wealthy lovers, condemns payment in love, but pursues Corinna’s handmaid Cypassis and threatens her. He complains of sexual failure, rivalry with a wealthy Knight, Corinna’s perjuries, and the public fame his own poetry gives her. Corinna’s dangerous pregnancy and attempted destruction of an embryo prompt prayers and reproaches. Other poems mourn Corinna’s parrot and Tibullus, praise poetry’s lasting power, describe Corinna’s damaged hair, and celebrate the narrator’s native Sulmo while lamenting her absence. Across the three books, poetry itself remains central: verses are presented as magical, as a substitute for wealth, and as a source of immortality, although they also expose the beloved to rivals. In the final book, Tragedy asks the poet to leave love poetry for heroic subjects but grants him time to continue. The closing elegy announces that he is ending these sportive, unwarlike Elegies for a longer work, leaving behind the Amours while claiming future glory for himself and the Pelignian land.

AN EPIGRAM ON THE AMOURS.

The unit concerns the Amours, described as a work that once consisted of five books and now consists of three. The author has preferred this work to the former version by removing two books. Although reading it may no longer give the reader pleasure, the reduced work requires less labour to read.

ELEGY I.

The poet begins by intending to write about arms and warfare in serious hexameters. Cupid interrupts by removing a metrical foot from every second line and then shoots the poet with an arrow. The poet consequently falls in love and abandons warfare for poetry in six-foot lines that close in five. He dedicates his new elegiac work to Love and wears myrtle around his temples.

ELEGY II.

Elegy II presents the narrator as newly conquered by Cupid and choosing to submit rather than resist Love. He imagines Cupid celebrating a triumph in which the narrator and the forces opposed to Love are led as captives, while Cupid is honored with a chariot, attendants, and acclaim. Elegy III has the narrator ask his beloved mistress either to love him or to give him a reason to continue loving her. He claims constancy, modesty, poetic talent, and a desire to have their names joined in song.

ELEGY IV.

The speaker, who is the lover, instructs his mistress how to behave at a banquet attended by her husband. He tells her to communicate with him through gestures, touch his foot, share wine discreetly, and avoid showing affection to her husband. He fears the husband's physical advances and sexual intimacy with the mistress, especially after the banquet, and asks her to assure him the next day that she did not enjoy them.

ELEGY V.

The narrator describes a midday encounter with Corinna during summer. Corinna enters his chamber in a loose tunic, and he undresses her despite her apparent attempt to remain covered. He praises her unblemished body, embraces her, and says that they later recline, wearied, wishing for such a midday again.

ELEGY VI.

The lover begs the porter to open the door of his mistress's house and repeatedly asks him to strike away the bar. He recalls that Love made him brave, and he tries entreaties, promises, threats, wine, and finally considers using his sword and torch. The porter never opens the door, and the lover remains outside through the night until dawn approaches. He bids farewell to the porter, the door-posts, and the doors, lamenting the time he has wasted.

ELEGY VII.

The speaker repents after beating his mistress and wishes to be punished for injuring her. He recalls her fear, tears, disordered hair, and pale appearance, and asks her to retaliate against him. In the next elegy, he overhears Dipsas advising a damsel to pursue wealthy lovers through deception, rivalry, pretended affection, and repeated demands for gifts and payments. The speaker is enraged by Dipsas's instructions and curses her.

ELEGY IX.

The speaker tells Atticus that every lover is a soldier and that Love has its own camp, since lovers endure sleepless nights, long marches, harsh weather, sieges, dangers, and watchfulness like soldiers. He argues that Love requires discernment rather than mere laziness, citing Achilles, Hector, Agamemnon, and Mars as figures affected by desire. The speaker says that Love roused him from idleness and commanded him to serve in its camp, making him active by night.

ELEGY X.

The speaker tells his mistress that requiring presents has destroyed his former love for her. He argues that love should be naked, childlike, and free from sordid payment, unlike prostitution or bribery. He says that sexual pleasure is shared equally by woman and man and should not be sold by one to the other. The speaker offers duty, zeal, fidelity, and poetry instead of money, promising lasting fame to the woman he chooses.

ELEGY XI.

The speaker asks Nape to deliver his inscribed tablets to Corinna and urges her to overcome any delay. He instructs Nape to observe Corinna's reaction, have her read the tablets immediately, and request a long reply, though he would be satisfied if she wrote only "Come." Corinna instead sends back a letter refusing to be seen that day. The speaker then curses the tablets, blaming their unlucky material and color for carrying her refusal.

ELEGY XIII.

The speaker begs Aurora not to hasten the arrival of morning because he is enjoying sleep and the company of his mistress. He criticizes Aurora for waking sailors, travelers, soldiers, farmers, children, lawyers, advocates, and workers. He recalls her passion for Cephalus and contrasts her conduct with the extra nights the Moon and Jupiter once granted to their lovers. Aurora blushes at his reproaches, but the day still rises no later than usual.

ELEGY XIV.

The mistress has nearly lost her hair after repeatedly treating it with noxious compositions, despite the speaker's earlier warnings. He recalls that her natural hair was abundant, fine, manageable, and beautiful, but that she subjected it to bodkins, combs, iron, and fire. He explains that the damage was self-inflicted rather than caused by magic, illness, or envy, and predicts that captured German hair will be used to adorn her. The mistress is ashamed of relying on bought hair, but the speaker comforts her that her natural hair may eventually grow back.

ELEGY XV.

The speaker answers Envy by defending poetry as a pursuit that seeks everlasting fame rather than perishable military or legal honors. He argues that poets including the Mæonian bard, the Ascræan, Sophocles, Menander, Ennius, Lucretius, and others will be remembered for as long as the world endures. Rome will remain associated with Tityrus, the harvests, and the arms of Æneas while it rules the conquered earth. The speaker concludes that poetry will survive monarchs, triumphs, wealth, and even death.

BOOK THE SECOND ELEGY I.

Naso says that Love and Cupid have made him write about love instead of the wars of heaven and the Giants. He recounts abandoning Jupiter and his thunderbolts when his mistress shut her door against him, because gentle Elegies and verses won access where force could not. He declares that verses have magical power and that singing of a beautiful fair being can win her as the Poet’s reward. He invites young lovers and charming fair women to read his lines, while warning prudish matrons away.

ELEGY II.

Ovid addresses Bagous, the eunuch assigned to watch his mistress, after the mistress refuses Ovid's request to visit her because of Bagous's strict surveillance. He urges Bagous to relax his watch and secretly aid the mistress's meetings by overlooking letters, visitors, absences, and visits to temples or theatres. Ovid argues that informers and accusers are punished or distrusted, while a husband is likely either indifferent or made miserable by accusations. He presents the proposed affair as harmless because it involves neither poison nor violence, only a wish to love and meet safely.

ELEGY III.

The poet addresses Bagous, who guards his mistress and has resisted the poet’s request, by sympathizing with his inability to experience mutual love. He urges Bagous to abandon manly pursuits, serve his mistress, and seek her favor while the opportunity remains. The poet then confesses that he is attracted to every kind of fair woman, finding charm in differences of character, appearance, age, talent, and manner. He concludes that his passion desires whatever qualities people admire in women throughout the City.

ELEGY V.

The speaker confronts his mistress after witnessing her secretly communicating and exchanging passionate kisses with a rival. He recalls their gestures, coded words, and behavior at the table, which reveal her guilt and infidelity. Though angered and tempted to hurt her, he is disarmed by her beauty and instead begs for kisses like those she gave the rival. Her kisses are so skilled and delightful that he suspects she learned them through closer acquaintance with someone else.

ELEGY VI.

The speaker laments the death of the parrot he gave to Corinna and calls on the birds to mourn it. He praises the parrot's plumage, peaceful nature, frugal habits, and skill at imitating human speech, noting that it died after crying, "Corinna, farewell!" The parrot is imagined among innocent birds at the foot of the Elysian hill, where a small sepulchre bears an inscription honoring its devotion to its mistress and its eloquence. The speaker then protests Corinna's repeated accusations and denies that he had an affair with her handmaid Cypassis.

ELEGY VIII.

Corinna discovers the speaker’s affair with Cypassis, her handmaid, and reacts angrily. The speaker defends the affair by denying that he revealed it and by citing famous men who loved enslaved or captive women. He then asks Cypassis for her company and threatens to expose their earlier meetings if she refuses.

ELEGY IX.

The speaker addresses Cupid, complaining that Cupid wounds and torments a loyal soldier while sparing those who resist him. The speaker compares his love-sickness to the exhaustion of soldiers, horses, ships, and weapons after service, yet admits that he would refuse release from love because the fair are such a sweet evil. He asks Cupid to resume attacking him and describes the uncertainty of his mistress’s affection as a source of both hope and misery. He finally asks Cupid, with his mother, to rule his heart and extend his realm over the damsels.

ELEGY X.

The speaker tells Græcinus that he is in love with two mistresses at the same time, and that his feelings fluctuate between them. He celebrates his devotion to Love and wishes to die in Love's service. He tries to dissuade Corinna from sailing to Baiæ by describing the dangers of the ocean, though he promises to welcome her ship if she returns safely. He then celebrates his success in overcoming the obstacles guarding Corinna and compares it to a triumph won through his own efforts.

ELEGY XIII.

Corinna lies exhausted and in danger of her life while attempting to deliver the burden of her pregnant womb. Naso believes that he may be the father of the child and fears for Corinna despite being angry that she undertook the risk without his knowledge. He prays to Isis and Lucina, or Ilithyia, to save Corinna and promises offerings and an inscription in her honor. He urges Corinna not to undergo such a struggle again.

ELEGY XIV.

The speaker reproaches his mistress for attempting to destroy the embryo in her womb. He argues that such an act is unnatural and recalls examples of famous mothers whose children became important figures, including Achilles, Romulus, and Aeneas. He warns that attempts to end a pregnancy can kill the woman herself and prays that his mistress will survive this transgression, while hoping she will not repeat it.

ELEGY XV.

The speaker addresses a ring that he is giving to his mistress and envies the ring’s opportunity to touch and be worn by her. He imagines being changed into the ring so that he could touch her bosom, seal her tablets, and remain with her. He sends the ring as a sign that his constancy is offered to his mistress.

ELEGY XVI.

The speaker is staying at Sulmo in the Pelignian land and praises its streams, fertile fields, vines, olives, and cool air. Despite these natural delights, the speaker cannot feel happy because his mistress is far away. He says that any journey would be pleasant with his love, even across dangerous mountains and seas. He urges his mistress to visit him and asks the hills and paths to become smooth for her arrival.

ELEGY XVII.

The speaker says he is the slave of Corinna and accepts disgrace for serving her, provided she torments him less severely. He praises her beauty while criticizing her pride and asks her to accept him despite their unequal standing. He compares their relationship with other unions between mortals and divine or heroic figures. He promises that his poems will celebrate only Corinna and provide her with fame instead of wealth.

ELEGY XVIII.

The speaker-poet contrasts Macer's work on the wrath of Achilles and the arms of heroes with his own retreat into Venus's shade and enslavement to Love. His mistress repeatedly defeats his attempts to leave her, drawing him from tragedy and other ambitious subjects back to love poetry. He describes letters and laments concerning figures including Penelope, Phyllis, Dido, and others, many of which his friend Sabinus has brought from different parts of the world. The speaker-poet urges Macer to include Love in his poems about war, suggesting that Macer prefers these subjects as much as warfare.

ELEGY XIX.

The lover tells Corinna's husband that he should watch Corinna more carefully, because obstacles, refusals, and uncertainty intensify desire and preserve love. He recalls how Corinna feigned headaches and faults, then rewarded his frustration with affection, kisses, and kind words. He urges the husband to become suspicious of Corinna's movements and companions, warning that an affair freely permitted will soon cease to interest him. The lover concludes that the husband's excessive leniency is spoiling his pleasure and that he should forbid the affair if he wants the lover to remain his rival.

---- BOOK THE THIRD. / ELEGY I.

The Poet walks in an ancient sacred grove and considers what work his Muse should begin. Elegy and Tragedy appear before him, with Tragedy urging him to abandon love poetry and write about heroic exploits, while Elegy defends her influence and usefulness. The Poet asks both forbearance, and Tragedy grants him a short respite to continue writing about tender Love before undertaking a greater task.

ELEGY II.

The speaker attends the chariot races in the Circus Maximus mainly to be near his mistress and watch her rather than the race. He flirts with her, helps adjust her clothing, and asks her to permit herself to be loved. During the race, he supports her favorite charioteer, who eventually wins after a difficult turn and a restart. The mistress signals her favor to the speaker, promising him something more in another place.

ELEGY III.

The speaker complains that his mistress has broken her oath to the Gods and to him, yet her beauty remains unchanged. He argues that the Gods allow beautiful women to commit perjury with impunity while men suffer punishment. The speaker questions whether the Gods exist or whether they favor charming women, recalling divine punishments inflicted on others. He finally asks the mistress to use the Gods’ favor more moderately and to show some regard for his eyes.

ELEGY IV.

The speaker advises a jealous husband that guarding his wife cannot make her chaste, because chastity must come from her own feelings and choice. Excessive surveillance and restraint instead increase temptation by making forbidden things more desirable. He cites Danaë, Penelope, and other examples to show that confinement does not prevent desire or wrongdoing. He urges the husband to treat his wife with indulgence and courtesy rather than asserting severe authority.

ELEGY V.

The narrator dreams of a white cow and a bull resting in a leafy grove beneath a sunny hill. A crow pecks the cow's breast, removes some of her white hair, and then leaves with the bull, causing the cow to become envious and depart among other herds. The interpreter of dreams explains that the heat represents Love, the cow represents the narrator's mistress, and the bull represents the narrator. The interpreter predicts that an old hag of a procuress will tempt the mistress, who will leave the narrator and may be inconstant.

ELEGY VI.

The speaker, hastening to reach his mistress, is stopped by a swollen, muddy river that he once crossed easily. He urges the river to remain within its banks and complains that it delays his love, wishing for legendary wings or a magical chariot to cross it. To persuade the river, he recounts the loves of rivers and river gods, including Inachus, Xanthus, Alpheus, Peneus, Asopus, Achelous, the Nile, Enipeus, and the river of Argive Tibur. The river continues to rise, and the speaker ends by regretting that he compared the unworthy torrent with such famous rivers.

ELEGY VII.

The male speaker describes being unable to have sex with an unnamed girl despite her embraces, kisses, flattering words, and other efforts to arouse him. He lies inert and feels shame, while wondering whether magic, poison, or exhaustion from love has caused his impotence. He recalls that he had recently satisfied other women and had previously endured repeated demands from Corinna. After the failed encounter, the male sexual organ becomes rigid too late, and the unnamed girl leaves after disguising the incident from her attendants.

ELEGY VIII.

The speaker-poet laments that his mistress prefers a wealthy rival Knight to him, despite the pleasure she takes in his poems. He describes the Knight as a recently enriched soldier whose wealth was gained through wounds and whose hands are stained with blood. He argues that gold and wealth now command love, status, and political power, while genius and poetry are neglected. He wishes that the Knight’s ill-gotten riches would be changed into dust.

ELEGY IX.

Elegy IX laments the death and funeral of the poet Tibullus. It depicts Venus, Cupid, Tibullus's relatives, Nemesis, and Delia grieving as his body is burned on the funeral pile. The poem reflects on Death's power over poets and the pious, while asserting that Tibullus's verse and fame will endure. It imagines Tibullus joining Catullus, Calvus, and Gallus in the Elysian vales.

ELEGY X.

The speaker complains to Ceres that her yearly rites separate him from his mistress, who must lie apart on a solitary couch. He recounts how Ceres taught humanity agriculture, including sowing, harvesting, ploughing, and yoking bulls. He then recalls Ceres's desire for Iasius, which caused her to neglect the fields until Crete alone remained fertile. The speaker argues that Ceres should not condemn lovers to single-blessedness, especially now that her daughter has returned and rules a realm.

ELEGY XI.

The speaker tells his mistress that her faults and betrayals have exhausted his patience, although he still cannot stop loving her. He recalls waiting at her door, witnessing her affairs, enduring her perjuries, and serving as her keeper, husband, and companion. Love and Hatred struggle within him: he despises her conduct but remains drawn back by her beauty. He asks the mistress to choose whether he may love willingly or must continue loving against his will.

ELEGY XII.

The speaker complains that his poems have made his mistress, Corinna, famous and thereby created many rivals for him. He blames himself for publicly proclaiming her charms and presenting himself as the procurer who introduces lovers to her. He questions whether poetry has any benefit, since it has harmed his happiness, and argues that poets are not usually believed as witnesses. He concludes that the mistress should likewise have been considered falsely praised, but that the credulity of others has injured him.

ELEGY XIII.

The speaker visits Falisci, the birthplace of his wife, to observe the festival of Juno. The festival includes a procession with animal victims, priestesses, youths, and girls, and honors Juno with rites associated with Argive ancestry. The sacred grove and altar are treated as holy, while a goat is excluded and used as a target in a ritual game. Halesus is said to have founded Falisci’s walls and taught its people the rites of Juno.

ELEGY XIV.

The speaker entreats his mistress to conceal her infidelity rather than confessing it or allowing him to witness its signs. He permits her sexual encounters in private but insists that she appear chaste in public and deny wrongdoing if questioned. He argues that concealment protects both her reputation and him from the pain of knowing, even promising to believe her denials against the evidence of his own eyes. The speaker admits that he loves her despite her faults and willingly lets her defeat him with a simple denial.

ELEGY X.

The poet tells Venus that he is ending his Elegies and turning to a more extended work. He identifies himself as a foster-child of the Pelignian fields and predicts that he will be the glory of the Pelignian race. He imagines Sulmo praised for producing so great a Poet. Lyæus has struck him with a powerful thyrsus, prompting him to leave his unwarlike, sportive Elegies behind.
