Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Cephalus is Deion’s Athenian son who owns an inescapable hound, accidentally kills Procris, is purified by the Cadmeans, and hunts the Teumesian fox. In The Library, Cephalus is Deion’s son, carried by Dawn to Syria, married to Procris, and the accidental killer of Procris, for which he is perpetually banished. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Cephalus is a prince, hunter, husband of Procris, ambassador from Athens, and accidental killer of Procris. In The Library, Cephalus is Deioneus’s son from Thoricus who joins Amphitryon with Laelaps, receives Tafian booty, and settles in a city founded under his name. In Bulfinch’s Mythology: The Age of Fable, Cephalus is a beautiful athletic youth, king of Athens, devoted hunter and husband of Procris who fatally wounds her while mistaking her for prey. (Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable)

Cephalus in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Cephalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cephalus is the son of Deïoneus and Diomeda, grandson of Æolus, prince, hunter, and later an ambassador from Athens. He marries Procris, and their early life is loving and happy. According to Ovid and other writers, Aurora carries him off; he later returns to Procris, though he is also said to love Aurora. Hunting and the chase shape his life: he frequents the forest, owns Lælaps, and uses a javelin. His affectionate calls to the breeze lead Procris to suspect him; he then tests her in disguise, later admits his fault, and they reconcile. When Procris hides in a thicket, he mistakes her sound for a wild beast and kills her with his javelin, leaving him grief-stricken and condemned to perpetual banishment in Cephalenia. As Athens’s ambassador, carrying an olive branch, he invokes old alliances and helps obtain Æacus’s forces for Athens.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Cephalus arrives from Athens as an ambassador, carrying his country’s request for aid. He reminds Æacus of their ancestors’ treaties and alliances, and his eloquence helps secure the forces of Æacus’s kingdom for Athens.

opening

Cephalus visits the king with the sons of Pallas and tells Phocus the story of his marriage, his suspicion of Procris, and their reconciliation. He owns that his disguised test of Procris was at fault and describes witnessing Lælaps pursue the wild beast.

notes

He was the son of Deïoneus, king of Phocis, and Diomeda, the daughter of Xuthus. He married Procris, and Ovid and other writers describe him as being carried off by Aurora and later returning to Procris.

opening

Cephalus describes his early marriage with Procris as mutually loving and happy, and he says that no other woman could have captivated him. While hunting, he often called affectionately to the breeze, unknowingly causing Procris to suspect him. He later mistook the sound of Procris concealed in the thicket for a wild beast and killed her with his javelin.

notes .. notes

Cephalus, the son of Deïoneus, loved the chase and rose early to enjoy it. He was reconciled with Procris, later became associated with the island of Cephalenia, and was condemned to perpetual banishment after Procris's death.

Cephalus in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855 · the work’s page · read it where Cephalus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cephalus is a beautiful, athletic youth, king of Athens, devoted to hunting and to his wife Procris. He visits Aeacus on Aegina seeking aid against Minos and learns of the Myrmidons. Later, mistaking Procris for prey in bushes, he fatally wounds her with her own javelin.

PYTHON

Cephalus was a beautiful youth devoted to hunting and to his wife, Procris. After mistaking Procris's movement in the bushes for a wild animal, he threw the javelin she had given him and fatally wounded her.

Chapter VII

Cephalus, king of Athens, visits Aeacus on Aegina to seek aid in his war with Minos, king of Crete. He is surprised by the number of young men around Aeacus and learns that they are the newly created Myrmidons.
