Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Abstract written by a model

This volume gathers nearly all surviving post-Homeric, pre-academic epic poetry: Hesiodic poems and fragments, Homeric Hymns and epigrams, fragments and summaries of the Epic Cycle, burlesque works, and the Contest of Homer and Hesiod. Its preface explains Hugh G. Evelyn-White’s editorial arrangement and manuscript-based text; its introduction contrasts the practical Boeotian tradition, represented by Hesiod, with the Homeric and Ionic heroic tradition, and recounts traditions of Hesiod’s life, quarrel with Perses, poetic inspiration, contest at Chalcis, death, and burial. A bibliography surveys manuscripts, papyri, editions, translations, and scholarship. The Hesiodic core divides between didactic and genealogical poetry. Works and Days addresses Perses, urging justice, industry, prudence, reciprocity, religious care, and seasonal preparation; myths of Prometheus, Pandora, and the declining races explain human hardship and Zeus’s punishment of injustice. Its later guidance covers farming, sailing, household conduct, purity, marriage, and auspicious days. Theogony begins with the Muses and primordial beings, traces divine births and conflicts, and culminates in Zeus’s rule: Cronos is overthrown, Titans imprisoned, Typhoeus defeated, and divine honors and genealogies distributed. Fragments of Astronomy, Precepts of Chiron, Great Works, Catalogues, Eoiae, Shield of Heracles, Melampodia, Aegimius, and doubtful works preserve moral sayings, star lore, seer tales, genealogies, heroic adventures, and Heracles’ battles. The Homeric Hymns celebrate gods through birth stories, powers, cults, and favors. Major narratives include Demeter’s loss and recovery of Persephone and the establishment of Eleusinian rites; Apollo’s births and foundations at Delos and Pytho; Hermes’ theft of Apollo’s cattle and invention of the lyre; Aphrodite’s union with Anchises; and Dionysus’ punishment of pirates. Shorter hymns invoke many gods for protection, prosperity, song, victory, seafaring safety, and peace. The Epic Cycle extends heroic legend beyond the Iliad and Odyssey. Theban poems recount Oedipus, his sons, and later avengers. The Trojan sequence moves from Zeus’s plan, Alexandrus’s judgment, Helen’s abduction, and the expedition in the Cypria, through Achilles’ death, Troy’s fall, Greek returns, and Odysseus’s death at Telegonus’s hands. The volume closes with disputed Homeric works, the comic Battle of Frogs and Mice, and a contest in which Homer’s verbal brilliance loses to Hesiod’s praise of peace and husbandry.

PREFACE

This volume contains practically all that remains of post-Homeric and pre-academic epic poetry. Hugh G. Evelyn-White largely formed his own text, using independent manuscript collations for Hesiod and relying otherwise on editions, especially Rzach. He arranged the complete and fragmentary poems to reflect their probable original order, placing the Catalogues after the Theogony. The volume also includes the Homeric Hymns, selected fragments of the Epic Cycle, the Batrachomyomachia, and the Contest of Homer and Hesiod.

INTRODUCTION

The unit traces early Greek epic through development, maturity, and decline, contrasting the romantic Homeric and Ionian tradition with the practical, matter-of-fact Boeotian School. It explains how the Boeotian School arose from indigenous maxims, Boeotian social character, and resistance to Homeric conventions. It presents Hesiod as an Aeolic settler’s son who lived as a farmer at Ascra, quarreled with his brother Perses, received poetic inspiration from the Muses, and won a contest at Chalcis. It also examines traditions about Hesiod’s death at Oenoe and the later treatment of his burial and remains.

The Hesiodic Poems

The Hesiodic Poems are divided into didactic poems centered on the Works and Days and genealogical poems centered on the Theogony. The Works and Days presents a moral program for living through industry, justice, prudence, and attention to favorable days, while the Theogony organizes the generations and genealogies of the gods. Other Hesiodic works, including the Catalogues of Women, the Eoiae, and the Shield of Heracles, survive only in fragments or are understood as later developments and appendices. The text dates the Works and Days probably to the middle of the ninth century B.C. and describes Hesiod's literary strengths as sincerity, vivid descriptions of nature, and a grave, child-like outlook.

The Ionic School

The Ionic School developed Homeric-style epic poetry that expanded heroic legend beyond the Iliad and Odyssey, especially through the Trojan and Theban stories. Its poems were later arranged into the Cycle, of which only fragments and Proclus’s synopsis survive. The Trojan Cycle connected events before and after the Iliad and Odyssey through works such as the Cyprian Lays, Aethiopis, Little Iliad, Sack of Troy, Returns, and Telegony. The unit also discusses the Homeric Hymns, Homeric epigrams, burlesque poems, and the later Contest of Homer and Hesiod.

BIBLIOGRAPHY

This unit describes the manuscript traditions, papyrus evidence, editions, and scholarship concerning Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and the Epic Cycle. It lists manuscript witnesses and family groupings for the Works and Days, Theogony, Shield of Heracles, and Homeric Hymns, with particular attention to classifications by Rudolf Rzach and T. W. Allen. It also identifies papyrus fragments of Hesiodic works, especially the Catalogues, and surveys ancient, early modern, and modern editions and translations. The unit concludes with references for studying Hesiod, the Hesiodic poems, Greek literature, and the Epic Cycle.

HESIOD / HESIOD’S WORKS AND DAYS

Hesiod addresses Perses about Zeus, justice, work, and the two forms of Strife, urging him to abandon violence and settle their inheritance dispute by right judgement. He recounts how Zeus hid the means of life, how Prometheus stole fire, and how Pandora was created as a punishment for mortals. He describes the successive races of mortal men, from the golden race to the present race of iron, and warns that Zeus punishes injustice. He concludes by instructing Perses about honest work, wealth, neighbors, reciprocity, and proper conduct.

The unit gives Perses a calendar of agricultural labor, advising him when to plough, sow, harvest, cut timber, prune vines, and store produce. It instructs him to prepare a house, ploughs, oxen, slaves, tools, clothing, fodder, and provisions before each season. The speaker instructs Perses about seasonal sailing, farming, trade, household conduct, religious purity, and auspicious days. He warns that autumn storms make the sea dangerous, recommends sailing fifty days after the solstice or cautiously in spring, and advises limiting cargo to avoid disaster. He recounts that their father sailed from Aeolian Cyme to escape poverty and settled near Helicon, while the speaker claims little sailing experience but authority from the Muses and Zeus. The unit concludes with rules for marriage, friendship, ritual behavior, bodily cleanliness, and the proper days for different kinds of work.

THE DIVINATION BY BIRDS

The unit concerns the work titled The Divination by Birds and its placement relative to Works and Days. Proclus states that some people place The Divination by Birds after verse 828 of Works and Days. Apollonius of Rhodes rejects The Divination by Birds as spurious.

THE ASTRONOMY

The Astronomy, attributed to Hesiod, discusses the Pleiades and Hyades and gives their names and star-related traditions. It recounts how Callisto became the Great Bear and how her son Arcas became Bootes. It also tells of Orion, his parentage, adventures, death by a scorpion, and placement among the stars, along with the scorpion. Hesiod further says that Orion piled up the promontory by Peloris, founded a close of Poseidon, settled in Euboea, and was honored among the stars.

THE PRECEPTS OF CHIRON

The Precepts of Chiron contains advice to offer sacrifices to the eternal gods upon returning home and to hear both sides before deciding a suit. It also preserves a sequence comparing the lifespans of crows, stags, ravens, phoenixes, and the rich-haired Nymphs. Quintilian reports that earlier writers attributed the work to Hesiod and that Aristophanes was the first to reject it as Hesiod's work. The unit presents no further information about Chiron beyond the work's title.

THE GREAT WORKS

The Great Works is cited in a commentary on Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics for a verse about the slaying of Rhadamanthys. The verse states that a person who sows evil will reap an increased evil, and that receiving treatment matching one’s own actions constitutes true justice. Proclus reports that some people attribute the Silver Race to the earth because Hesiod identifies silver as belonging to the family of Earth in the Great Works.

THE IDAEAN DACTYLS

Hesiod says that the Idaean Dactyls taught the smelting and tempering of iron in Crete. Clement reports that Celmis and Damnameneus, identified as the first Idaean Dactyls, discovered iron in Cyprus. Clement also says that Delas discovered bronze smelting, while Hesiod calls him Scythes.

THE THEOGONY

The Muses of Helicon inspire Hesiod to sing of the gods and their origins, declaring that they can speak both falsehoods and truths. The divine genealogy begins with Chaos, Earth, Tartarus, and Eros, then recounts the births of Heaven, the Titans, the Cyclopes, and the Hundred-Handed Ones. Earth helps Cronos overthrow Heaven with a sickle, after which many divine beings and monsters are born from the gods. The unit also describes the Muses, Zeus's rise to power, and Styx's alliance with him against the Titans.

(ll. 404-452) Again, Phoebe came to the desired embrace of Coeus.

Phoebe and Coeus beget Leto, Asteria, and Hecate. Zeus honours Hecate above all and preserves her original share in earth, heaven, and sea. Hecate grants favour, wealth, victory, glory, success in contests, prosperity in herds, and abundant catches at sea to those she chooses. Zeus also makes her a nurse of the young who see the light of Dawn.

(ll. 453-491) But Rhea was subject in love to Cronos and bare splendid

Rhea bears Zeus and, with the help of Earth and Heaven, conceals him in Crete while Cronos swallows a stone in his place and later regurgitates his children. Zeus frees the brothers of his father, receives the thunderbolt, defeats the Titans with the aid of Cottus, Gyes, and Briareos, and imprisons the Titans in Tartarus. Prometheus deceives Zeus at Mecone, steals fire for mortal men, and is punished, while Zeus has the Limping God create a maiden as an evil for humanity. The unit also describes the boundaries and inhabitants of Tartarus, including Night, Sleep, Death, Hades, Persephone, and Styx.

Earth bears Typhoeus, her youngest child, who has immense strength, a hundred snake-headed necks, fiery eyes and heads, and many terrifying voices. Zeus confronts Typhoeus with thunder, lightning, and his thunderbolt, burns his heads, and hurls him into Tartarus after defeating him. The battle shakes heaven, earth, the sea, Olympus, Hades, and Tartarus, while the earth is scorched and melted by the heat. Winds come from Typhoeus; some are beneficial, but others damage ships, kill sailors, and ruin fields.

The blessed gods ended their struggle with the Titans and pressed Olympian Zeus to rule, after which he distributed their dignities. Zeus swallowed Metis to prevent her prophesied son from challenging his rule, and later gave birth to Athene from his head. The unit recounts Zeus’s marriages and offspring, including Hera’s children, and the births and unions of many gods, heroes, and divine descendants. It ends with the Muses being asked to sing of the company of women.

THE CATALOGUES OF WOMEN AND EOIAE1701

This unit presents fragments attributed to Hesiod’s Catalogue of Women and related Catalogue genealogies, preserving genealogies, marriages, births, heroic exploits, and geographical traditions. It traces lines from Deucalion and Hellen, describes descendants of Zeus and Poseidon, and recounts stories involving Atalanta, Europa, Sarpedon, Phineus, Peleus, Thetis, and Asclepius. Several fragments concern the Argonauts, the Harpies, and distant peoples and places. The evidence comes from scholia, papyri, lexica, historians, and other later authors who quote or summarize Hesiod.

The unit preserves Hesiodic accounts of Helen’s suitors, Tyndareus’s oath, and the marriage of Helen and Menelaus, along with genealogies and episodes involving the gods and heroic families. Aphrodite punishes Tyndareus’s daughters for his neglect, while Zeus plans to destroy the demigods and separate gods from mortals. THE SHIELD OF HERACLES

Alcmena bears twin sons, Heracles by Zeus and Iphiclus by Amphitryon, after Zeus and Amphitryon separately share her bed in one night. Heracles and Iolaus prepare for battle against Cycnus and Ares, while Athena instructs Heracles to kill Cycnus and wound Ares without taking their horses or armor. Hephaestus’s elaborate Shield of Heracles depicts war, divine figures, death, cities, agriculture, contests, and Ocean surrounding the rim. Heracles and Cycnus finally meet in battle, but the unit ends before the fight’s outcome is given.

(ll. 402-412) As two lions 1806 on either side of a slain deer spring

Heracles kills Cycnus by striking him in the unguarded neck with a spear. Ares attacks Heracles in grief for Cycnus, but Athene diverts Ares’ spear and Heracles wounds Ares in the thigh. Ares is carried away to Olympus, while Heracles and Iolaus strip Cycnus’ armour and go to Trachis. Ceyx buries Cycnus, but Anaurus later washes away the grave and memorial at Apollo’s command.

THE MARRIAGE OF CEYX

The Marriage of Ceyx is attributed to Hesiod, though some grammar-school boys disputed its authorship. It concerns Heracles, who landed from the Argo to seek water and was left behind in Magnesia near Aphetae. Heracles is also shown as a regular visitor to Ceyx’s house at Trachis. The poem mentions horse-driving Ceyx, calls tables tripods, and describes wood being brought from the forest to be burned after a feast.

THE GREAT EOIAE

The fragments of the Great Eoiae preserve genealogies, heroic episodes, and accounts of divine and human figures. They concern figures including Heracles, Telamon, Endymion, Melampus, Phineus, and the descendants of Phrixus. Hesiod is cited as the poet of the Great Eoiae, which includes stories about Heracles and about Hermes and Battus. In the story of Hermes and Battus, Hermes steals cattle, tests Battus’s secrecy, and changes him into a rock when he reveals the theft.

THE MELAMPODIA

The Melampodia concerns the seers Calchas, Mopsus, and Teiresias, as well as the fate of Amphilochus. Calchas challenges Mopsus to count the figs on a wild fig-tree, but dies after Mopsus gives the correct answer. Teiresias is said to have lived through seven generations, changed sex after killing snakes, and judged the relative pleasure of men and women in intercourse. Hera blinds Teiresias for his answer, while Zeus grants him prophetic power.

AEGIMIUS

The fragments of the “Aegimius” concern Phrixus’s reception by Aeetes, Thetis’s testing of her children by Peleus, and the story of Io, Zeus, Hera, Argus, and Hermes. They also attribute accounts of Io and her transformation, the killing of Argus, and oaths concerning love to Hesiod or the author of the “Aegimius.” Another fragment describes a speaker’s place of refreshment, and the final fragment explains the name “Three-fold People” for the Pelasgi, Achaeans, and Dorians who settled in Crete.

FRAGMENTS OF UNKNOWN POSITION

The unit preserves 23 fragments quoted by ancient authors and commentators. Linus is described as the lovely son of Urania, mourned and invoked by singers and harpers at feasts and dances. Other fragments concern Apollo, gods, Muses, mythic peoples, customs, conduct, and proverbial sayings. Hesiod is called a servant of the sweet Muses, and it is said that those whom the immortals honor also receive good report from mortals.

DOUBTFUL FRAGMENTS

This unit presents ten doubtful fragments attributed or reported by Galen, a Homeric scholiast, a Pindaric scholiast, Julian, Servius, Plato, Clement of Alexandria, Apollonius, Stephanus of Byzantium, and another Pindaric scholiast. The fragments concern Zeus taking sense from Athamas, the grinding of yellow grain, and a song addressed to Phoebus Apollo by Delos singers identified as the speaker and Homer. They also mention starvation, the Hesperides and their golden apples, gifts moving gods and princes, the sun’s bright light on the seventh day, water mixed with Ocean’s streams, the sons of Orchomenus, and Telemon’s slaying of Melanippe.

THE HOMERIC HYMNS / I. TO DIONYSUS 2501

The hymn addresses Dionysus and recounts conflicting claims about where he was born. It says that Zeus secretly gave birth to him far from humans, at a high, wooded Nysa near the streams of Aegyptus. Zeus ordains that mortals will sacrifice perfect hecatombs to Dionysus at his feasts every three years. The singers ask Dionysus to be favorable and say that no one who remembers him may call holy song to mind.

II. TO DEMETER

Demeter’s daughter Persephone is abducted by Hades with Zeus’s permission, and Demeter searches for her while mourning. Hecate and Helios reveal the abduction to Demeter, who withdraws from the gods and disguises herself as Doso in Eleusis. There she nurses Demophoon and attempts to make him immortal, but Metaneira discovers the act and interrupts it. Demeter then reveals herself, commands the people of Eleusis to build her temple and altar, and promises to teach her rites.

(ll. 301-320) Now when they had finished building and had drawn back

Demeter causes a devastating famine by hiding the seed because Persephone has been taken from her, threatening both mankind and the honors of the gods. Zeus sends Hermes to bring Persephone from Hades, but Persephone has eaten a pomegranate seed and must spend one-third of each year below the earth and two-thirds with Demeter and the gods. Demeter restores the earth’s fertility and teaches the Eleusinian Mysteries to Triptolemus, Diocles, Eumolpus, and Celeus. The unit presents the seasonal return of Persephone and the establishment of rites at Eleusis.

III. TO DELIAN APOLLO

The hymn celebrates Apollo’s birth on Delos, where Leto gives birth after traveling through many lands and securing the island’s promise to receive him. Apollo grows immediately into a powerful god who declares his love for the lyre and bow and his role in revealing Zeus’s will. Delos becomes honored through Apollo’s temple, sacrifices, and the gatherings of the Ionians, whose songs and dances praise the gods. The poet-narrator asks the maidens of Delos to identify the blind singer of Chios as the sweetest singer and promises to spread their renown.

TO PYTHIAN APOLLO—

Apollo searches through Greece for a place to establish an oracle and chooses Crisa beneath Parnassus after Telphusa deceives him about her spring. He builds a temple there, slays the dragoness that threatens men, and establishes Pytho as the site of his sanctuary and prophetic authority. Apollo then captures a ship of Cretans in the form of a dolphin and brings them to Crisa to serve as ministers of his temple. He instructs them to receive sacrifices, preserve righteousness, and proclaim the will of the gods.

IV. TO HERMES

Hermes, son of Zeus and Maia, is born on Cyllene and immediately shows his cunning by inventing the lyre and stealing fifty of Apollo's cattle from Pieria. He disguises the cattle's tracks, drives them to the Alpheus, sacrifices two of them, and returns unseen to his cradle. Apollo discovers the theft, follows the reversed tracks to Hermes, and quarrels with him over the cattle. Hermes and Apollo are then brought before Zeus and the immortals assembled on Olympus for judgment.

(ll. 327-329) Then Hermes and Apollo of the Silver Bow stood at the

Hermes is brought before Zeus and Apollo over the theft of Apollo’s cattle, but he cunningly denies the charge. Hermes reveals the lyre, and Apollo, delighted by its music, exchanges his whip and cattle-keeping for it. Apollo and Hermes then become friends, and Apollo gives Hermes a golden staff, limited access to divination, and authority over various animals and omens. Zeus confirms Hermes’ new honors and appoints him messenger to Hades.

V. TO APHRODITE

Aphrodite, who subdues gods, mortals, birds, and beasts through desire, is unable to bend Athene, Artemis, and Hestia. Zeus causes Aphrodite to desire Anchises, a mortal herdsman on Mount Ida, and she disguises herself as a mortal woman before sleeping with him. She reveals her divinity afterward, warns Anchises not to boast of their encounter, and tells him that their son Aeneas will be raised by mountain Nymphs and become a ruler among the Trojans. The hymn contrasts divine immortality with mortal vulnerability, especially through the examples of Ganymedes and Tithonus.

VI. TO APHRODITE

The text praises Aphrodite, whose dominion is the walled cities of sea-set Cyprus. The western wind carries her over the sea in soft foam, where the Hours welcome and dress her in heavenly garments and golden ornaments. The gods welcome Aphrodite and pray to lead her home as a wife because of her beauty. The speaker asks Aphrodite to grant victory in a contest and to order the song.

VII. TO DIONYSUS

Dionysus, son of Semele, is captured by Tyrsenian pirates while appearing as a young man on a shore. When the pirates try to bind him and sail away, vines, ivy, wine, a lion, and a bear appear aboard the ship. The pirates leap into the sea and are changed into dolphins, while Dionysus spares the helmsman and reveals his identity.

VIII. TO ARES

The hymn addresses Ares as a powerful, bronze-armored warrior, defender of Olympus, and father of warlike Victory. It asks him to help the speaker gain courage and strength while driving away cowardice and deceitful impulses. The speaker also asks Ares to restrain the fury that leads toward bloodshed and instead grant the boldness to follow the harmless laws of peace.

IX. TO ARTEMIS

The unit is a song to Artemis, the virgin goddess who delights in arrows and was fostered with Apollo. She waters her horses from the Meles and drives her all-golden chariot through Smyrna to vine-clad Claros. At Claros, Apollo waits for her, and the singer begins with Artemis before turning to another song.

X. TO APHRODITE

The unit concerns Aphrodite, called Cytherea, a goddess associated with Cyprus and Salamis. She gives kindly gifts to men, and smiles and brightness appear on her lovely face. The speaker greets her and asks her to grant a cheerful song, promising to remember her and another song.

XI. TO ATHENA

The unit is a hymn to Pallas Athene, guardian of the city. It describes her as dread and as loving, with Ares, deeds of war, the sack of cities, shouting, and battle. Athene saves the people as they go out to war and return. The speaker greets her and asks for good fortune and happiness.

XII. TO HERA

The unit sings of Hera, whom Rhea bore. Hera is the golden-throned queen of the immortals and is described as surpassing all in beauty. She is the sister and wife of Zeus. The blessed throughout high Olympus reverence and honour Hera as they do Zeus, who delights in thunder.

XIII. TO DEMETER

The unit begins a song about rich-haired Demeter and her daughter, lovely Persephone. Demeter is described as an awful goddess. The speaker asks the goddess to keep the city safe and govern the song.

XIV. TO THE MOTHER OF THE GODS

The speaker asks the clear-voiced Muse, daughter of mighty Zeus, to sing about the Mother of all gods and men. The Mother of the Gods is pleased by rattles, timbrels, flutes, wolves, lions, echoing hills, and wooded coombes. The speaker offers a song of greeting to the Mother of the Gods and to all goddesses.

XV. TO HERACLES THE LION-HEARTED

The hymn praises Heracles, son of Zeus and Alcmena, as the mightiest of men on earth. At Eurystheus's bidding, Heracles wandered over land and sea, performing many violent deeds and enduring many hardships. He now lives happily in snowy Olympus, where he is married to Hebe. The speaker asks Heracles to grant success and prosperity.

XVI. TO ASCLEPIUS

The unit concerns Asclepius, son of Apollo and healer of sicknesses. Coronis, daughter of King Phlegyas, bore Asclepius in the Dotian plain. Asclepius is described as a great joy to men and a soother of cruel pangs. The speaker offers praise and prayer to Asclepius.

XVII. TO THE DIOSCURI

The unit concerns Castor and Polydeuces, identified as the Tyndaridae. It says that they sprang from Olympian Zeus and were borne by stately Leda beneath the heights of Taygetus. Zeus had privately bent Leda to his will. The unit hails Castor and Polydeuces as children of Tyndareus and riders upon swift horses.

XVIII. TO HERMES

The unit praises Hermes, the Cyllenian god and Slayer of Argus, as lord of Cyllene and Arcadia and messenger of the deathless gods. Hermes was born to Maia, the daughter of Atlas, after she joined with Zeus. Maia was a shy goddess who avoided the blessed gods and lived in a shadowy cave, where Zeus visited her at night without the knowledge of gods or mortals. The song ends by greeting Hermes as a giver of grace, guide, and giver of good things.

XIX. TO PAN

The text praises Pan, the goat-footed, two-horned son of Hermes, as a lively shepherd-god who roams mountains, hunts, and plays his reed pipes with nymphs. It recounts how Hermes fathered Pan with the rich-tressed daughter of Dryops in Arcadia. After Pan’s nurse fled in fear, Hermes carried him to the immortal gods, who welcomed him, while Bacchie Dionysus especially rejoiced. The text ends by greeting Pan and seeking his favor through song.

XX. TO HEPHAESTUS

The text praises Hephaestus for his inventions and his role in teaching men glorious gifts. With bright-eyed Athene, Hephaestus taught men crafts that transformed them from cave-dwellers living like wild beasts. After learning these crafts, men lived peacefully in their own houses throughout the year. The speaker asks Hephaestus to grant success and prosperity.

XXI. TO APOLLO

The unit praises Apollo, also called Phoebus. A swan sings of him with a clear voice as it lands beside the river Peneus, and a sweet-tongued minstrel sings of him first and last while playing a high-pitched lyre. The speaker greets Apollo as lord and seeks his favor through the song.

XXII. TO POSEIDON

The unit is a hymn to Poseidon, who is described as the great god and mover of the earth and fruitless sea. He is lord of the deep, Helicon, and wide Aegae. The gods allotted him a twofold office as tamer of horses and saviour of ships. The hymn asks Poseidon to be kindly and help those who voyage in ships.

XXIII. TO THE SON OF CRONOS, MOST HIGH

The unit is a hymn to Zeus, the Son of Cronos, most high. Zeus is described as chiefest among the gods, greatest, all-seeing, lord of all, and the fulfiller. He whispers words of wisdom to Themis as she sits leaning toward him. The speaker asks Zeus to be gracious.

XXIV. TO HESTIA

The unit addresses Hestia, who tends the holy house of Apollo at goodly Pytho. Hestia is invited to come into the house with one mind with all-wise Zeus. She is also asked to bestow grace on the speaker's song.

XXV. TO THE MUSES AND APOLLO

The text begins with the Muses, Apollo, and Zeus. The Muses and Apollo are said to bring singers and lyre players into the world, while kings come from Zeus. A person loved by the Muses speaks sweetly, and the Muses and Apollo are asked to honor the speaker’s song.

XXVI. TO DIONYSUS

The hymn celebrates Dionysus, the ivy-crowned and loud-crying son of Zeus and Semele. The Nymphs receive, foster, and nurture him in the dells of Nysa, where he grows up in a sweet-smelling cave and is counted among the immortals. Afterward, Dionysus wanders through wooded valleys, followed by the Nymphs, while the forest resounds with their cries. The hymn asks Dionysus to grant that the singers may return rejoicing in future years.

XXVII. TO ARTEMIS

The hymn celebrates Artemis as a golden-armed huntress, archer, and pure maiden who delights in pursuing and destroying wild beasts. She hunts across shadowy hills, windy peaks, mountains, woods, and the sea, causing the natural world to tremble and echo. Afterward, she goes to the house of her brother Phoebus Apollo in the rich land of Delphi, where she hangs up her bow and leads the Muses and Graces in dance. The hymn hails Artemis and Apollo as children of Zeus and rich-haired Leto.

XXVIII. TO ATHENA

The hymn celebrates Pallas Athene, describing her as a bright-eyed, inventive, courageous virgin and saviour of cities. Wise Zeus gives birth to her from his awful head, and she emerges fully armed with a sharp spear. Her appearance causes Olympus to reel, the earth to cry out, and the sea to toss, while the Son of Hyperion stops his horses. Zeus is glad when Athene removes her heavenly armour, and the singer promises to remember her in another song.

XXIX. TO HESTIA

The unit praises Hestia as honored in the dwellings of deathless gods and earth-walking men. It says mortals hold no banquet without pouring sweet wine to Hestia first and last. Hermes is invoked as the slayer of Argus, son of Zeus and Maia, messenger of the blessed gods, bearer of the golden rod, and giver of good. Hestia and Hermes are asked to dwell together in the glorious house and aid the wisdom and strength of men.

XXX. TO EARTH THE MOTHER OF ALL

The hymn praises Earth as the mother of all and the eldest of all beings. Earth feeds every creature on land, in the sea, and in the air, and gives mortal men the means of life while also taking life away. Men whom Earth honors are blessed in their children and harvests, with abundant crops, livestock, wealth, and orderly cities. The hymn asks Earth, called the mother of the gods and wife of starry Heaven, to grant the singer heart-cheering substance.

XXXI. TO HELIOS

The hymn invokes the Muse Calliope to sing of Helios, the glowing son of Hyperion and Euryphaessa. Helios is described as tireless and godlike, riding a chariot drawn by stallions and shining upon mortals and immortals. He rests at heaven’s highest point after stopping his golden-yoked chariot, then drives his horses down through heaven to Ocean. The speaker greets Helios and asks him to grant heart-cheering substance.

XXXII. TO SELENE

The Muses are asked to tell of the long-winged Moon, whose radiant light embraces the earth and makes the air glow. The Moon’s light is brightest at mid-month when her orbit is full, making her a sure token and sign to mortal men. The Son of Cronos joined Selene in love, and she conceived and bore Pandia. Selene is praised as a white-armed, bright, mild goddess and queen.

XXXIII. TO THE DIOSCURI

The unit concerns the Tyndaridae, Castor and Polydeuces, whom it calls the glorious children of Leda and Zeus. They are described as deliverers of people on land and of ships caught in stormy seas. When shipmen call upon them, they appear on tawny wings, calm the winds and waves, and bring safety and relief. The song ends by greeting the Tyndaridae and promising to remember them in another song.

HOMER’S EPIGRAMS2601

The unit presents epigrams concerning hospitality, travel, divine favor, human fortune, and everyday life. Homer appears in a brief exchange with fishermen about what they caught and carried home. Zeus is invoked as father, ruler, protector of strangers, and source of fate and vengeance. Cyme is described as a beautiful high city near Sardene and the Hermus, and as the place from which a disappointed poet wishes to depart.

FRAGMENTS OF THE EPIC CYCLE / THE WAR OF THE TITANS

The Epic Cycle begins with the union of Heaven and Earth, who produce three hundred-handed sons and three Cyclopes. The War of the Titans attributes various traditions to Eumelus, or possibly Arctinus, including descriptions of a shield, Zeus dancing, and the guarded apples of the Hesperides. It also relates that Aegaeon was the son of Earth and Sea and an ally of the Titans. The fragments further connect Cronos with Philyra and Cheiron, and identify the War of the Titans as the earliest source for the story of Heracles sailing in a cauldron.

THE STORY OF OEDIPUS

The Story of Oedipus was an epic by Cinaethon consisting of six thousand six hundred verses. Pausanias says it clearly shows that Oedipus’s sons were born to Euryganeia rather than Iocasta. The story also says that the Sphinx killed Haemon, the son of Creon.

THE THEBAID

The Thebaid is said to have comprised seven thousand verses and to have begun with a song of parched Argos. It recounts how Polyneices presented Oedipus with treasures of Cadmus, prompting Oedipus to curse his sons and pray that they would never share their inheritance peacefully. It also describes Oedipus cursing his sons after they sent him a haunch, and it tells of Adrastus fleeing Thebes, the burial of seven dead, and the deaths of Parthenopaeus and others in battle.

THE EPIGONI

The Epigoni is attributed to Homer and was said to contain seven thousand verses, beginning with an invocation to the Muses about younger men. The Teumesian fox was sent by the gods to punish the descendants of Cadmus, and Cephalus hunted it with his hound after being purified by the Cadmeans for killing Procris. The fox and hound were turned into stones near Teumessus. The Epigoni sent Manto, daughter of Teiresias, to Delphi as a first offering of their spoil, and she later married Rhacius and came to Colophon.

THE CYPRIA

The Cypria recounts Zeus and Themis’s plan to bring about the Trojan war, beginning with the dispute among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite over their beauty and Alexandrus’s judgment in favor of Aphrodite. It describes Alexandrus’s abduction of Helen from Sparta, the preparations and early campaigns of the Greek expedition against Ilium, and the first battles and losses at Troy. The poem also relates events involving Castor and Polydeuces, Odysseus and Palamedes, the sacrifice of Iphigeneia, Achilles’s actions, and the deaths of Protesilaus and Palamedes. Its fragments additionally preserve traditions about the marriage of Peleus and Thetis, Helen’s birth, and various heroes and gods.

THE AETHIOPIS

The Aethiopis, attributed to Arctinus of Miletus, follows the Iliad and concerns the final events of the Trojan War. Penthesileia comes to aid the Trojans but is killed by Achilles, who is later purified after killing Thersites. Memnon kills Antilochus and is then killed by Achilles, while Achilles is subsequently killed by Paris and Apollo. The Achaeans recover and bury Achilles, and a dispute over his arms arises between Odysseus and Aias, who later kills himself.

THE LITTLE ILIAD

The Little Iliad, attributed to Lesches of Mitylene, recounts events after Achilles’ death leading to the fall of Troy. Odysseus wins Achilles’ arms through Athena’s contriving, captures Helenus, helps bring Philoctetes and Neoptolemus to the war, and steals the Palladium with Diomedes. Philoctetes kills Alexandrus, Neoptolemus kills Eurypylus, and Epeius builds the wooden horse, which the Trojans bring into Ilium after the Achaeans sail to Tenedos. The fragments also describe the night sack of Ilium, including the deaths of Priam and Astyanax and the enslavement of Andromache.

THE SACK OF ILIUM

The Sack of Ilium, attributed to Arctinus of Miletus, describes the Greek capture and destruction of Ilium through the wooden horse. The Trojans debate what to do with the horse, but dedicate it to Athena before Sinon signals the Achaeans, who enter the city and storm it. Priam, Polyxena, Astyanax, and others die, while Helen, Andromache, Aethra, and the spoils are taken by the Greeks. After burning Ilium, the Greeks sail away, and Athena plans to destroy them at sea.

THE RETURNS

The Returns by Agias of Troezen describes the Greek leaders’ journeys after the Sack of Ilium, including quarrels, storms, deaths, burials, and homecomings. Athena causes a quarrel between Agamemnon and Menelaus, while Achilles’ ghost warns Agamemnon and his followers of what will happen. Neoptolemus travels overland, meets Odysseus, buries Phoenix, and is recognized by Peleus. The poem also recounts Agamemnon’s murder by Aegisthus and Clytaemnestra, Orestes and Pylades’ vengeance, and Menelaus’s return home.

THE TELEGONY

The Telegony tells of Odysseus’s travels after the Odyssey, including his visit to Elis, sacrifices in Ithaca, marriage to Callidice in Thesprotis, and war against the Brygi. After Callidice’s death, Odysseus returns to Ithaca, where Telegonus arrives and ravages the island. Odysseus comes out to defend Ithaca but is unwittingly killed by Telegonus, who then takes his body, Penelope, and Telemachus to his mother’s island. Circe makes them immortal, after which Telegonus marries Penelope and Telemachus marries Circe.

HOMERICA / THE EXPEDITION OF AMPHIARAUS

Homer sat in a tanner’s yard and recited his poetry to those present. Among the works he recited was the Expedition of Amphiarus to Thebes. He also recited the Hymns to the Gods, which were composed by him.

THE TAKING OF OECHALIA

The Taking of Oechalia concerns Eurytus, his daughter Iole, and Heracles, who sacked Oechalia for Iole's sake. The poem is attributed either to Homer or to Creophylus of Samos, who is said to have hosted Homer. Creophylus says that Eurytus had two sons, differing from Hesiod's account of four. The poem also preserves an account in which Medea poisoned Creon in Corinth, left her sons at the altar of Hera Acraea, and fled to Athens.

THE PHOCAIS

The unit concerns Homer’s composition of the Lesser Iliad and the Phocais. Pseudo-Herodotus states that Homer composed both works while living with Thestorides. The Phocaeans claim that Homer composed the Phocais among them.

THE MARGITES

The unit concerns the Margites, which Suidas says was written by Pigres and attributed to Homer. It identifies Pigres as a Carian of Halicarnassus and brother of Artemisia, wife of Mausolus. The fragments describe Margites as a man who knew many things badly, lacked practical skills, and behaved foolishly toward his parents and wife. A proverb in the work contrasts the fox’s many tricks with the hedgehog’s single effective trick.

THE CERCOPES

The Cercopes were two brothers, Passalus and Acmon, who lived on earth and practised every kind of knavery. They roamed widely, deceiving people with their cunning and relentless tricks. Their mother warned them to keep away from Black-bottom, identified as Heracles, and they were later said to have been turned to stone for trying to deceive Zeus.

THE BATTLE OF FROGS AND MICE

The Battle of Frogs and Mice begins after Puff-jaw abandons Crumb-snatcher in the lake, causing the Mouse host to declare war on the Frogs. Both sides arm themselves with improvised weapons and armor, while Zeus and the gods watch rather than directly fight. Many Mice and Frogs are killed in the battle, and Slice-snatcher nearly destroys the Frogs before Zeus sends the crabs to rescue them. The crabs drive the Mice into flight, ending the one-day war at sunset.

OF THE ORIGIN OF HOMER AND HESIOD, AND OF THEIR CONTEST

The unit recounts competing traditions about the origins, parentage, careers, and deaths of Homer and Hesiod. It describes a poetic contest between them at Chalcis, where Homer excelled in answering riddles and questions but Hesiod won the prize because he praised peace and husbandry. It also recounts Hesiod’s murder after a misinterpreted oracle and Homer’s later travels, performances, death at Ios, and burial there.
