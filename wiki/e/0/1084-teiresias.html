Teiresias in The Bacchae of Euripides

Euripides · -0406/-0405

Teiresias is a blind aged Theban prophet who supports Dionysian worship, joins Cadmus, and warns Pentheus that resisting Dionysus is madness.

Narrative, section by section

The Bacchae

Teiresias comes dressed for Dionysian worship and joins Cadmus in defending the new god. He teaches Pentheus that Dionysus brings relief from grief, prophecy, courage, and divine joy, but warns that Pentheus's resistance is madness.

Teiresias in The Odyssey

Homer · -0632~/-0599~

Teiresias is a blind Theban prophet whose reason remains unshaken after death. Carrying a golden sceptre, he drinks blood before advising Ulysses. He explains the voyage home: suffering and Neptune’s danger, the need to spare the Sun’s flocks, the suitors’ slaughter, a later oar-bearing journey, and peaceful old-age death.

Teiresias in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Teiresias, a famous seer, lives through seven generations from Cadmus to Eteocles and Polyneices, though some say nine, by Zeus’s ordinance. After seeing mating snakes on Cithaeron, killing the female changes him into a woman; killing the male restores him. His judgment about sexual pleasure brings Hera’s blindness and Zeus’s prophetic power.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

THE MELAMPODIA

Teiresias is said to have lived through seven generations, from the time of Cadmus to the time of Eteocles and Polyneices, though others say nine. After killing a female snake and then a male snake, he changed from a man to a woman and back again. He judged that a man enjoys one part of sexual pleasure while a woman enjoys all ten parts, and he was blinded by Hera but given prophetic power by Zeus.

Teiresias in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Teiresias is a mortal, blind prophet and seer of Thebes, guided by a boy yet possessing prophetic knowledge. Renowned and previously consulted about Laius’s murder, he serves Loxias alone, not Creon. Brought before Oedipus to identify Laius’s murderer, he initially refuses to speak, then accuses Oedipus and foretells that Oedipus will discover his connection to Laius’s murder and his own family, lose his sight, and go into exile. His claims perplex the Chorus, though it calls his prophecy reliable, while Creon and Oedipus question his motives and knowledge. Later, he warns Creon that the gods reject the city because Polyneices is unburied. When Creon charges him with seeking profit, Teiresias foretells that Creon will lose his own child and that his halls will fill with lamentation.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

OEDIPUS THE KING

Teiresias is brought before Oedipus as a prophet who might reveal Laius’s murderer. After initially refusing to speak, he tells Oedipus that he is the murderer and that he lives unknowingly in shame with his nearest kin.

CHORUS.

Teiresias claims the right to speak freely and says that he serves Loxias rather than Creon. He tells Oedipus that he is unknowingly hostile to his own kin, then prophesies his loss of sight, exile, and discovery of his connection to Laius's murder and his family.

CHORUS.

The master seer’s words perplex the Chorus, which does not know whether they are true or false. The Chorus questions whether a mortal seer has ever proved that he knows more than others or can accuse someone without assured signs.

CREON.

The seer had a high reputation and had previously been consulted about Laius’s murder, but he did not identify Oedipus at that time. Oedipus claims that Creon prompted the seer to accuse him of killing Laius.
