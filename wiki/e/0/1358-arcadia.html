Arcadia

place · 5 works · 14 quoted facts · 23 connections
Also called: Arcadian, Arcadians, Arcady, land of many springs and mother of flocks, the country, the kingdom of Arcadia

Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Arcadia is a land of many springs and flocks containing Hermes’s sacred place, where he tended sheep and fathered Pan. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Arcady is the region of the Iamid clan’s hereditary priestly functions and is portrayed as the nurse of goodly men through Stymphalos and Arcadian royal ancestry. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Arcadia is invaded by Lacedaemonian forces, serves as a base for allied forces invading Laconia, and receives them after their ravaging of the country. In Description of Greece, Arcadia is a division of the Peloponnese inhabited by Arcadians, later traversed by Dorians returning to the Peloponnese, with boundaries that shifted from Pisa to Elis. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Arcadia is an inland division of the Peloponnese, its people and places linked by traditions, migrations, wars, settlements, and religious accounts.

Arcadia in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Arcadia first appears

Abstract written by a model

Arcadia is an inland, interior division of the Peloponnese, entirely shut off from the sea. Near Argolis it is inhabited by Tegea, Mantinea, and other Arcadians, and it is entered through passes toward Tegea and Mantinea. Roads connect Mantinea with other parts and lead into the interior; the region contains towns, rivers, temples, ruins, Melangea, Pallantium, Heræa, and land near Schœnus. Its people and places are linked by traditions: Pelasgus first settled the land, which was called Pelasgia during his reign and before Arcas; in the third generation after Pelasgus it increased in population and cities, many founded by Lycaon’s sons. Under Arcas it became Arcadia. After Clitor died childless, the kingdom passed to Æpytus, son of Elatus, and later to Aleus. Arcadians went to Troy in transports provided by Agamemnon, fought the Lacedæmonians with each town under its own commander, and later crowded into Megalopolis for strength against them, despite resistance from several towns and the departure of Trapezus’ people from the Peloponnese. Pallantium sent Evander and Arcadians to establish a colony near the river Tiber. Arcadia also figures in accounts of Pan, Leto, and a common altar maintained by Tegea for all Arcadians.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

12 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK VIII.--ARCADIA. / CHAPTER I.

Arcadia is an inland region of the Peloponnese, inhabited near Argolis by the people of Tegea and Mantinea along with other Arcadians. It is entirely shut off from the sea, so Homer describes the Arcadians as going to Troy in transports provided by Agamemnon. The Arcadians say that the region was first settled by Pelasgus and was called Pelasgia during his reign.

CHAPTER III.

Arcadia advanced in population and cities in the third generation after Pelasgus. The sons of Lycaon founded many cities in Arcadia.

CHAPTER IV.

During Arcas’s reign, the country was called Arcadia instead of Pelasgia. After Clitor died without children, its kingdom devolved upon Æpytus and later passed to Aleus.

CHAPTER VI.

Arcadia was the land from which the Arcadians came, and it could be entered from Argolis through passes leading toward Tegea and Mantinea. The region included Melangea, which supplied Mantinea with drinking water.

CHAPTER X.

Arcadia has roads leading from Mantinea to its other parts. In the battle against the Lacedæmonians, the Arcadians held the left wing, with each town under its own commander.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Arcadia is the region to which the inhabitants of Caphyæ say they fled after being expelled from Athens. Its dialect is said to account for the form of the name Caphyæ.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Arcadia is associated with Heræa, whose founder was the son of Lycaon, and Pan is said to have been a native of Arcadia. According to the Arcadian account, the Erymanthus is the boundary between Heræa and Elis.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The Arcadians crowded into Megalopolis to strengthen themselves against the Lacedæmonians, following the example of Argos. Most Arcadian towns accepted the plan, though the people of Lycæatæ, Tricolonus, Lycosura, and Trapezus resisted, with some being forced into Megalopolis and the people of Trapezus leaving the Peloponnese.

CHAPTER XXXV.

The roads described lead into the interior of Arcadia. The text identifies the land near Schœnus and the unnamed place beyond it as Arcadian soil.

CHAPTER XLIII.

Arcadia was the region containing Pallantium, from which Evander and a force of Arcadians departed to establish a colony near the river Tiber.

CHAPTER XLIV.

Arcadia is described through roads, towns, rivers, temples, and ancient ruins, including places connected with Megalopolis, Pallantium, Tegea, and Asea.

CHAPTER LIII.

Arcadia is the region through which Leto wandered while pregnant, according to the tradition that Apollo and Artemis punished those who neglected her. Tegea’s people also maintain an altar called common to all Arcadians.
