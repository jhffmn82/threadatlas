  • In The Aeneid, it is the Trojan fortification defended by soldiers who cast down rocks, shoot through loopholes, and throw javelins; a tower set on fire by Turnus collapses and buries many Trojans.
  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, it is among Troy’s walls and towers that burn, collapse with a great crash, and disappear into smoke and darkness as the city is destroyed.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, it is the city’s defenses under siege by the Greek fleet, which Rhesus promises to overthrow while Hector discusses where he may position his forces.
  • In The Iliad, it is the broad defensive wall of Ilius, built by Phoebus Apollo and Neptune for Laomedon and later defended by Apollo as fleeing Trojans approach it.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, it is the walls Apollo and Neptune build for Laomedon in exchange for gold, which Hercules later captures after Laomedon refuses the promised rewards.
  • In The Fall of Troy, it consists of the fortified walls and gates defended by the Trojans against repeated Argive assaults, with Aeneas fighting from them and driving the attackers back.

the city wall of Ilius in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

The city wall of Ilius is a broad, fair defensive wall, built with much labour by Phoebus Apollo and Neptune for Laomedon, intended to be impregnable. Hector calls it weak and worthless and says it will not stop his fury; later fleeing Trojans approach it as Apollo defends it.
