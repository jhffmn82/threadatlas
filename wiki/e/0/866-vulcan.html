Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, Vulcan is a lame god of fire and a divine craftsman who forges armor for Achilles and burns the plain and Xanthus at Juno’s command. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Vulcan is a great god associated with fire who preserves its use, works metals, and is said by some priests to become the first king. In The Aeneid, Vulcan is the fire god and Venus’s husband who commands the Cyclopes to forge armor and a shield for Aeneas. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Vulcan is Juno’s son and Venus’s husband, a divine blacksmith who creates Phaëton’s chariot and traps Venus and Mars in invisible chains. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Vulcan, or Hephaistos, is Jupiter and Juno’s lame celestial artist son who forges Jupiter’s thunderbolts and gives gifts to Harmonia and Orion.

Vulcan in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Vulcan first appears

Abstract written by a model

Vulcan is a lame god of fire, a cunning divine workman of heaven and Olympus, and Juno’s son. He has a house with golden handmaids, is Charis’s husband, and was cared for by Thetis and Eurynome for nine years after his fall. His work includes Diomed’s cuirass, tripods, a golden seat for Sleep, and Achilles’ armor. When Thetis asks, he remembers her care and forges Achilles a shield, breastplate, helmet, and greaves. Later, obeying Juno, he burns the plain and Xanthus until Juno orders him to stop.

BOOK XVIII.

Thetis goes to Olympus to ask Vulcan, the cunning workman, to make a splendid suit of armour for Achilles.

Vulcan welcomes Thetis and agrees to make armor for Achilles, remembering that Thetis and Eurynome cared for him for nine years after his fall. He forges a great shield, breastplate, helmet, and greaves, then gives the completed armor to Thetis.

BOOK XXI.

Vulcan answers Juno's command by kindling a fierce fire that dries the plain, burns the dead and the vegetation beside Xanthus, and heats the river until it can no longer flow. He stops only when Juno tells him to quench the flames after Xanthus submits.

Vulcan in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Vulcan first appears

Abstract written by a model

Vulcan is an ennobled deity, described as Juno’s son or the only son of Jupiter and Juno, and Venus’s husband. Precipitated from heaven for deformity, he falls on Lemnos, where he works as a blacksmith and divine craftsman, creating Phaëton’s chariot. Told by the Sun of Venus’s affair with Mars, he fashions nearly invisible brass chains and nets, traps the lovers in bed, and exposes them to assembled gods.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Vulcan, the son of Juno and husband of Venus, is informed by the Sun of Venus’s affair with Mars. He makes nearly invisible brass chains and nets, traps the lovers together in bed, and exposes them to the assembled gods.

notes .. notes

Vulcan is called the son of Juno, either because Juno alone was said to be his mother or because he was said to be the only son of Jupiter and Juno. In Palæphatus’s historical explanation, Helius is identified as the son of Vulcan and king of Egypt.
