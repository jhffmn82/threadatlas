the Fates in Four Plays of Aeschylus

Aeschylus · -0471

The Fates are triform powers governing destiny: they ordain Prometheus’s inescapable punishment, govern Xerxes, and through divine intervention cause Persia’s defeat. They bring overwhelming disaster and ruin, fulfilling an oracle and divine presage as Xerxes advances toward fate.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Enter a MESSENGER.

Fate is described as bringing an overwhelming disaster upon Persia. The MESSENGER says that a fiend or fate intervened against the Persians, and the CHORUS calls the force of fate too heavy for Persia to withstand.

The GHOST OF DARIUS rises.

Fate is presented as bringing ruin upon Xerxes and Persia, fulfilling an oracle and a divine presage of war. Darius says that when a king advances toward his fate, a god may lure him onward to destruction.

Fates in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Fates are powers governing life, lifespan, age, fortune, and even Jupiter. At Meleager’s birth, they bind his life to a burning billet, making its preservation essential. They later determine changes of youth and age, including Iolaüs’s restoration, and are invoked as allotting mortal years; their authority also limits Eurydice’s possible return.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLE IV. [VIII.260-546]

At Meleager’s birth, the three Fates place a billet in the flames and declare that the newborn and the wood will share the same span of life. Their pronouncement makes the billet’s survival essential to Meleager’s life.

FABLE V. [IX.426-665]

The Fates determine the changes in age and fortune described in this unit, including Iolaüs's return to youth and the aging of Rhadamanthus, Æacus, and Minos. Jupiter says that the Fates govern him as well.

BOOK THE TENTH.

The Fates are invoked by Orpheus as the powers that determine the thread and allotted years of mortal life. Their authority is acknowledged in the condition governing Eurydice's return.

Fates in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Fates are twin divine powers, one dark and one bright, who ruthlessly control death and determine destiny for mortals, warriors, and cities. Unyielding even toward the gods, they appoint deaths that no prayer can avert. They doom Penthesileia and Achilles, appear beside Achilles and Memnon with shadow over Memnon and radiance around Achilles, and give Trojans courage while decreeing widespread descent to Hades. As battle continues, they mock hope, decide outcomes such as Eurypylus’ death and Teucer’s failed arrow, and accompany destruction, fear, and slaughter with indifference to individual and civic ruin.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK II

The Fates are described as relentless, even toward the gods. Zeus declares that no prayer can prevent the deaths appointed for the coming battle.

Then to the side of Aeacus' mighty son

The twin Fates appear beside Achilles and Memnon while they fight. One dark Fate casts her shadow over Memnon, while the bright Fate's radiance surrounds Achilles.

BOOK III

The Fates fill the Trojans with courage while also determining that many will descend to Hades. They have decreed that Achilles will die on the same day as many of the warriors he kills.

BOOK VIII

The Fates mock the hopes of Telephus' son before the battle. They determine the deaths and outcomes of the warriors, including Eurypylus' death and the failure of Teucer's arrow to strike Agenor.

BOOK XI

The Fates accompany the battle with destruction, fear, and slaughter. The text presents them as determining long destinies and as indifferent to the destruction of individuals and cities.
