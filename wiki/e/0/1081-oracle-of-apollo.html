Apollo’s oracle in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · about 699 BC · the work’s page · read it where Apollo’s oracle first appears

Abstract written by a model

Apollo’s oracle is a place of prophecy and divine counsel for people who question Apollo. Delos first seeks Apollo’s promised temple and oracle, but the oracle is established at Crisa, where Apollo offers unfailing counsel to visitors from many lands. Its responses guide Homer toward Ios and warn Hesiod of death at Nemean Zeus’s grove, though Hesiod misunderstands the warning.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

III. TO DELIAN APOLLO

Delos asks Leto to swear that Apollo will first build there a glorious temple to serve as an oracle for men. Leto swears that Apollo will have his altar and precinct on the island, which he will honor above all.

TO PYTHIAN APOLLO—

Apollo’s oracle is established at Crisa to answer people who come from many lands seeking divine counsel. Apollo promises to deliver counsel that cannot fail from his rich temple.

OF THE ORIGIN OF HOMER AND HESIOD, AND OF THEIR CONTEST

The oracle associated Homer with Ithaca, Telemachus, and Epicasta, and another response directed him toward Ios while warning him about the riddle of young children. It also foretold Hesiod’s fame and warned him of death at the grove of Nemean Zeus; Hesiod misunderstood the warning and went to Oenoe in Locris.

oracle of Delphos in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where oracle of Delphos first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Oracle at Delphos was an institution claimed by the Phocians under an ancestral right of power and protection. Its control became the central issue of the Sacred War, with Boeotians, Locrians, and others defending it. Apollo's temple held sacred treasures, first avoided and later seized by Philomelus to fund the Phocian war; the Amphictyons called this sacrilege. The oracle interpreted a spider's-web omen for Boeotia and its neighbors, and later gave Philip an ambiguous answer whose outcome signified his death.

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. VI.

The Oracle at Delphos was claimed by the Phocians as an institution formerly under their ancestors' power and protection. Its possession became the central issue of the Sacred War, and the Boeotians, Locrians, and others took up arms to defend it.

CHAP. VII.

Apollo's temple contained the sacred treasures that Philomelus initially avoided using but later rifled to finance the Phocian war. The Amphictyons treated the seizure of its treasures as sacrilege.

The oracle was robbed of its sacred treasures by the sacrilegious forces. Those who participated in or supported the robbery were described as receiving punishment, while Philip prospered after defending it.

CHAP, XV.

The Delphic oracle answered Philip's question about victory over the Persian king with the words, "The ox is crowned when's end is near at hand. To offer him a man doth ready stand." Philip construed the ambiguous answer to his own advantage, but its outcome was presented as signifying his own death.

CHAPTER V.

The oracle of Apollo at Delphi has several reported origins and successions. Phemonoe is regarded as its first priestess and the first to recite its oracles in hexameters, while other traditions associate its earliest prophetic figures with Daphnis, Olen, shepherds, and others.

CHAPTER XIV.

The oracle of Apollo rejected Themistocles’ proposed dedication of Persian spoils. Its response ordered that the spoils not enter the temple and suggested that they be sent home immediately.

temple of Apollo in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where temple of Apollo first appears

Abstract written by a model

A temple of Apollo is a sacred place associated with Patrae and Delphi. At Patrae it stands in the market-place near a naked bronze Apollo and the tomb of Patreus. At Delphi it was plundered before Philip ended the war, after which the Phocians lost participation. Its vestibule bears sayings, and its contents include Homer, Poseidon, the Fates, Pindar’s chair, an altar linked to Neoptolemus, and gold Apollo statues.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XX.

A temple of Apollo stands in the market-place of Patræ. Near it are a naked bronze statue of Apollo and the tomb of Patreus.

CHAPTER III.

The temple at Delphi had been plundered before Philip ended the war. The Phocians were thereafter deprived of participation in it, and their seizure of the temple was cited as part of their guilt.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The temple at Delphi has a vestibule bearing the sayings “Know thyself” and “Not too much of anything.” It contains a brazen statue of Homer, an oracle concerning Homer, an altar of Poseidon, statues of two Fates, an altar where the priest of Apollo slew Neoptolemus the son of Achilles, the iron Chair of Pindar, and another gold statue of Apollo.
