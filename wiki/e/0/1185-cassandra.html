Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Cassandra is being dragged away by Aias the son of Ileus, who tears away the image of Athena with her. In The House of Atreus, Cassandra is a captured Trojan prophetess given to Agamemnon as a slave and prize, who foresees the curse of Atreus’s house, Agamemnon’s murder, and her own death before being killed inside the palace. (The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies) In Hecuba and other plays, Cassandra is Hecuba’s daughter and Apollo’s prophetess, who foretells Troy’s fall, Ulysses’ perilous return, Agamemnon’s death, the fall of Atreus’s house, and her own death before becoming Agamemnon’s captive and leaving with Talthybius. In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Cassandra is Hecuba’s daughter and Apollo’s prophetess, forced to become Agamemnon’s captive bride, who foretells his death, the destruction of his house, and Odysseus’s difficult return before leaving for the Greek fleet. In The Aeneid, Cassandra is a royal Trojan prophetess loved by Coroebus, dragged from Minerva’s shrine while Coroebus’s rescue attempt leads to the mistaken attack on his followers. In The Library, Cassandra is Priam and Hecuba’s daughter, made a prophetess by Apollo and doomed to have her prophecies disbelieved; she warns against the Wooden Horse, is violated by Ajax the Locrian, and is awarded to Agamemnon. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Cassandra is shown seated on the ground and holding fast to Athena’s wooden statue after tearing it from its base when Ajax dragged her from the altar. In The Fall of Troy, Cassandra is Priam’s prophetess daughter, who warns against the Argive Horse and attacks it with a blazing brand and halberd before becoming Agamemnon’s captive prize after Troy’s ruin.

and 1 more work

In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Cassandra is Priam’s daughter, granted prophecy by Apollo but condemned to disbelief, and carried captive to Greece after Troy falls.

Cassandra in The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies

Aeschylus · 457 BC · the work’s page · read it where Cassandra first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassandra is a Trojan prophetess and seeress captured in war and given to Agamemnon as a slave and prize. Apollo granted her prophecy and once loved her, but after she rejected him he made her warnings disbelieved. Brought to the house of Atreus, she foresees its curse, Agamemnon’s murder, and her own imminent death inside the palace. Though she understands she cannot escape, she enters and is killed with Agamemnon, later lying beside him dead. Her death is followed by matricidal vengeance.

The stranger maid is a captive brought back as the prize and flower of the army's victory and given to Agamemnon. Agamemnon orders that she be led inside gently.

[_Exeunt all but Cassandra and the Chorus._

Cassandra prophesies the curse on the house of Atreus, the murder of Agamemnon, and her own death. She explains that Apollo gave her prophetic power but caused her prophecies to be disbelieved after she rejected him; she then enters the palace, accepting that she cannot escape her fate.

[_The scene opens, disclosing Clytemnestra, who comes forward. The body

Cassandra lies dead beside Agamemnon. Clytemnestra describes her as Agamemnon’s captive prize, seeress, and paramour, and says that she died singing like a swan.

Cassandra in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Cassandra first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassandra, Hecuba’s daughter, is Apollo’s inspired prophetess and priestess. She warns about Paris, foretells Troy’s fall and Ulysses’ perilous return, then becomes Agamemnon’s captive, concubine, and envisioned bride. Carrying a bridal torch, she predicts Agamemnon’s death, the fall of Atreus’ house, and her own death before leaving with Talthybius.

Cassandra in The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · 414 BC · the work’s page · read it where Cassandra first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassandra, Hecuba's daughter, is Apollo's virgin prophetess and priestess, inspired through divine possession. Allotted and forced as captive bride to Agamemnon, she enters carrying a torch and celebrates the marriage in prophetic frenzy. She foretells Agamemnon's death, the destruction of his house, and Odysseus's difficult return, then leaves for the Greek fleet, doomed to die beside Achilles' tomb.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

THE TROJAN WOMEN

Cassandra is described as Hecuba's daughter and a prophetess whom Apollo spared to remain a virgin and a chosen spirit. She has been allotted to Agamemnon, and Hecuba asks the women not to awaken her lest the enemy mock her dreaming.

[TALTHYBIUS, _followed by some Soldiers, enters from the left_.

Cassandra enters as a priestess carrying a torch and celebrates her supposed marriage while speaking in prophetic frenzy. She declares that she will kill Agamemnon and destroy his house, foresees Odysseus's long suffering, and leaves to join the Greek fleet.

Cassandra in The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Cassandra first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassandra, daughter of Priam and Hecuba, becomes a prophetess when Apollo teaches her prophecy in exchange for favours. After she refuses him, he leaves her prophecies unable to persuade others. She correctly warns the Trojans that the Wooden Horse contains armed forces, but they ignore her. Ajax the Locrian violates her at Athena’s image, and Agamemnon later receives her as an award.

Cassandra in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where Cassandra first appears

Abstract written by a model

Cassandra is Priam's loveliest daughter, a clear-sighted prophetess whose true warnings are never believed. Violated in Athena's temple, she understands Troy's portents and warns against the Argive Horse, attacking it with a blazing brand and halberd before Trojans disarm and drive her away. After Troy's ruin, she becomes Agamemnon's captive prize, bitterly laughing as others recall her prophecy.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

But then a yet more hideous punishment

Cassandra alone understands the meaning of the portents and loudly warns that Troy is doomed. She rushes toward the Argive Horse with a burning brand and a halberd, but the Trojans seize them from her and drive her back, after which she flees in anguish.

BOOK XIV.

Cassandra is taken as Agamemnon's prize. The captive Trojan women remember her prophecy of doom, while she laughs bitterly at the ruin of her land.
