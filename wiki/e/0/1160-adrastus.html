Adrastus

person · 6 works · 31 quoted facts · 15 connections
Also called: Adraitus, Adrastus and the Argives, O Adrastus, Son of Talaus, king, king Adrastus, the Son of Talaus, the son of Talaus, ᾿Αδράστου, ᾿Αδράστῳ, “Adpactos

Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Adrastus flees Thebes in miserable garments with Areion and laments his dead host after the seven receive last rites, praising him as seer and spearman. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Adrastus is king of Argos, father-in-law to Polynices and Tydeus, interpreter of Apollo’s oracle, arranger of their marriages, guarantor of their restoration, and leader of Argive support against Thebes. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Adrastus is king of Argos, receives Polynices and Tydeus, marries his daughters to them by oracle, prepares war against Thebes, and survives after the other captains perish. In The Library, Adrastus is a son of Talaus and Lysimache, king of Argos, father of five children, mediator of Polynices and Tydeus, husband-giver to them, leader of the Seven against Thebes, victor at Nemea, sole surviving Argive, and seeker of burial for the dead. In Description of Greece, Adrastus flees Argos, gains power at Sicyon after Polybus’s death, returns to become king of Argos, builds a temple and altars, is honored at Megara, and dies there of old age and grief for Ægialeus. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Adrastus is foremost among the Danai in driving praised horses, gathers Arcadian and Messenian allies for Argos, and alone survives the army’s destruction at Ismenius.

Adrastus in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Adrastus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Adrastus is a son of Talaus and Lysimache, a descendant of Bias and Pero, and king of Argos. With Amphithea he fathers Argia, Deipyle, Aegialia, Aegialeus, and Cyanippus. He stops Polynices and Tydeus fighting, marries his daughters Argia and Deipyle to them in response to a prophecy, and promises to restore them to their homelands. He gathers and leads the Seven Champions against Thebes, is stationed at the Homoloidian Gate, and wins at Nemea. Sole survivor of the Argives, he is saved by Arion, flees to Athens, and seeks burial for the Argive dead.

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Now Tyro, daughter of Salmoneus and Alcidice,

Adrastus was a son of Talaus and Lysimache, and therefore a descendant of Bias and Pero. With Amphithea, daughter of Pronax, he fathered Argia, Deipyle, Aegialia, Aegialeus, and Cyanippus.

VI. ᾿Ἑτεοκλῆς δὲ καὶ ἸΠολυνείκης περὶ τῆς

Adrastus, son of Talaus, rules Argos and separates Polynices and Tydeus when they fight at night. Recalling a prophecy about yoking his daughters to a boar and a lion, he marries Deipyle to Tydeus and Argia to Polynices, promising to restore both men to their homelands. He gathers the Seven Champions for the war against Thebes, wins the horse race at Nemea, and later leads the army.

VII. Κρέων δὲ τὴν Θηβαίων βασιλείαν παραλα-

Adrastus alone escaped from the first expedition against Thebes, saved by his horse Arion. He fled to Athens, sought refuge at the altar of Mercy, and asked that the Argive dead be buried.

Adrastus in Description of Greece

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Adrastus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Adrastus fled Argos, went to Polybus at Sicyon, and gained chief power there after Polybus died. He later returned to Argos and became its king. He built a temple beside Hera’s, with altars to Pan and the Sun God. Honored at Megara, he died there of old age and sorrow for Ægialeus.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

After fleeing Argos, Adrastus went to Polybus at Sicyon and obtained the chief power there after Polybus died. He later returned to Argos, after which Ianiscus became king of Sicyon.

CHAPTER XI.

Adrastus built the temple next to Hera’s temple, as well as altars to Pan and the Sun God behind Hera’s temple. No statues remained in the temple he built next to Hera.
