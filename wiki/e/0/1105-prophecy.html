  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, prophecy and divination involve inspired speech about the future, including Pythia’s coerced answer, an eagle omen, and Alexander’s rejection of Chaldean divination.
  • In The Argonautica, an oracle declares that the sons of Boreas will restrain the Harpies and reveals to Phineus that the Argonauts’ arrival will relieve him.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, prophecy and divination provide allegedly unreliable guidance: Theonoe’s prophetic knowledge identifies Menelaus’s arrival, describes the conflict over his return, and enables her to choose whether to expose or save him.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, divination is known fully and exclusively by Apollo, who teaches Hermes the method of the prophetic sisters while withholding Zeus’s counsel.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, prophecy is attributed to the Sibyls as a gift whose obscure utterances were treated as predictions, though St. Jerome called it chastity’s reward.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, oracles are chanted by the second Herophile at several sacred places and include predictions about Helen, Asia Minor, Europe, and the capture of Ilium.

prophecy and divination in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Prophecy and divination involve inspiration at Delphos and future speech; Pythia’s coerced answer and an eagle omen encouraged Philomelus, while Alexander contemned Chaldean divination after philosophers’ arguments.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAP. VI.

People near the chasm at Delphos were said to become inspired and speak about future events. Philomelus used Pythia's coerced answer and an omen involving an eagle and temple pigeons as encouragement for his campaign.

prophecy in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Prophecy is a gift attributed to the Sibyls, whose obscure utterances ancient people treated as predictions; some viewed them as benefactors, while St. Jerome called it chastity’s reward.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

FABLE III. [XIV.101-153]

The Sibyls' obscure utterances were treated by ancient people as predictions, and some ancient writers considered the Sibyls benefactors of mankind because of them. St. Jerome is also said to have regarded prophecy as a reward for the Sibyls' chastity.
