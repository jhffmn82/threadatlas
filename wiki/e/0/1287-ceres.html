Ceres in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Ceres is an ennobled, genial, fertile goddess of corn and agriculture, credited as the first to turn the earth with the crooked plough, give corn and wholesome food, and establish laws; agriculture and civilization are described as her gifts. Her worship is highly esteemed at Henna, and her mysteries are established at Eleusis. She is the mother of Proserpina, also called Proserpine and her only daughter. When her daughter disappears and Pluto carries her away, Ceres searches all lands, seas, and the whole world without rest, using pine torches lit at Ætna and drinking barley liquor when thirsty. She learns from Arethusa that her daughter is in the world of darkness, grieves and angers, damages Sicily's agriculture, and appeals to Jupiter. Jupiter's decision governs Proserpine's periods on earth and in the Infernal Regions; Ceres accepts it and rejoices at recovering her. Ceres also sends Triptolemus in her dragon-drawn chariot to scatter seed and cultivate the earth, having nursed him with her milk and purified him by fire. When Lyncus tries to kill him, she transforms Lyncus into a lynx.

Demeter in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Demeter is a worshipped deity associated with temples, precincts, statues, rites, mysteries, and sacred communities across several places. In the Argive account, she appears as a woman and kills Pyrrhus by throwing a tile; an oracle leads to a temple being erected where he dies. Near the Nine Springs, traditions connect her with learning of Proserpine’s rape and with teaching Triptolemus, Eubules, and others to sow corn. She repays Phytalus’s hospitality with a fig tree, is honored with Proserpine, Athene, and Poseidon in the grove of Lacius, and is represented in a temple once dedicated to Apollo. The Rheti are sacred to her, and rites with Proserpine are performed by Celeus’s daughters; after the rape, she is said to sit disguised as an old woman at the Well of Flowers. Taking compassion on Plemnæus, she rears his son, prompting a temple. She has further worship at Phlius, where mysteries are celebrated, and at Hermione as Chthonia, as well as at Lerna and Didymi.

Ceres in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Ceres, also called Demeter, is a goddess, daughter of Saturn and Rhea, mother of Proserpine, and presider over agriculture. When Proserpine is abducted, Ceres searches in grief and withholds the earth’s fertility until she learns what occurred. After Jupiter’s compromise gives Proserpine a partial return, Ceres restores agriculture and teaches Triptolemus ploughing and sowing, leading to the Eleusinian mysteries. She pities Psyche but can only advise submission to Venus. When Erisichthon destroys her sacred oak, Ceres answers the Dryads’ appeal by arranging for Famine to punish him, though she cannot approach Famine herself.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter I

Ceres, or Demeter, was the daughter of Saturn and Rhea and the mother of Proserpine. She presided over agriculture.

PROSERPINE

Ceres searches throughout the world for her abducted daughter, grieving for nine days and nights and later receiving Proserpine's girdle at the River Cyane. In anger she deprives the earth of fertility until Arethusa tells her what happened. After Jupiter's compromise restores Proserpine part of the year, Ceres restores the earth and teaches Triptolemus agriculture.

CUPID AND PSYCHE

Ceres finds Psyche sorting the disorderly grain in her temple and pities her. She cannot protect Psyche from Venus but advises her to surrender to Venus and seek forgiveness through modesty and submission.

Chapter XV

Ceres has a sacred grove containing a venerable oak. After Erisichthon destroys the oak, she agrees to the Dryads’ appeal and sends an Oread to command Famine to possess him.
