  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Atlas receives by lot the country bordering the ocean, becomes its namesake, studies astrology, discovers knowledge of the sphere, and assists Hercules by teaching him astrology.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, glorious Atlas begets the seven Pleiades and upholds wide Heaven at earth’s borders.
  • In The Library, Atlas has seven daughters by Pleione, who are called the Pleiades.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Atlas, son of Iapetus and king of his country, supports Heaven, owns the orchard of golden fruit, rejects Perseus, and is transformed into a mountain by the head of Medusa.
  • In The Library, Atlas bears the sky as a burden, is relieved by Hercules while obtaining three golden apples from the Hesperides, and is tricked into taking the sphere back.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Atlas is a giant king, a defeated Titan, father of the Hesperides, keeper of the golden gardens, and bearer of the heavens; he briefly lets Hercules carry them before resuming the burden.

Atlas in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Atlas receives by lot the country bordering the ocean, calls its people after himself, and is counted among the most renowned children of Coelus after Hyperion's death. An excellent astrologer and first discoverer of knowledge of the sphere, he studies astrology and possesses a wrought sphere of stars; these give rise to the belief that he carries the world on his shoulders. The greatest mountain bears his name. He has many sons, including Hesperus, and seven daughters, the Atlantides; another record calls Hesperus his brother and names Hesperis as his wife and the mother of the Atlantides and Hesperides. After Hercules returns abducted girls, Atlas assists him and teaches him astrology.

Atlas in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Atlas is a giant king of the western realm, a defeated Titan, father of the Hesperides, and keeper of golden gardens. A prophecy makes him deny Perseus hospitality; Perseus shows Medusa’s head and Atlas becomes a mountain. Condemned to bear the heavens, he briefly has Hercules carry them while fetching the apples, then resumes the burden, later feeling Hercules’ added weight among the stars.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

PERSEUS AND MEDUSA

Atlas rules the western realm and possesses golden gardens, but he refuses Perseus hospitality because of a prophecy that a son of Jove will take his golden apples. Perseus shows him Medusa's head, and Atlas becomes a mountain; the text explains that the prophecy actually concerned Hercules.

Chapter XII

Atlas was one of the Titans who warred against the gods and was condemned to bear the heavens on his shoulders. Hercules temporarily carried that burden while Atlas fetched the golden apples, then Atlas resumed his burden. Atlas was the father of the Hesperides, and he later felt the added weight when Hercules took his place among the stars.
