Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Medusa is the mortal Gorgon born to Ceto and Phorcys, from whose severed body Chrysaor and Pegasus spring. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Medusa is the fair-faced third Gorgon sister whose maiden and serpent heads lament after Perseus strikes her down and takes her head. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Medusa is a viperous Gorgon monster whose petrifying gaze turns beholders to stone, and whose head Perseus beheads, carries, and uses as a weapon. In The Library, Medusa is the only mortal Gorgon, beheaded by Perseus while she sleeps; Pegasus and Chrysaor spring from her body afterward. In Description of Greece, Medusa is portrayed either as Phorcus's daughter, ruler, hunter, and battle leader, or as a wild Libyan woman, and Perseus kills her by night. In Description of Greece, Medusa is identified with the Gorgon, whose monument and Perseus's tomb stood near the market-place of Argos. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Medusa is a mortal Gorgon whose serpent hair and petrifying gaze result from Minerva's transformation, until Perseus cuts off her head.

Medusa in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Medusa first appears

Abstract written by a model

Medusa is a viperous monster and Gorgon, daughter of Phorcys, described in some accounts as a ruler, one of three wealthy sisters, or a beautiful woman. She is deflowered by Poseidon in the Temple of Minerva and has serpents mingled with her hair. Her petrifying gaze turns beholders, men, and beasts to stone. Perseus approaches her through her reflection in a shield while she sleeps, seizes her by the hair, and beheads her. Her blood produces Pegasus and Chrysaor, and drops from her carried head produce serpents. Perseus carries and uses her head as a weapon and proof of her death; its face turns Polydectes to stone without blood.

CHAPTER XIX.

Medusa was said by one account to be the daughter of Phorcus, ruler near the Tritonian marsh, a hunter, and a leader in battle who was treacherously killed by Perseus. Another account treated her as a wild woman from Libya who mistreated the local inhabitants until Perseus slew her; her head was said to lie beneath a mound in Argos.
