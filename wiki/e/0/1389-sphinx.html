Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Sphinx kills Haemon, the son of Creon. In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Sphinx is the ravening fiend that once shamed Thebes, and her image appears on Parthenopaeus's shield carrying a Cadmean in her claws. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Sphinx is a hateful, destructive songstress who devastates Thebes by devouring failed riddle-solvers until Oedipus overcomes her. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Sphinx is a double-shaped monster who poses a riddle at Thebes and destroys those unable to solve it before dying after Oedipus answers it. In The Library, Sphinx is a monster who poses a riddle to the Thebans from Mount Phicium, devours those who fail, and dies after Oedipus solves it. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Sphinx is associated with the mountain where she recites a riddle, and is also described as a pirate and Laius's illegitimate daughter whom Oedipus kills. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Sphinx is a monster who riddles and kills travelers on the high-road to Thebes, then destroys herself after Oedipus solves her riddle. In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Sphinx is a songstress whose riddle afflicts Thebes and delays the investigation of Laius's death until Oedipus solves it after Teiresias fails.

Sphinx in The Library

Apollodorus · about AD 70 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Sphinx first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sphinx is a monster, daughter of Echidna and Typhon, with a woman’s face, lion’s breast, feet and tail, and bird’s wings. She occupies Mount Phicium, where she poses a riddle learned from the Muses to the Thebans and devours failures. After Oedipus solves it, she falls from the citadel of Thebes and dies.

Sphinx in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Sphinx first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sphinx is associated with the mountain of the Sphinx, where she recites a riddle that ruins captives. Another account calls her a pirate: she sails with a naval force, uses Anthedon as a port, and occupies the mountain for robberies. Called Laius’s illegitimate daughter, she is killed by Œdipus after his Corinthian force defeats her.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXVI.

The Sphinx is said to have recited a riddle that caused the ruin of those she captured. Another tradition makes her a pirate who sailed with a naval force, used Anthedon as her port, and occupied the mountain for robberies. She is also called the illegitimate daughter of Laius, and Œdipus is said to have killed her after defeating her with a superior force from Corinth.
