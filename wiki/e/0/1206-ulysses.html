  • In Heroides and Amores, Odysseus survives Troy’s fall, remains absent from Penelope and Telemachus, and is associated with Aeolus’s wind skins and a plow.
  • In The Aeneid, Ulysses frames Palamedes, engineers Sinon’s supposed sacrifice, helps invade Troy from the Wooden Horse, blinds Polyphemus, and encounters Charybdis.
  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Odysseus receives Hecuba as a captive slave, prevails in the decision to kill Astyanax, and faces a prophesied ten-year homeward ordeal.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Ulysses serves as a Greek warrior and strategist, helps conduct the Trojan War, escorts Polyxena to sacrifice, receives Hecuba, and escapes Polyphemus.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Ulysses is chosen by the Greek army to conduct Polyxena to sacrifice in honor of Achilles.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Odysseus defends Aias’s burial and leads the effort to retrieve Philoctetes, using authority, strategy, deceit, and occasional compassion.
  • In The Rhesus of Euripides, Odysseus infiltrates Troy, joins Diomede in a night raid, kills Rhesus, seizes his horses, and escapes.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Odysseus secretly wins the Danaoi’s vote for Achilles’ golden arms, contributing to Aias’s death.
  • In The Odyssey, Ulysses fights at Troy, endures prolonged wanderings, returns disguised to Ithaca, and reclaims his household by killing the suitors.
  • In The Odyssey, Ulysses returns to Ithaca disguised as a beggar, reveals himself, kills the suitors, reunites with Penelope, and resumes ruling.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Odysseus participates in the Trojan War, steals the Palladium, kills Astyanax, rules in Thesprotis, and is killed by Telegonus.
  • In The Iliad, Ulysses leads the Cephallenians, restores order among the Achaeans, fights and counsels in battle, conducts reconnaissance, and wins athletic contests.
  • In The Library, Ulysses leads the Cephallenians, uses strategy throughout the Trojan War and his wanderings, kills Penelope’s suitors, and dies from Telegonus’s wound.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Ulysses wins Achilles’ armor, aids the Greeks against Troy, blinds Polyphemus, survives the winds’ reversal, and overcomes Circe’s potion.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Odysseus appears in traditions concerning his wounds, Trojan deeds, descent to Hades, return, worship, and monuments.
  • In Description of Greece, Odysseus takes the Palladium, appears before Nausicaa, wins Penelope’s suitors’ race, and sleeps with Circe.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Odysseus fights the Trojans, wins Achilles’ arms, helps retrieve Neoptolemus and Philoctetes, and devises and leads the Wooden Horse deception.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Ulysses helps capture Troy, survives his long voyage home, and returns disguised to kill Penelope’s suitors.

Ulysses in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Ulysses is a Greek warrior, chief, hero, and king of Ithaca, son of Laertes, whose fluent speech, courage, and strategic action shape both the Trojan War and his troubled departure from it. Supported by Minerva through many toils, he serves as a spy in Troy, allies with Diomede in a night raid, kills Rhesus, takes his chariot, horses, and Dolon’s weapons, and escapes the Trojan camp. He also steals Minerva’s image. After Troy’s fall, he obeys the Greek order to escort Polyxena to sacrifice and persuades the army that Achilles must be honored. He persuades the Greeks to condemn Astyanax, then receives Hecuba as a captive slave by lot. Cassandra foretells that he will remain away from home for ten more years and suffer dangers at sea, abroad, and at his house. Storms drive his ship from Troy to Sicily, where he seeks provisions for his faithful companions but is trapped by Polyphemus. After the Cyclops kills some of his men, Ulysses uses wine to intoxicate him, directs an escape plan, blinds him with a heated olive pole, reveals his name, and hastens back toward the shore.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

HECUBA.

Ulysses tells Hecuba that the Greek army has ordered Polyxena’s death and that he has been commanded to escort her. He refuses Hecuba’s plea to spare her, arguing that Achilles must be honored, and leads Polyxena away.

ULYSSES, DIOMEDE.

Ulysses enters the Trojan camp with Diomede, kills Rhesus, and takes Rhesus’s chariot and horses. He follows Minerva’s direction to withdraw, gives the watchword Apollo to the Trojan guards, and escapes with Diomede.

THE TROJAN CAPTIVES.

Ulysses has won Hecuba by lot. Cassandra prophesies that he will spend ten additional years away from home, face many dangers at sea and abroad, and encounter countless troubles after reaching his house.

CHOR. Attendants of the aged Hecuba,

Ulysses persuades the assembled Greeks that Astyanax, as the son of Hector, must not be allowed to grow up. His argument leads to the decree that Astyanax be thrown from the towers of Troy.

TALTHYBIUS, HECUBA, CHORUS.

Ulysses has obtained Hecuba as a prize, and Talthybius orders her to follow those who come from Ulysses. Hecuba is therefore destined to serve him far from her country.

THE CYCLOPS.

Ulysses arrives from Troy after storms drive his ship to Sicily and seeks food and water. He exchanges wine for provisions, defends himself and his men before Polypheme, appeals to hospitality and the gods, and is taken into the Cyclops’s cave under threat of being eaten.

ULYSSES, CHORUS.

Ulysses describes Polyphemus’s slaughter of his companions and devises a plan to escape by intoxicating and blinding him. He directs the Chorus, personally carries out the attack with a heated olive pole, reveals his name to Polyphemus, and then hastens toward the shore.

Ulysses in The Odyssey

Homer · -0632~/-0599~

Ulysses is the king of Ithaca, husband of Penelope, father of Telemachus, son of Laertes, and master of a great estate and household. He is described as wealthy, wise, strong, skilled in warfare and with his bow, and marked by a boar-tusk scar received in youth. He fought for nine years in the Trojan War and was absent from Ithaca for twenty years, during which his household did not know his fate and the suitors abused his property, servants, wife, and son. On returning, Ulysses conceals himself as a ragged old beggar, calling himself Aethon and telling invented accounts of a Cretan life, foreign warfare, Egypt, Phoenicia, shipwreck, and travel. He first reaches Eumaeus’s hut, where the faithful swineherd shelters him without recognizing him. He endures insults and hunger while secretly observing Ithaca and preparing vengeance. He eventually reveals himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius through his scar, joins Telemachus, and proves his strength by stringing his bow and shooting through the axe handles. Ulysses then throws off his disguise and leads Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius against the suitors. He kills them for their insolence, their wasting of his substance, their abuse of his household, and their courtship of Penelope, while sparing Phemius and Medon. He proves himself to Penelope by describing the olive-tree bed he made, reunites with her, and visits Laertes. After revealing himself to his grieving father, he prepares to resist the Ithacans but obeys Minerva’s order to end the fighting, and rules Ithaca under a peace settlement. His future remains subject to Teiresias’s prophecy concerning another journey and his death.

Odysseus in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Odysseus is a cunning and resourceful son of Laertes whose record follows him from seeking Helen in marriage to his death. He sends Helen no gifts, expecting Menelaus to prevail, and pretends madness to avoid the expedition until the deception is exposed through Telemachus. In the struggle over Achilles’s body he helps drive off Trojans, later wins Achilles’ arms, captures Helenus, and helps bring Neoptolemus from Scyros. Disguised as a beggar in Ilium, he plots with Helen, kills Trojans, and steals the Palladium with Diomedes; after the sack he kills Astyanax. In later travels he sacrifices to the Nymphs, has herds in Elis, receives a mixing bowl from Polyxenus, follows Teiresias’s sacrificial orders, and goes to Thesprotis. There he marries Callidice and leads the Thesprotians against the Brygi before returning to Ithaca. He is father of Agrius, Latinus, and Telegonus with Circe, and Telegonus unwittingly kills him while he defends Ithaca.

Ulysses in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Ulysses is an Achaean leader, warrior, counsellor, Ithacan hero, and son of Laertes, father of Telemachus. He commands and leads the Cephallenians, contributing twelve ships, and is repeatedly valued for craft, stratagems, daring, counsel, and Minerva’s favor and protection. At the outset he obeys Agamemnon’s order to return Chryseis and the hecatomb to Chryses. He then keeps the Achaeans together: he refuses to launch his ship, restores order in the assembly, rebukes Thersites, and recalls the prophecy of eventual victory. In battle he promises to fight in the foremost ranks, kills Democoon after Leucus dies, attacks Lycian forces, and later is wounded while resisting Hector’s side before Menelaus and Ajax rescue him. He also flees toward the ships when Diomed calls for help defending Nestor. As an envoy with Phoenix and Ajax, he presents Agamemnon’s offers to Achilles, urges him to save the Achaeans, and reports Achilles’ refusal. During the night reconnaissance, he accompanies Diomed, helps intercept and question Dolon, learns the enemy dispositions, honors Minerva, and helps seize Rhesus’s horses. He strongly opposes withdrawing the ships, advises Achilles that the Achaeans must eat and arm before battle, and presses Agamemnon to swear concerning Briseis. The records end with his athletic cunning: he wrestles Ajax and wins the running contest after praying to Minerva.

Ulysses in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Ulysses is a Greek commander, leader of the Cephallenians, son of Laertes and Anticlea, husband of Penelope, and father of Poliporthes, Telegonus, and Leontophonus. He first secures his marriage to Penelope by promising to help Tyndareus, then tries to evade the expedition against Troy by feigning madness. Exposed after Telemachus is threatened, he joins the Greeks and repeatedly acts through strategy, deception, and persuasion: he finds Achilles by a trumpet blast, engineers Palamedes’ ruin, helps obtain Philoctetes and his bow, captures Helenus, steals the Palladium with Diomedes, wins Achilles’ arms, and invents, enters, and leads the men in the Wooden Horse. After Troy’s fall he receives Hecuba. His return is marked by raids, encounters with the Lotus-eaters, Laestrygones, Polyphemus, Circe, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and Calypso. He calls himself Nobody against Polyphemus, uses Moly against Circe’s enchantments, and is bound to the mast to hear the Sirens safely. At last he returns to Ithaca disguised as a beggar and, aided by Telemachus and Eumaeus, kills Penelope’s suitors. Following Tiresias’s directions, he travels and sacrifices to propitiate Poseidon. In the final account, Telegonus unknowingly wounds him with a sting-ray-spined spear while defending cattle in Ithaca, and Ulysses dies from the wound; other records give an exile to Aetolia as an alternative ending.

Ulysses in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Ulysses is an Ithacan, a Greek commander, strategist, and son of Laërtes, descended from Sisyphus, Jupiter, and Mercury. He is marked above all by stealth, stratagem, and eloquence: qualities that win him Achilles’ armor, yet draw Ajax’s charge that he is a coward who avoided war by feigning madness and arranged Palamedes’ death. During the Trojan War, Ulysses acts with Diomedes in reconnaissance and danger, kills Dolon and Rhesus, discovers the statue of Minerva, carries Achilles’ body, and claims to have helped restore the Greeks’ resolve and make Troy conquerable. He wins the contest against Ajax through a successful speech, retrieves Hercules’ arrows from Lemnos, and brings Philoctetes and the arrows to the Greeks, helping end the war. After Troy’s destruction he takes Hecuba as spoil. On his voyage afterward, he blinds Polyphemus and escapes with his companions, receives a leather bag of winds from Æolus, and suffers reversal when his sailors open it. Reaching Circe’s palace, he resists her potion with moly and compels her to restore his transformed companions.

Odysseus in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Odysseus is a figure represented through traditions, depictions, and accounts of travel, conflict, worship, and return. He is wounded at the knee by a boar while hunting with the sons of Autolycus, later appears in a coat of mail, and is associated with comrades Perimedes and Eurylochus. During a night-engagement he recognizes and rescues Helicaon; he kills Leocritus and is portrayed as an enemy of Ajax, urging the Greeks to stone Ajax after Cassandra’s rape and, in the Cyprian Poems, helping Diomede drown Palamedes. Seeking his return home, he descends to Hades and consults Tiresias, while Anticlea sits nearby and former heroes are mentioned. After Troy, traditions differ: one says he sent Penelope away, while another makes him father of Ptoliporthes. He loses and finds horses at Pheneus, keeps oxen opposite Ithaca, erects a temple of Artemis Heurippe, and offers Poseidon Hippius a bronze statue, though Pausanias doubts that offering.

Odysseus is an Ithacan Danaan, Achaean, Greek, and Argive warrior and leader: Laertes’ son, a battle-staunch commander, and a resourceful, wise man renowned for keen wit and counsel. He fights beside Aias around Achilles’ corpse, kills several Trojans, and persists despite a knee wound. His wit, speech, and deeds help him win Achilles’ arms over Aias by the Trojan captives’ unanimous judgment; Athena protects him from Aias’ attack. Yet he mourns Aias with remorse, wishing he had not won, and teaches steadfast endurance against misfortune and passion. As Achilles’ deeply loved friend, he joins Tydeides/Diomede in bringing Achilles’ son from Scyros, promises him Achilles’ armor, and helps equip and lead defenders of the Achaean wall. He also travels with Diomede to recover Philoctetes, comforts and persuades him, and helps return him to the ship. Finally, Odysseus devises the Wooden Horse deception, organizes its ambush, enters it with the chiefs, directs the hidden warriors, unbars its sides, and descends first into Troy.

Ulysses in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Ulysses is the king and ruler of Ithaca, a Greek chief and hero known for sagacity, husband of Penelope and father of Telemachus. After winning Penelope, he leaves for the Trojan War, exposing her to suitors during his long absence. He urges Helen’s suitors to swear an oath, briefly feigns madness to avoid the expedition, helps recruit Achilles, rescues Achilles’ body, gains his armor, enters Troy disguised with Diomedes to steal the Palladium, and advises use of the Wooden Horse. Returning from Troy, he loses men and endures Polyphemus, Circe, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, shipwreck, and the loss of his crew. Help from Mercury, Minerva, Nausicaa, and the Phaeacians brings him home after twenty years. Disguised as a beggar, recognized by Argus, he reunites with Telemachus, strings his bow, reveals himself, and slaughters Penelope’s suitors.
