  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Corinth is described as a city in Achaia that experienced successive governments, wars, political conflicts, and eventual Roman destruction and rebuilding.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Corinth is described as a city marked by refuge, war, political upheaval, and the Corinthian War.
  • In Medea, Corinth is described as the city ruled by Creon where Jason brings Medea and their children, and where Medea faces rejection, ruin, and exile.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Corinth is described as the city and land ruled by Creon, where Medea lives with Jason and their children before being ordered into exile.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Corinth is described as the country to which Jason's family and children are established and from which Medea is being banished.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Corinth is described as Oedipus's home region, where he was raised by Polybus and Merope and from which he fled to escape the oracle.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Corinth is described as a prosperous city and portal of Isthmian Poseidon that celebrates athletic, musical, martial, and heroic traditions.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Corinth is described as the city where Medea was living when she poisoned its ruler, Creon.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Corinth is described as Ephyre or Bimaris, bordering the Aegean and Ionian Seas and serving as the setting for Medea's revenge.
  • In The Library, Corinth is described as the place where Jason and Medea live for ten years before Jason's marriage to Glauce makes it the scene of Medea's revenge.
  • In Description of Greece, Corinth is described as a city containing the Isthmus of Corinth and notable structures, later destroyed by the Romans and rebuilt by Julius Caesar.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Corinth is described as a fortified garrison-town that joined the Achæan League before Mummius burned it, killed its inhabitants, and destroyed its walls.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Corinth is described as the realm of Periander and the home to which Arion returns after the dolphin rescues him.
  • In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Corinth is described as the former realm of Polybus whose people resolve to make Oedipus king after his death.

Corinth in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Corinth was a city in Achaia, between Cenchreae and Lechaeum. Alethes enlarged it and ruled for thirty-eight years; after its royal succession ended, the Bacchidae jointly governed, annually appointing a Prytanis, until Cypselus. It later faced war with the Lacedaemonians, sedition, and assaults by Epaminondas; Chabrias and the Athenians defended it, driving out Boeotians who entered its gates. It aided Sicilian affairs: Dion gathered mercenaries and arms there, and Corinth sent Timoleon to aid the Syracusans. It hosted Alexander’s meeting with Greek representatives, where he became general of all Greece. Contested by Ptolemy, Cassander, and Demetrius, it was captured by Demetrius, who took its havens, Sisyphium, and citadel, restored liberty, and garrisoned the citadel. Romans later destroyed it; almost a hundred years afterward, Caius Julius Caesar undertook its rebuilding.

Corinth in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Corinth is a city marked by refuge, war, and political upheaval. Jason and Medea lived there for ten years; after Medea fled, Corinthians buried her murdered children in Juno’s temple and worshipped them as demi-gods. Corinth received Deucetius in lifelong exile, aided Epidamnus against Corcyra, rebuilt its fleet after defeat, and supported Potidea. It hosted an assembly against the Lacedaemonians, then became the center of the Corinthian War, where sedition, killings, banishments, and fighting ended in Argive control.

Jason and Medea lived together in Corinth for ten years. After Medea murdered most of her children and fled, the Corinthians consulted the oracle at Delphi and buried the children in Juno’s temple, where they were afterward worshipped as demi-gods.

CHAP. XXVIII.

Corinth was the place to which the Syracusans sent Deucetius after accepting his submission. They commanded him to remain there for the rest of his life and promised to provide what was necessary for his comfortable support.

CHAP. VI.

Corinth aided Epidamnus by sending citizens and soldiers, partly from pity and partly because of an old grievance against Corcyra. After losing a naval battle, Corinth rebuilt its fleet, secured ships from its confederates, and later sent forces to support Potidea.

CHAP. IX,

Corinth hosted the general assembly against the Lacedaemonians and later became the center of the Corinthian War. A sedition there resulted in killings, banishments, fighting at the Lechaeum, and eventual control of the city and territory by the Argives.

Corinth in Medea

Euripides · -0430

Corinth is a city ruled by Creon. After the catastrophe at Iolcos, Jason brings Medea there, where they live with their children. Jason is to marry Creon’s daughter, and the royal bride’s household is there. Creon treats Medea as a dangerous foreigner and orders her and the children beyond the city’s borders. For Jason, Corinth represents orderly life under justice and law; for Medea, it becomes the place of rejection, ruin, and exile.

Corinth in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Corinth is a city containing the Isthmus of Corinth and notable structures including the Theatre, white-stone Stadium, Temple of Poseidon, Well of Glauce, and Odeum. Its name is attributed either to Corinthus or, in Eumelus’s account, to Ephyre, Marathon, and the Ephyræans. Ancient Corinthians no longer inhabit it. It was held by a Macedonian garrison under Antigonus until Aratus attacked, defeated the garrison, and helped free it. After Corinth joined the Achaean revolt, Romans destroyed it; it was later rebuilt by Julius Caesar, inhabited by Roman colonists, and given an imperial government. It lies on the coastal road toward Sicyon, amid sites marked by wars and fires, and is associated with Isthmian games and truce traditions.

BOOK II.--CORINTH.

Corinth is said to have taken its name from Corinthus, though Eumelus gives an alternative account involving Ephyre, Marathon, and the Ephyræans. The ancient Corinthians no longer inhabit the city; it was destroyed by the Romans after joining the Achaean revolt and was later rebuilt. Corinth is located near the burnt temple on the coastal road toward Sicyon. Several wars have taken place around Corinth, and fires have consumed houses and temples outside its walls; the Corinthians are said either to have built the temple to Olympian Zeus or to have lost it in an accidental fire.

CHAPTER VI.

Corinth was held by a Macedonian garrison under Antigonus. Aratus attacked the garrison suddenly, defeated it, and later helped free Corinth.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Corinth is associated with the Isthmian games and with a tradition explaining the exclusion of the people of Elis from them. Corinthians also announced a truce during the Isthmian games, allowing the sons of Actor to attend before Hercules killed them.

Corinth in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Corinth is a strongly fortified garrison-town in the Peloponnese. Occupied by Philip’s Macedonian garrison and besieged by Flaminius, it joined the Achæan League after the garrison was overcome. Though the Senate said it should not be forced into that League, it became an Achæan military center. After battle it was left open; Mummius entered, burned it, killed inhabitants, sold captives, removed valuables, and destroyed its walls.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VIII.

Corinth was occupied by Philip’s garrison and was besieged by Flaminius. After the Macedonian garrison was overcome, Corinth joined the Achæan League.

CHAPTER XIV.

Corinth was one of the communities that the Roman Senate said should not be forced into the Achæan League. It was the place where Lacedæmonians were attacked by the Achæans and where Critolaus later collected an Achæan army.

CHAPTER XV.

Alcamenes and his men fled to Corinth, where the Achæan camp was located, after abandoning Megara.

CHAPTER XVI.

Corinth was left open after the battle while many Achæans and Corinthians fled. Mummius entered it with his full force on the third day, set it on fire, killed many inhabitants, sold captives, and removed its valuable offerings and ornaments.
