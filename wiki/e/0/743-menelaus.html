Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, Menelaus fights Alexandrus, is wounded by Pandarus, battles repeatedly, helps defend and carry away Patroclus’s body, and ends his quarrel with Antilochus at the chariot race. In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Menelaus wins Helen, plans the expedition against Ilium, kills Deiphobus, takes Helen to the ships, survives the voyage to Egypt, and returns home. In The Odyssey, Menelaus rules Argos and Lacedaemon with Helen, returns from Troy after delays and adventures in Egypt, hosts Telemachus, and is destined for the Elysian plain. In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Menelaus is separated from the Greek fleet in a storm, with his fate unknown. In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Menelaus orders Teucer not to tend or bury Aias’s body, but leaves when Teucer defies him. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Menelaus presses for Iphigenia’s sacrifice, later refuses to save Orestes and Electra, and is commanded by Apollo to return to Sparta. In Hecuba and other plays, Menelaus leads the Greeks against Troy, reunites with the real Helen in Egypt, sails with her toward Greece, and later flees Sparta after Peleus subdues him. In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Menelaus enters armed, postpones Helen’s execution, and orders her sent to Greece for punishment.

In Heroides and Amores, Menelaus is Helen’s absent husband and king, whose authority guards her while he is away in Crete. In The Library, Menelaus marries Helen, rules Sparta, leads the Lacedaemonians against Troy, rescues Helen, wanders after storms scatter his fleet, regains his kingdom, and ultimately goes with Helen to the Elysian Fields. In Description of Greece, Menelaus pursues Helen to kill her during Ilium’s sack, returns after the capture, places statues near Aphrodite Migonitis, and is buried with Helen at Therapne. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Menelaus musters an army against Troy, plants a plane-tree at Caphyæ, bears a dragon omen on his shield, delays sailing to bury his pilot, and helps spare Laodice. In The Fall of Troy, Menelaus wins a chariot race, enters the Wooden Horse, kills Deiphobus, spares Helen, and leads her away after Troy burns. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Menelaus calls the Greek chieftains to recover Helen, regains her after Troy falls, travels with her, and resumes his royal dignity in Sparta.

Menelaus in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Menelaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Menelaus is a son of Atreus, younger brother and brother-in-law of Agamemnon, husband of Helen, commander of Sparta and neighboring Lacedaemonian settlements, and a Danaan and Achaean warrior. Helen’s removal to Troy is the central injury shaping his actions: he seeks revenge for the toil and sorrow it caused, and the Argives fight for his sake. Though called brave and loud in battle-cry, he is also described as often remiss and unwilling to exert himself. He begins by urging his people to fight and by eagerly meeting Alexandrus in single combat. He pierces Alexandrus’s defenses, breaks his sword, and nearly drags him away before Venus intervenes; Menelaus is declared victor. As champion in the agreement with the Trojans, he is then wounded by Pandarus’s arrow, reassures Agamemnon, and receives treatment from Machaon. He captures Adrestus but is persuaded by Agamemnon not to spare him. He volunteers against Hector after rebuking reluctant Achaeans, but Agamemnon prevents the duel. Menelaus remains anxious for the Argives, helps rouse the camp, aids in rescuing Ulysses, and fights repeatedly: he wounds Helenus, kills Pisander and Dolops, and denounces the Trojans for taking Helen and treasure and attacking the ships. His most sustained effort centers on Patroclus’s body. He stands over it, kills Euphorbus and Podes, seeks Ajax’s help against Hector’s force, sends Antilochus to tell Achilles of Patroclus’s death, and helps defend and carry the body away. At the chariot race, Antilochus forces him aside. Menelaus protests and demands an oath, but when Antilochus yields the mare, Menelaus’s anger ends and he returns it.

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Menelaus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Menelaus is a Greek king, Spartan leader, son of Atreus, brother of Agamemnon, lawful husband of Helen, and father of Hermione. He leads Greeks against Troy, saying that his purpose is to punish Paris for violating hospitality and taking Helen rather than merely to fight for a woman. After Troy’s destruction, he intends to take Helen to Greece and have her killed, though he agrees not to bring her onto his own ship. Accounts initially report that he and Helen are dead after the Greek fleet is scattered by a storm. In fact, he is shipwrecked, impoverished, and separated from his ships after years of wandering. In Egypt he learns that the Helen brought from Troy was a phantom, reunites with the real Helen, disguises himself as a shipwrecked mariner, and spreads the false report of his own death. Armed and supported by surviving sailors, he deceives Theoclymenus, seizes a ship, defeats Egyptian sailors, and sails toward Greece with Helen. Later, as Hermione’s father and a ruler with power over others, he assists Hermione against Andromache. He threatens Molossus to force Andromache from Thetis’s altar, seeks the deaths of both mother and son, and claims authority based on having taken Andromache at Troy and on her connection to Hermione. Peleus subdues him, however, and Menelaus flees toward Sparta, leaving Hermione behind while intending to lead forces and later confront Neoptolemus.

Menelaus is Helen’s husband and king of Sparta, chosen by Tyndareus among her suitors. Menelaus, king of Sparta and husband of Helen, receives Paris before Helen leaves with him. He calls Greek chieftains to recover her, regains her after Troy falls, travels with her through Cyprus, Phoenicia, and Egypt, returns to Sparta, and resumes royal dignity.

Chapter XX

Menelaus, king of Sparta, hospitably receives Paris before Paris elopes with Helen. He calls upon the Greek chieftains to fulfill their oath and help recover his wife.

Chapter XXI

Menelaus recovered Helen after Troy fell, and the two traveled through Cyprus, Phoenicia, and Egypt before returning safely to Sparta. They resumed their royal dignity and later welcomed Telemachus.
