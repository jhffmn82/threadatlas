Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Sun is the supreme star, parent of Days, observer of all things, and a power associated with eclipses, the blessed dead, and Thebes. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Sol is an eternal, hot, active god who loved Rhode, named Rhodes after her, and fathered several children by her. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the sun is a heavenly body whose heat and light nourish the earth and water and influence southern regions. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, the Sun is a god, ruler of radiant light, driver of the chariot, and witness to and participant in several mythological events. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, the Sun melts the wax of Icarus's wings when he flies too high. In Toto's Merry Winter, the Sun attracts and devours Short-Tail Comet No. 73, causing it to travel faster and burn, and is replaced by the moon at night. In Gallegher and Other Stories, the sun rises as a broad, red, heated disc whose glare sweeps the city and dangerously heats a baby's bedding.

the Sun in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where the Sun first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sun is a God, son of Hyperion, eye of the universe, author of light, and ruler of all things by radiant æthereal light. It possesses light, heat, horses, a disk, and the chariot driven by Phaëton; it moves in the ecliptic, measures the long year, and dissipates noxious exhalations after the receding waters. It is associated with Scripture and traditions of standing still upon Gibeon or of a backward-moving shadow. After Phaëton’s death, the Sun hides its sorrow-darkened face, suffers an eclipse of its disk, and refuses to drive the light-bearing chariot; one day passes without the sun. First to discover Venus and Mars’s adultery, it tells Vulcan. Venus’s retaliation causes excessive love for Leucothoë: the Sun neglects its course, disguises itself as Eurynome, embraces her, mourns her burial, and changes her into a frankincense tree. It ends intercourse with and despises Clytie, who continues longing for it.

The sun is connected with the story through Phœbus and his chariot. The explanation suggests that the fable may have originated in an episode of excessive heat or in the account of the sun standing still at Joshua’s command.

notes

The sun is associated with a possible faint reference to traditions in which it stood still or in which a shadow moved backward.

opening

The Sun’s father is described as suffering an eclipse of his disk when he is squalid and deprived of his comeliness. He is unwilling to continue driving the chariot that carries the light after Phaëton’s death.

opening

The Sun first sees the adultery of Venus with Mars and informs Vulcan of the affair. Venus’s retaliation makes him fall in love with Leucothoë, causing him to neglect his usual course and duties; he then disguises himself as Eurynome and embraces Leucothoë.

opening

The Sun loves Leucothoë, grieves when she is buried alive, scatters the sand covering her, and tries to restore warmth to her body. He then uses odoriferous nectar to transform her into a frankincense tree, rejects Clytie, and remains the object of Clytie's longing.

The sun rose hissing out of the East River, a broad, red disc of heat.

The sun rises as a broad, red disc and sweeps the city with a glare compared to a man-of-war's searchlight. Its light and heat penetrate the windows and make the sleeping quarters dangerously hot.
