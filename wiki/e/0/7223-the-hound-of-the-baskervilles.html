The Hound of the Baskervilles in The Hound of the Baskervilles

Arthur Conan Doyle · 1901/1902

The document is *The Hound of the Baskervilles*, presented as an adventure of Sherlock Holmes by A. Conan Doyle, inspired by a West-Country legend. It follows Holmes, Dr. John Watson, Sir Henry Baskerville, and Dr. James Mortimer as they investigate the apparent supernatural curse surrounding the Baskerville family. In London, Holmes and Watson first deduce facts about Mortimer from his walking stick. Mortimer then brings the Baskerville legend and evidence about Sir Charles Baskerville’s unexplained death: the frightened man died near the moor, with gigantic hound-like footprints nearby. Sir Henry, the new heir, receives an anonymous warning to avoid the moor, loses boots under suspicious circumstances, and is followed by a disguised spy. Although Holmes investigates the London clues, he sends Watson with Sir Henry to Baskerville Hall in Devonshire. Watson reports from the moor, where escaped murderer Selden, the secretive Barrymores, strange cries, and the dangerous Grimpen Mire deepen the mystery. He meets Jack Stapleton and Beryl Stapleton; Beryl warns that Sir Henry should leave, while Sir Henry becomes attached to her. Watson learns that the Barrymores are aiding Selden, that Sir Charles had arranged to meet Laura Lyons, and that an unknown man watches from the moor. The watcher proves to be Holmes, secretly investigating from a stone hut. Holmes concludes that Stapleton, whose supposed sister is actually his wife, is conducting a deliberate plot against Sir Henry. Stapleton has manipulated Laura Lyons and used the Baskerville legend to terrorize and kill Sir Charles. Selden is killed after wearing Sir Henry’s discarded clothes and being pursued by the hound. Holmes then identifies Stapleton as a concealed Baskerville descendant seeking the estate. Holmes, Watson, and Inspector Lestrade set a trap when Sir Henry visits Merripit House. Stapleton releases a huge black hound, made fiery-looking with phosphorus, but Holmes and Watson shoot it before it kills Sir Henry. They free the abused Mrs. Stapleton, while Stapleton flees toward the Grimpen Mire. Evidence at the hound’s hiding place, including Sir Henry’s missing boot, suggests that Stapleton was swallowed by the mire. Holmes finally explains that Stapleton used the painted hound, the family curse, deception, and fear to eliminate the heirs and inherit Baskerville property; Mrs. Stapleton ultimately tried to warn Sir Henry.

The Hound of the Baskervilles

This unit introduces *The Hound of the Baskervilles*, identified as another adventure of Sherlock Holmes by A. Conan Doyle. A. Conan Doyle dedicates the tale to Robinson, saying that Robinson’s account of a West-Country legend inspired it and that Robinson helped with its details. The contents list chapters concerning Sherlock Holmes, the Baskerville curse, Sir Henry Baskerville, Dr. Watson’s reports and diary, and events on the moor.

Chapter 1.

Mr. Sherlock Holmes and Dr. Watson examine a stick left by Dr. James Mortimer and use it to infer details about its owner. Holmes corrects Watson’s assumptions, identifying the initials “C.C.H.” as Charing Cross Hospital and deducing that Mortimer is a young country practitioner with a dog. Dr. Mortimer returns for the stick and explains that it was presented to him by friends at Charing Cross Hospital on the occasion of his marriage. He then asks Holmes for help with a serious and extraordinary problem.

Chapter 2.

Dr. James Mortimer brings Sherlock Holmes an 1742 Baskerville manuscript recounting the legend of the Hound of the Baskervilles and its alleged origin with Hugo Baskerville. He also presents a Devon County Chronicle report on the recent death of Sir Charles Baskerville, whose body was found at the end of the yew alley after he apparently fled toward the moor. Mortimer explains that Sir Charles feared the family legend, suffered from heart disease and nervous distress, and died without visible physical injury. Mortimer finally reveals that he found fresh footprints near the body which he identifies as those of a gigantic hound.

Chapter 3.

Dr. Mortimer tells Sherlock Holmes that Sir Charles Baskerville was found dead after apparently fleeing through the yew alley, and that enormous, unexplained footprints were discovered near the body. Mortimer reports sightings of a luminous, spectral creature resembling the Baskerville demon and fears that Sir Henry Baskerville, the heir, will meet the same fate if he goes to Baskerville Hall in Devonshire. Holmes agrees to meet Sir Henry at Waterloo Station through Mortimer but advises Mortimer to say nothing about the case until Holmes has considered it. After studying a map of Dartmoor, Holmes concludes that Sir Charles was likely waiting for someone near the moor-gate before running desperately from a source of terror.

Chapter 4.

Sherlock Holmes interviews Sir Henry Baskerville, who has received a warning letter telling him to keep away from the moor and has lost one of his newly purchased boots. Holmes determines that the warning message was assembled from words cut out of a Times leading article, with “moor” written in ink, and infers that its educated sender tried to disguise his writing. Dr. Mortimer then explains Sir Henry’s inheritance, his uncle’s death, and the family story of the hound. Despite the possible danger, Sir Henry insists that he will go to Baskerville Hall and invites Holmes and Dr. Watson to lunch at the Northumberland Hotel.

We heard the steps of our visitors descend the stair and the bang

Holmes and Watson follow Dr. Mortimer and Baskerville from their lodgings in an attempt to identify the person shadowing Baskerville. The unknown pursuer, traveling in hansom cab No. 2704 and disguised with a bushy black beard, notices them and escapes down Regent Street. Holmes admits that his eagerness caused them to lose the man, though he had obtained the cab number. He then sends Cartwright to investigate the waste-paper of twenty-three nearby hotels for a cut-out center page of The Times and plans to identify the cabman by wire.

Chapter 5.

Sherlock Holmes investigates the surveillance and strange incidents surrounding Sir Henry Baskerville, including the theft and return of Sir Henry's boots and the black-bearded spy who followed him. Holmes learns that the suspected watchers are staying at other hotels rather than the Northumberland Hotel, while a telegram confirms that Barrymore is at Baskerville Hall. Sir Henry decides to go to Devonshire, and Dr. Watson agrees to accompany him because Holmes cannot leave London indefinitely. The cabman John Clayton reveals that the spy falsely identified himself as Sherlock Holmes, causing Holmes to lose his third investigative lead and worry about Watson's safety.

Chapter 6.

Sir Henry Baskerville, Dr. Watson, and Dr. Mortimer travel from London to Devonshire and arrive at Baskerville Hall, where Barrymore and his wife receive them. Sherlock Holmes instructs Dr. Watson to report facts, keep Sir Henry under close protection, and investigate the people surrounding him on the moor. During the journey, they learn that Selden, the Notting Hill murderer, has escaped from Princetown and is hiding somewhere nearby. That night, Dr. Watson hears a woman sobbing somewhere inside Baskerville Hall.

Chapter 7.

Dr. Watson investigates the Barrymores’ account of the telegram and suspects that Barrymore may have been in London when Sir Charles Baskerville died. On the moor, Watson meets Jack Stapleton, a naturalist from Merripit House, who discusses Sir Charles’s death, the Baskerville family legend, and the dangers of the Grimpen Mire. Stapleton brings Watson toward Merripit House, where Watson encounters Beryl Stapleton, who secretly warns him to return to London and leave the moor. During their walk, Watson and Stapleton hear a strange cry that Stapleton attributes possibly to a bittern, though local peasants call it the Baskerville hound.

Dr. Watson visits Stapleton and Beryl Stapleton at their bleak moorland house, where Stapleton explains their former life and present interests. Beryl initially warns Watson to take Sir Henry away from the moor, then follows him to withdraw the warning and attributes it to fears about the Baskerville curse. Watson remains troubled by the death of Sir Charles, the mysterious sound linked to the hound, and the danger surrounding Sir Henry. Beryl says she cannot provide any definite information and returns before her brother notices her absence.

Chapter 8.

Dr. John Watson writes a report to Mr. Sherlock Holmes from Baskerville Hall about Sir Henry Baskerville and the people and dangers surrounding him on the moor. He describes Sir Henry’s growing attraction to Miss Stapleton, Jack Stapleton’s apparent disapproval, and the continuing uncertainty surrounding the escaped convict. Watson also investigates Barrymore’s questionable behavior and observes him conducting a secret nighttime vigil at an upstairs window. Watson and Sir Henry have made a plan of campaign based on these observations, which Watson promises to explain in his next report.

Chapter 9.

Dr. Watson reports that Barrymore appears to watch the moor from a particular western window at night, leading him and Sir Henry Baskerville to plan to observe Barrymore. Sir Henry goes onto the moor alone, but Watson follows and witnesses him meeting Miss Stapleton, whose brother Stapleton angrily interrupts their conversation. Sir Henry tells Watson that he proposed marriage to Miss Stapleton, but Stapleton opposed the relationship and demanded that the matter rest for three months. Stapleton later apologizes, and Sir Henry agrees to dine at Merripit House while limiting himself to Miss Stapleton’s friendship.

Dr. Watson and Sir Henry Baskerville discover that John and Eliza Barrymore have been secretly signaling to and feeding Eliza’s escaped brother, Selden, on the moor. They pursue Selden but fail to capture him after he throws a rock and runs away. During the pursuit, they hear the cry on the moor, which Sir Henry believes is the cry of the Hound of the Baskervilles. Watson also sees a mysterious tall man standing on a tor, but the figure disappears before he and Sir Henry can investigate.

Chapter 10.

Dr. Watson records growing fears about the moor, the spectral hound, the unknown watcher, and the danger surrounding Sir Henry. Barrymore explains that Selden is still being secretly supplied and reveals that Sir Charles went to the gate to meet a woman whose initials were L. L., based on a partially burned letter. Dr. Mortimer identifies L. L. as Laura Lyons of Coombe Tracey, prompting Watson to plan an interview with her. Barrymore also reports that another, non-convict man is hiding in the old stone huts on the moor and may be the stranger Watson has seen.

Chapter 11.

The narrator travels to Coombe Tracey to interrogate Mrs. Laura Lyons about her connection with Sir Charles Baskerville. Mrs. Lyons admits that she wrote to Sir Charles asking him to meet her at the garden gate at ten o’clock on the day he died, but insists that she never kept the appointment. She explains that she sought his financial help to regain her freedom from her husband, but received help from another source before the meeting. The narrator remains doubtful of her complete innocence and turns his attention to the clue concerning the lurking man among the stone huts on the moor.

Dr. Watson follows a boy across the moor after Mr. Frankland observes him carrying food toward a remote hill. Watson finds an occupied abandoned hut containing supplies and a written report that he has gone to Coombe Tracey. The message convinces Watson that the stranger has been monitoring him through an agent, possibly the boy. Watson waits in the hut, where the stranger eventually arrives and addresses him in a familiar voice.

Chapter 12.

Dr. John Watson discovers that Sherlock Holmes has been secretly living in a hut on the moor while investigating the danger surrounding Sir Henry Baskerville. Holmes explains that he concealed his presence to avoid alerting their opponents and praises Watson’s reports and investigation of Mrs. Laura Lyons in Coombe Tracey. Holmes reveals that Miss Stapleton is actually Jack Stapleton’s wife and that Stapleton has deceived Mrs. Laura Lyons, who expected to marry him after divorcing her husband. Holmes concludes that the matter involves a deliberate murder plot against Sir Henry and warns Watson to guard him closely until the case is complete.

A terrible scream—a prolonged yell of horror and anguish—burst

Sherlock Holmes and Dr. John Watson hear a terrible scream and find a man apparently killed while fleeing the hound on the moor. They initially believe the victim is Sir Henry Baskerville, but discover that the body is Selden, who was wearing Sir Henry’s discarded clothing. Holmes concludes that the hound was attracted by an article of Sir Henry’s clothing, though he remains puzzled about how Selden knew the hound was pursuing him. Stapleton arrives, conceals his alarm, and accepts the explanation that Selden died in an accidental fall.

“We’re at close grips at last,” said Holmes as we walked together

Sherlock Holmes tells Dr. Watson that they are finally in close conflict with a highly capable criminal after the wrong man was killed by the criminal’s plot. Holmes says they cannot arrest the man because they lack evidence connecting him to the hound or to the deaths, and he hopes Mrs. Laura Lyons and his own plan will help establish a case. Holmes intends to conceal the hound from Sir Henry and asks Dr. Watson to avoid a dinner with Stapleton so that Sir Henry will face the ordeal alone.

Chapter 13.

Sherlock Holmes identifies Mr. Stapleton as a Baskerville descendant by comparing his face with the portrait of Hugo Baskerville and plans to trap him. Holmes arranges for Sir Henry Baskerville to visit the Stapletons and walk home alone while Holmes and Dr. Watson secretly return with Inspector Lestrade. Mrs. Laura Lyons reveals that Stapleton dictated her letter to Sir Charles Baskerville, deceived her about his marriage, and persuaded her not to keep her appointment with Sir Charles. Holmes believes the evidence against Stapleton is nearing completion, though he intends to gather further proof before arresting him.

Chapter 14.

Sherlock Holmes, Dr. Watson, and Inspector Lestrade ambush Stapleton near Merripit House while Sir Henry remains inside with him. A huge black hound attacks Sir Henry, but Holmes and Watson shoot it and discover that its fiery appearance was caused by phosphorus. They find Mrs. Stapleton bound and abused in a room at Merripit House, and she reveals that Stapleton may have fled to an old tin mine on an island in the Grimpen Mire. Stapleton is not found, while Sir Henry is taken back to Baskerville Hall and later becomes delirious with fever.

Holmes and Watson follow Mrs. Stapleton through the Grimpen Mire to investigate Stapleton’s escape and the hound’s hiding place. Holmes finds Sir Henry’s missing boot, indicating that Stapleton reached the mire safely but may have been swallowed by it before reaching firmer ground. On the bog-girt island, they discover the hound’s confinement and evidence of Stapleton’s luminous-hound deception. Holmes concludes that Stapleton was a particularly dangerous man and is likely buried in the mire.

Chapter 15.

Sherlock Holmes explains to Dr. Watson how Stapleton, a hidden Baskerville descendant, used the family hound legend and a disguised, painted hound to cause the death of Sir Charles Baskerville. Stapleton then plotted to eliminate Sir Henry Baskerville and inherit the Baskerville estate, while controlling and imprisoning Mrs. Stapleton and manipulating Mrs. Laura Lyons. Holmes secretly investigated from Devonshire, used Sir Henry as bait, and helped bring Stapleton’s scheme to an end. Mrs. Stapleton ultimately turned against her husband and tried to warn Sir Henry.

The Hound of the Baskervilles is a tale by A. Conan Doyle, subtitled Another Adventure of Sherlock Holmes and conceived from Robinson’s account of a West-Country legend. It follows an adventure featuring Sherlock Holmes through a Baskerville curse and problem involving Sir Henry Baskerville, Baskerville Hall, the Stapletons of Merripit House, and the moor. Reports and a diary by Dr. Watson lead through deaths, nets, and a retrospection to the hound.
