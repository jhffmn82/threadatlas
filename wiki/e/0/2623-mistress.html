Abstract written by a model

The mistress is a beautiful, fair, free-born married woman, deeply loved by the speaker and involved with him in secret despite her husband and Bagous's watchfulness. She is associated with clandestine letters, signals, wine, meetings, and sexual intimacy, while publicly speaking and behaving as though chaste in order to protect her reputation. At the banquet, she is instructed to sit modestly beside her husband while secretly communicating with the speaker, resisting her husband's affection, and later reporting an unwanted encounter at home. After the speaker hurts her, she weeps, recoils from him, and appears disordered, pale, and faint with fear. When Bagous restricts access to her, the speaker urges covert visits, letters, excuses, and religious or social outings; he also treats her beauty and age as suited to dalliance and says she may deceive Bagous. In the cow episode, a white cow with a bull is said to represent the mistress: disturbed by a crow, she leaves her mate for other herds, suggesting inconstancy. Finally, the speaker explicitly advises her not necessarily to be faithful, but to conceal private intrigues, erase visible signs of desire, and deny them in public. Her central condition is the tension between secret infidelity and the performance of chastity.

mistress in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where mistress first appears

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

ELEGY IV.

The mistress is instructed to arrive early, recline beside her husband modestly, touch the speaker's foot, exchange secret signs, and share wine with him discreetly. She is told to reject food tasted by her husband, prevent his embraces and kisses, and walk near the speaker when the guests leave. At home, she is expected to submit unwillingly to her husband and report afterward that she did not enjoy the encounter.

ELEGY VII.

The mistress weeps after being hurt by the speaker, silently condemns him through her features and tears, and repels his hands when he tries to throw himself at her feet. Her hair is disordered, her cheeks are hurt, and she becomes pale and faint with fear.

ELEGY II.

The mistress is the young lady whom Ovid sees walking in the portico of the temple of Apollo. She refuses Ovid's request because Bagous watches her too strictly. Ovid suggests that she may secretly receive visitors, read letters, visit supposedly sick female friends, attend sacrifices, or go to the temple of Isis and the theatres.

ELEGY III.

Bagous's mistress is described as beautiful and of an age suited for dalliance, so her charms should not fade through neglect. The poet says that she may deceive Bagous, but that her gratitude might benefit him if he serves her.

ELEGY V.

The white cow stands in the meadow seeking grass among variegated flowers and is described as whiter than fresh snow and newly drawn milk. A crow pecks her breast and removes her white hair, after which she becomes envious and leaves the bull to join other herds. The interpreter says that she represents the narrator's fair-complexioned mistress and suggests that she is not free from the reproach of inconstancy.

ELEGY XIV.

The mistress is told that she need not be entirely faithful, but she must conceal her intrigues and deny them publicly. She is urged to behave wantonly in private sexual encounters, then abandon all signs of lasciviousness when she leaves. Her denials can easily overcome the speaker, who desires to be vanquished.
