lyre in Musical Instruments

Carl Engel · 1875-08

The lyre is a stringed instrument. In Assyrian forms it occurred in three types, was held horizontally or nearly so, and used strings shifted along an oblique or slightly curved front bar to change pitch; it was played by plectrum or fingers. It was widely represented in Greek contexts, with varying construction, form, size, and names, and Roman forms included lyra, cithara, chelys, testudo, fidis, and cornu. Hebrew coins show three, five, or six strings. Later it appears in fourth-century Christian monuments, including Christ as Apollo, and in western European popular pastimes and Anglo-Saxon performance.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER III.

The Assyrian lyre occurred in three forms and was held horizontally or nearly so. Its strings could be shifted on a front bar to alter their pitch, and it was played with either a small plectrum or the fingers.

CHAPTER IV.

The lyre was one of the most popular and most frequently represented Greek stringed instruments. Greek lyres varied in construction, form, size, and name, and were commonly played with a plektron, although they could also be played with the fingers. The Romans adopted several forms of the lyre, including the lyra, cithara, chelys, testudo, fidis, and cornu.

CHAPTER VII.

The lyre appears in Christian monuments dating from the fourth century, including one depiction of Christ as Apollo touching it. It was used in popular pastimes in western Europe, and ninth-century Anglo-Saxon imagery shows a gleeman playing it with a plectrum.
