Troy in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy, also called Ilius, is Priam’s city and the stronghold of the Trojans. It has walls, the Scaean and Dardanian gates, the sacred Pergamus sanctuary, and rich plains; before it stand Batieia and the battlefield where Sarpedon dies. Priam rules it, while Hector is its chief guardian, defending its gates, battlements, wives, children, and people alongside the Trojans and numerous allies. From the beginning, Troy is the object of the Achaeans’ campaign: Agamemnon expects it to be taken, and a prophecy says it will fall in the tenth year. Alexandrus’s conflict endangers the city and its children, while Juno desires its destruction despite Jove’s respect for Troy, Priam, and their people. The war repeatedly moves between the city, its walls, and the Achaean ships. Trojans sally out to fight, may retreat within the walls, and seek divine protection in Pergamus; their women pray to Minerva. The city remains untaken through severe pressure. Hector rejects advice to withdraw behind its walls, and Patroclus nearly takes Troy before Apollo drives him back. Achilles then drives defenders toward the city, seeks to hasten its destruction, and pursues Hector around its walls. When Hector is forced to fight and dies, the gates and walls have failed to save Troy’s protector. The city receives his body in widespread mourning. Hecuba, Priam, and the people fear that Troy will eventually be razed and overthrown, now deprived of Hector, though these records do not report its capture.

Ilius in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Ilius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Ilius, also called Troy, is the strong city and citadel of the Trojans, Priam, and Priam’s people. It is protected and respected by Jove, yet its fate is repeatedly threatened: Juno is allowed to seek its destruction, and Agamemnon, Hector, and others foresee that Ilius, Priam, and the Trojan people will be laid low. Hector is its chief guardian and defender. Early in the fighting, Trojans retreat into the city, Hector enters it to seek support, and its women pray in Minerva’s temple on the acropolis for its safety. Hector returns there for Trojan council, while its walls, houses, and watchfires become part of its defense. The Achaeans have failed to take Ilius, whose former wealth is remembered, but they seek to sack it. As losses mount, Hector declares it undone; several gods desire its destruction, though Jove’s protection remains contested. The Trojans try to bring Patroclus’s body into Ilius, and later the Achaeans prepare to fight against it. Achilles drives the Trojans toward its citadel, while Apollo protects the city and Agenor stands before its walls. The Trojans withdraw within Ilius as Achilles is drawn away, then Hector is pursued beneath its wall and killed. Its people mourn, and all of frowning Ilius seems smirched with fire. In the end, the city remains hated by Juno, Neptune, and Jove’s grey-eyed daughter because of Alexandrus’s choice, while Priam leaves it to ransom Hector’s body and his family returns to it from the plain.

Abstract written by a model

Troy, also called Ilium, is a Phrygian city in Asia, the home of Priam’s family and a city ruled by Priam. After the fall, Troy is no longer prosperous, and Menelaus and Helen return from it after the war, while Argives remember losses suffered at Ilium. Troy’s central importance is thus as the destination and object of the Greek campaign, and as the ruined city whose fall devastates its people.

Troy in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · about 429 BC · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy is a wealthy, formerly prosperous Phrygian city, Priam’s city, situated on Scamander and once fortified by walls, towers, palaces, temples, shrines, sacrifices, and a citadel sacred to Neptune. What matters most is its transformation from a royal, protected, hospitable city into a ruined and nearly nameless site through the Greeks’ ten-year siege and war. Under Greek attack, Troy receives Rhesus and the Thracians as allies; Hector and the Chorus hope their aid will preserve the city, defeat the besiegers, and restore its celebrations. Rhesus’s loss reverses this hope, although Hector still imagines freeing Troy by attacking the Greek ramparts and fleet. The city ultimately falls to Menelaus and the Greek chiefs. Greek spear, war, and fire tear down its defenses, walls, palaces, streets, towers, and sanctuaries; its bulwarks smoke, its roofs and citadel burn, and no trace of its walls remains. Its rulers die or are reduced to captivity, its women enter servitude, and its dead, including Astyanax, are mourned. Hecuba foresees the disappearance of Troy’s illustrious name, while the Chorus says that even its memorials are lost. Its destruction is repeatedly linked to Helen, Paris’s taking of Helen, or a phantom resembling Helen; Menelaus and a messenger say the Greeks’ labors may therefore have been in vain. Yet the records consistently present Troy’s fall as a disaster whose suffering reaches both Phrygia and Greece.

Abstract written by a model

Troy is an ancient, imperial Trojan city: the Trojans’ ancestral homeland, home, and origin, enclosed by Trojan walls and associated with Priam, Anchises, Hector, Aeneas, and its household gods. It matters chiefly as the lost center of Trojan identity and as the ruin from which the surviving people, their gods, memories, gifts, and future purpose proceed. Troy in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy is a Phrygian, Neptunian Pergamum: Priam’s kingdom and a royal city of high towers, strong walls, temples, wealth, and crowded people. Paris, restored there as a royal child, is celebrated at its festivals; he imagines bringing Helen there with rich garments, textiles, and gold. Helen acknowledges its wealth and population but calls it barbarous, fearing separation from her family, judgment by Priam’s household, and reproach from Dardanian wives. Cassandra warns that Paris’s departure will bring flames, and prophecy marks Troy for destruction by fire. It becomes the enemy city sought by the Danaans, destination of their fleet, and site of the Trojan War, feared by Laodamia for the dangers awaiting Protesilaus. In the end Troy falls, burns, and is reduced to ashes; returning Greeks take Trojan spoil, while crops and grass cover its ruins and the bones of the dead.

Troy in The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy is a city named after Tros, with Priam’s palace among its places. Its central story is the Trojan war: after Alexander carries Helen away from Sparta, the Greeks assemble an expedition. They first mistake Mysia for Troy, then, after Telephus shows them the route, sail toward Troy when the Trojans refuse to restore Helen and the property. The Greeks shut the Trojans inside the city and besiege it. Calchas says that capture will take ten years and cannot occur without Achilles; Achilles comes to Troy after Odysseus finds him at Lycomedes’s court. During the war, Alexander is wounded by Philoctetes with Heracles’ bows and dies while being carried back to Troy. After nine years, allies defend Troy, drive the Greeks behind their wall, breach it, and burn the ships. Memnon arrives with Ethiopians, and Troy becomes the setting for battles among Memnon, Achilles, Ajax, and the other forces. Yet the city cannot be taken while the Palladium remains inside it; capture also requires the bones of Pelops, Neoptolemus’s aid, and the bow of Hercules. In the end, the Greeks use the Wooden Horse, stationed beside Priam’s palace. They emerge from it, open the gates, kill the inhabitants, burn Troy, carry away and divide its spoils, and lay it waste before sailing away. Afterward, Troy remains a destination for the Locrian maidens sent to propitiate Athena, who are pursued by its inhabitants and take refuge in the sanctuary; it is also the place of the siege from which Idomeneus returns.

Troy in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · about AD 8 · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy, also called Ilion, is a city near the narrowed Hellespont, marked first by walls built by Apollo and Neptune for Laomedon. After Laomedon’s perjury, Neptune inundates its land, and Hercules later captures and plunders it. In its final great crisis, Paris brings Helen to Troy, prompting the Greeks to proceed against it. Calchas foretells that it will fall only after many years. Troy endures a long siege and is defended from within by the Trojan army; thousands die, and Hector becomes a central memory of its resistance. Although the Greeks are also described as defending Troy against the Trojans, they are chiefly its attackers and besiegers. Ulysses says that entering its walls and towers and taking Minerva’s statue made its capture possible; Philoctetes’ possession of Hercules’ arrows also fulfills its destiny. After ten years of bloodshed, Troy and Priam fall together: the city is captured, sacked, burned, and left prostrate, with its temples, towers, walls, and royal household destroyed. Yet its hope remains through Æneas’s escape and the departing Trojan survivors; prophecy holds that Troy survives in him and will reach a foreign land and a powerful future city.

Troy in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · about AD 200 to 299 · the work’s page · read it where Troy first appears

Abstract written by a model

Troy is a city, fortress, and Priam’s royal, god-built burg, also called Ilium. A stately, lofty-towered stronghold by the Hellespont, it has divine and seemingly imperishable walls, fortified gates including the Scaean Gate, palaces, temples, and homes. It is the homeland and refuge of the Trojans, who defend it from towers, ramparts, and gates against the Argives, Achaeans, and Danaans throughout the war. After Hector’s death, Troy is already terrified and moving toward ruin. Penthesileia briefly restores hope, but falls; her body is brought into Troy and buried by its wall. Memnon and later Eurypylus likewise offer hope of safety, yet battle continually drives wounded and fleeing defenders back through the city’s gates. Achilles threatens to leap its walls, kill its people, and burn its sacred and royal buildings, but his death brings Troy a short-lived celebration and expectation of relief. Despite repeated resistance, prophecies foretell the city’s fall, and fear, death, and loss increasingly surround its walls. Troy endures attacks on its gates and defenses, at one point protected when Zeus veils its unscaleable walls. It remains a fount of tears, with warriors and inhabitants perishing as its defenders retreat inside. Its ruin is prepared by the theft of Pallas the Gracious and by the Wooden Horse: the Trojans break part of their wall to bring the Horse within. Panic and ominous signs fill the streets, but the people do not heed them. At night, after feasting and sleep, the city is taken. Its palaces and temples burn; its people are slaughtered or enslaved; houses collapse; treasures are stripped; and Aeneas abandons the sea of fire. In the end, Troy is captured, sacked, burned, desolate, and mourned by its surviving people, who bury the slain.

Abstract written by a model

Troy is a city ruled by Priam, threatened by a Greek armament seeking Helen and chiefly defended by Hector. Though Hector foresees its fall, the city resists; its defenders briefly prosper while Achilles refuses to fight. After Hector’s death, Troy shelters its fleeing people, mourns and burns him, and continues fighting with allies including Memnon and Penthesilea. The Greeks finally capture it through the Wooden Horse: armed men emerge at night, and Troy is burned and subdued. Aeneas escapes with Anchises and his young son, while its exact site remains uncertain.
