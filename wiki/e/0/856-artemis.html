  • In Heroides and Amores, Diana is the maiden goddess who witnesses Cydippe's oath, may punish her broken faith, and is asked to restore her health.
  • In The Aeneid, Diana is the chaste goddess of wilderness and arms who protects Camilla by sending Opis to avenge her and preserve her body and weapons.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Diana is a goddess associated with Dictynna who is said to have come from the Hyperboreans and established worship in several regions.
  • In Iphigenia in Tauris, Artemis is the virgin huntress who saves Iphigenia from sacrifice, makes her priestess at Tauris, and possesses the shrine's sacred image.
  • In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Artemis is the virgin goddess who favors Hippolytus, exposes Phaedra's false accusation, comforts him, and promises him lasting honors and vengeance.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Diana is the virgin daughter of Jove and Latona who reveals Hippolytus's innocence, substitutes a stag for Iphigenia, and receives Taurian worship.
  • In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies: Artemis.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Artemis is the archer maiden who helps Hieron tame colts and honors an unspecified subject as highly as Delos.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Artemis is the pure maiden and golden-arrowed huntress who hunts wild beasts and rescues Iphigeneia from sacrifice.
  • In The Iliad, Diana is the huntress goddess who challenges Juno, is defeated, loses her arrows, and flees weeping to Olympus.
  • In The Library, Artemis is the Taurian goddess who demands Iphigenia's sacrifice, replaces her with a deer, makes her a priestess, and causes Adonis's death.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Diana is the virgin goddess of the woods, hunt, bow, arrows, and Moon who punishes violations of chastity and neglect of worship.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Diana is the goddess who avenges neglected worship, punishes insults, substitutes a hind for Iphigenia, and protects Hippolytus as Virbius.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Diana is the Delian goddess of the chase associated with plague among women and the deaths of Niobe's daughters.
  • In The Library, Artemis is the chaste maiden devoted to the chase who helps Leto, kills several offenders, punishes Actaeon, and hunts with Callisto.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Artemis is the widely worshipped goddess of hunting, archery, temples, festivals, sacrifices, and divine punishments.
  • In Description of Greece, Artemis is a deity honored through numerous temples, cults, statues, images, processions, sacrifices, and regional titles.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Hymnian Artemis is jointly worshipped by Orchomenus and Mantinea through a temple, annual festival, and separate priesthood.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Laphrian Artemis is the goddess honored at Patræ under a foreign title and statue associated with Laphrius or reduced wrath against Calydon.
  • In Description of Greece: Artemis the Saviour.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Diana is the virgin goddess of the Moon, woods, and hunting who punishes offenses, protects favorites, and rescues Iphigenia.

Diana in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Diana is a maiden goddess of Delos, daughter of Leto and sister of Apollo, associated with hunting, the bow, and quiver. Present in her temple when Cydippe read Acontius’s oath, she witnesses, records, and acknowledges the pledge, making her a binding divine witness to the promised marriage. Acontius invokes her as a powerful, violent avenger who may punish Cydippe for breaking faith and who favors him beyond what seems just. Cydippe interprets her continuing illness and failed marriages as Diana’s anger, despite offerings of incense and sacrifice at her shrine. Diana is also said to be able to restore Cydippe’s health, and Cydippe asks her to relent and seek Apollo’s healing aid.

Diana was present when Cydippe spoke the oath in her temple and is said to have recorded it and acknowledged it by moving her hair. Artemis is a goddess, virgin and pure maiden, daughter of Leto and Zeus, and sister and foster-companion of Apollo. An arrow-loving huntress, she delights in archery and the chase, shoots stags, hunts wild beasts, cheers hounds, and bears a golden curved bow and grievous golden-shafted arrows. She has horses and an all-golden chariot, travels through Smyrna to Claros, and at Delphi sets aside her weapons to lead the dances of the Muses and Graces. She accompanies Callisto in mountain hunting, angrily changes the pregnant Callisto into a bear, and later prays with Leto for Orion. When Agamemnon kills a stag and boasts against her, she stops the Greeks with winds; after Iphigeneia is brought for sacrifice, she removes her, takes her to the Tauri, makes her immortal, and leaves a stag instead.

Artemis is a golden-armed huntress, archer, and pure maiden who cheers on hounds and shoots stags. She hunts across mountains, woods, and the sea, destroying wild beasts with her arrows; when satisfied, she slackens her bow, goes to Delphi, hangs up her weapons, and leads the dances of the Muses and Graces.

Diana in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Diana is a Delian, Ortygian goddess of the woods, hunt, bow and arrows, associated with the Moon and perhaps identified with Isis. She is Phoebe, sister of the Sun and Apollo, daughter of Latona, and a perpetual virgin attended by Nymphs. Accounts also distinguish three Dianas, naming daughters of Jupiter and Proserpine, Jupiter and Latona, and Upis and Glauce; one episode is probably attributed to Diana Britomartis, who is especially fond of hunting. Chaste and prudent in explanation yet severe in action, she joins Apollo in punishing Niobe’s daughters after Latona’s complaint, hides Arethusa in a cloud and carries her to Ortygia, expels Calisto when her pregnancy reveals a broken vow, and punishes Actæon for seeing her bathe at Gargaphie by turning him into a stag. She is incensed at Œneus for omitting her first-fruit offering and is believed subject to magicians during an eclipse.

Diana in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Diana is a goddess, daughter of Latona, whose actions center on avenging neglected worship or insults and protecting those in her care. When Œneus forgets her in his first-fruit offerings, she sends the Calydonian boar to ravage Calydon; after Meleager’s death, she is said to turn most of his sisters into birds. She kills Chione after Chione disparages her beauty, and, at Mars’s request, sends a wild boar against Adonis. Her anger delays the Greek fleet until virgin blood is required; when Iphigenia is offered, Diana substitutes a hind and the winds become favorable. At Aricia, she transforms Egeria into a fountain and restores, conceals, renames, and protects Hippolytus as Virbius in her grove.

Artemis in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Artemis is a goddess whose presence is chiefly expressed through widespread worship, temples, statues, festivals, sacrifices, and accounts of her power. From the outset she is associated with the temple of Ephesian Artemis, whose worship predates the Ionian migration; Amazons had sacrificed and sought refuge there, while Coresus and Ephesus are said to have built it. This sanctuary is described as the finest in Ionia for size and opulence. She appears in forms and titles including the Huntress, Hymnia, Hemerasia, the Leader, Hegemone, Triclaria, and The Most Beautiful, and is represented as a huntress and archer. Her sanctuaries extend across many places: at Ægira, Stymphelus, Lusi, Tegea, Hyampolis, Bulis, near Acacesium, and on hills and rocks. Arcadians worshipped Artemis Hymnia throughout Arcadia; rules governing her priesthood changed after a priestess was defiled at her altar. Neglect or improper observance of her rites brings severe consequences: at Stymphelus, a negligently celebrated festival was followed by a blocked river and flooding; Artemis Triclaria’s anger after her temple was violated was said to bring famine, sickness, and unusual mortality. Her annual human sacrifice, ordered by an oracle, continued until Eurypylus brought a chest containing the statue of Dionysus, ending it. Artemis acts decisively in legend and ritual. She kills Buphagus for attempting her chastity, shoots Limon in vengeance for Scephrus, punishes people who neglect Leto, and is depicted shooting at Tityus. She shoots Callisto when Callisto is a she-bear, gives Procris a dog, and causes a doe to be sacrificed at Aulis instead of Iphigenia. She inspires a stratagem against the Sicyonians, stirs Chronius in a dream against Aristomelidas, and helps appease Apollo during his anger over the tripod. Her images emphasize hunting and light: deer skin, quiver, hunting dog, torches or lamp, and two dragons. The records end with temples and statues where she holds a torch and is accompanied by a dog, reaffirming her continuing cult presence.

Diana in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Diana, also called Artemis, is a goddess of the moon, woods, and hunting, a virgin goddess born on Delos, daughter of Jupiter and Latona, and sister of Apollo. Attended by nymphs and worshipped faithfully by Syrinx, she fiercely punishes violations and offenses: after Actaeon sees her bathing, she changes him into a stag and causes his dogs to kill him; with Apollo she kills Niobe’s daughters; and after Agamemnon kills her sacred stag, she sends pestilence, halts the Greek fleet, and demands a virgin sacrifice. Yet she also protects and favors others. She loves the sleeping Endymion, increases and guards his flock, and favors Orion as a hunting companion. After unintentionally killing Orion during Apollo’s challenge, she places him among the stars. At Iphigenia’s sacrifice, she relents, substitutes a hind, carries Iphigenia to Tauris, and makes her priestess there, where Diana’s heaven-fallen statue is kept.
