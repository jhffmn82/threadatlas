marital fidelity and chastity in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Marital fidelity and chastity are presented as enduring, voluntary moral commitments. Oenone contrasts her faithfulness with Helen’s alleged infidelity and warns that violated chastity cannot be restored; Helen calls fidelity sacred and protects her honor; the final record defines genuine chastity as restraint freely maintained without fear or external prohibition.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Oenone Paridi 2

Oenone presents herself as chaste and faithful despite Paris’s betrayal. She argues that Helen’s past abduction and rapid transition to Paris show a lack of fidelity, and she warns Paris that wounded chastity cannot be restored.

XVII

Helen presents marital fidelity as a sacred obligation and the basis of her refusal. She insists that her life has remained free from fault and that she must preserve her honor rather than become the spoil of an outsider's desire.

III

Female chastity is genuine only when it remains after fear and external restraint have been removed. A woman who refrains from wrongdoing solely because she is forbidden to act is not considered truly chaste.

chastity in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Chastity is a natural, fair, and well-regarded virtue, defined by Hippolytus as freedom from sexual intercourse rather than something taught in schools. It unfolds through Hippolytus’s devotion to Diana, rejection of marriage and Venus, and claim to an undefiled body. Phædra relies on it to resist love and preserve reputation, while the Chorus praises it. Hippolytus’s steadfast refusal of Phædra and reverence for the Gods make chastity both his piety and the cause of Venus’s hatred.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

HIPPOLYTUS.

Chastity is represented by Hippolytus’s devotion to Diana, rejection of marriage, and refusal to honor Venus. Hippolytus distinguishes natural chastity from a kind learned in schools.

PHÆDRA, NURSE, CHORUS.

Phædra treats chastity as the principal means by which she tried to resist love and preserve her reputation. The Chorus praises chastity as beautiful and honorable, while Phædra fears that she will be seen as unchaste.

HIPPOLYTUS, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Hippolytus claims that he is the most chaste man and that his body has remained undefiled by the couch of love. He says that he knows sexual acts only through report and pictures and has never desired Theseus's wife.

DIANA, THESEUS, CHORUS.

Hippolytus is shown to have remained chaste and to have reverenced the Gods, refusing Phaedra's advances and keeping the oaths imposed on him. Diana calls him pious and noble, and his chastity is identified as the reason Venus hated him.
