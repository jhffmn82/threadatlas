fall of Troy in The Trojan Women of Euripides

Euripides · -0414

The fall of Troy is the Greek conquest of the city, attributed to the Greek attack and the wooden horse that passed through its walls. It brings the killing of Priam, the deaths of Troy's men and Paris, blood in the sanctuaries, the destruction of the citadel, and the collapse of walls and towers. Greek forces carry Troy's wealth to their ships, while fire, desolation, and scorn consume the city. The conquest most grievously determines the survivors' fate: Trojan women, including Andromache and Hecuba, are allotted to Greek masters and taken away as captives; Astyanax is killed because the Greeks fear Hector's lineage. Though Hecuba imagines rebuilding, the final departure for the Greek ships leaves Troy destroyed, without defenders or a future.

The sack of Troy is a Greek victory achieved by Menelaus and the Greek chiefs after the Greeks, led by Menelaus, defeated and entered the city from a wooden horse. Its central consequence is Troy’s utter ruin: its men and royal family are killed, its palaces, temples, altars, sacred places, rites, and towers are destroyed or profaned, and its women become captives and slaves.
