  • In The Aeneid, Neptune is the sea god who calms storms, frees ships, and guides Aeneas's fleet toward Italy while being the divine father of Messapus.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Neptune is the navigator and horseman who served Saturn, fathered several figures, carried islands to Cercyra and Salamis, and was worshipped.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian: Neptune.
  • In The Argonautica, Poseidon is the deity honoured by Pelias and the father of Triton, whose car and guidance help the Argo reach the sea.
  • In The Trojan Women of Euripides, Poseidon is the sea god who helps build and protect Troy, then joins Athena in attacking the returning Greek fleet.
  • In Hippolytus; The Bacchae, Poseidon is Theseus's divine father who grants Theseus's prayer against Hippolytus and sends the force that kills him.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Neptune is the god who helps Apollo build Troy and later joins Minerva in punishing the returning Greeks with storms and waves.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Neptune is the ocean god who promises Theseus three curses and sends a destructive bull against Hippolytus.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Poseidon is the god of the sea, horses, earth, and the Isthmus who aids Pelops, supports voyagers, restores a house's glory, and desires Thetis.
  • In The Odyssey, Neptune is the sea god whose anger at Ulysses leads him to raise storms, destroy the hero's raft, hinder his return, and petrify the Phaeacians' ship.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Poseidon is the earth- and sea-shaking god who tames horses, protects or destroys sailors, fathers heroes, and is invoked to aid voyagers.
  • In The Iliad, Poseidon is the god of the sea and earthquakes who secretly supports the Achaeans, fights for them, and later withdraws when commanded by Zeus.
  • In The Library, Poseidon is the sea deity who fathers many figures, contests Athena for Attica, floods the land, destroys Erechtheus's house, favors Pelops, and persecutes Odysseus and the Phaeacians.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Neptune is the ruler of the waters who commands floods and storms, transforms mortals into sea deities, sends a sea monster, and contests Minerva for Athens.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Neptune is the ocean god who transforms mortals, builds and later inundates Troy, fathers Cygnus, grants invulnerability, protects the Centaurs, and seeks Achilles's destruction.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII: God of the Ocean.
  • In The Library, Poseidon is the sea deity who receives the sea by lot, wields a trident, fathers many children, creates floods and springs, and sends monsters and bulls in anger.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Poseidon is the deity of the sea, horses, riding, temples, oracles, and earthquakes who fathers many figures and is worshipped throughout Greece.
  • In Description of Greece, Poseidon is the water and sea deity who contests lands with Athena and Hera, receives the Isthmus, threatens regions with barrenness, and is widely worshipped.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Poseidon of Helice is the deity whose holy temple stood at Helice and whose worship continued at altars and precincts in Miletus and Teos.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Poseidon is the Sea-king and earth-shaker who strengthens Greeks, delays their departure, destroys Aias, and floods and erases the Achaean walls.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Neptune is the ruler of the ocean who commands storms, creates the horse, aids Jupiter, competes for Athens, and assists Greeks and Trojans.

Neptune in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Neptune is a god: lord and shaker of the earthquake, ruler of the sea, king of Helice, and holder of the world in his embrace. He is one of Saturn’s three sons, brother to Jove and Hades, with the sea as his allotted realm; though he claims equal honor with Jove, he submits when Jove commands. He has a golden palace at Aegae beneath the sea and a golden chariot drawn by brazen-footed horses. With Phoebus Apollo he built walls for Laomedon, including the wall of Ilius, and he later objects when the Achaeans build a wall and trench without honoring the gods, fearing it will rival their work. Jove permits him to destroy that wall after the Achaeans depart; Neptune will shatter it with his trident, fling it into the sea, and cover the beach with sand. During the fighting he is angered by Jove’s favoring of the Trojans and secretly supports the Achaeans and Danaans. From Samothrace and then from the sea, he uses disguises—including Calchas and an old man—to strengthen, cheer, rally, and lead them; he protects Antilochus, helps bring about Alcathous’s death through Idomeneus, and fights with a terrible flashing sword. When Iris brings Jove’s order, he protests but withdraws beneath the sea. Later he counsels restraint at Jove’s council, rescues Aeneas from Achilles because fate preserves Aeneas and the race of Dardanus, and reassures Achilles against Xanthus. Finally, he challenges Apollo, recalls their service to Laomedon, but does not fight when Apollo refuses.

Poseidon in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Poseidon is a deity closely associated with the sea, divine transformations, parentage, gifts, vengeance, and acts of destruction. He fathers many figures in differing accounts, including Lycus by Celaeno, Aethusa, Hyrieus, and Hyperenor by Alcyone, Triton by Amphitrite, Polyphemus by Thoösa, Neleus, Nauplius, Thasus in one tradition, and others; he also has unions with Aethra and is named in genealogies involving Asopus, Cychreus, Rhode, Salamis, Sarpedon, and Scamander’s lineage. He makes Lycus dwell in the Islands of the Blest. He first arrives in Attica, strikes the Acropolis with his trident to produce the sea Erechtheis, and contests Athena for Attica. After the twelve gods favor Athena, he floods the Thriasian plain in anger. Later, after Eumolpus is killed, he destroys Erechtheus and his house. He favors Pelops, his beloved and minion, giving him a winged chariot that can run through the sea without wet axles; he also receives Pelops’ prayer. He gives Xanthus to Peleus, receives the dedication of Argo, fortifies Pergamum with Apollo, and is connected with Minos’s unkept vow to sacrifice a bull. He answers Theseus’s prayer against Hippolytus by sending a bull from the surf, which terrifies the horses and wrecks the chariot. After Polyphemus is blinded, Poseidon is wrathful toward Odysseus and angry with the Phaeacians for aiding him: he breaks Odysseus’s raft, petrifies their ship, and encloses their city with a mountain. Odysseus later propitiates him among the Thesprotians.

Poseidon in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Poseidon is a deity associated with water, sea-facing lands, and powerful contests over territory. He is shown bringing forth water and leaving a trident mark in rock during his contest with Athene for Attica; he loses Argos to Hera’s arbitrators, floods the region, and later withdraws the water after Hera’s intervention. Briareus awards him the Isthmus and land toward the sea in a dispute with the Sun. His anger also threatens Trœzen with barrenness through brine, but sacrifices and prayers mollify him. He is linked by parentage or consort to Amphitrite, Palemon, Peratus, Athene, Cercyon, and Æthra, and is represented carrying off Taygetes. His importance is expressed through widespread worship: groves, temples, sanctuaries, altars, statues, and representations occur at Colonus, Corinth, Calaurea, Tænarum, Olympia, Sparta, and elsewhere. Images show him with a dolphin, while local traditions preserve his territorial claims, punishments, reconciliations, and honors.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIV.

Poseidon is said in a Libyan tradition to be the father of Athene, whose eyes are therefore grey like his. He is also identified as the father of Cercyon in the account given by Choerilus.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Poseidon is represented showing water in the contest with Athene for the soil of Attica. The contest is depicted behind the Parthenon, opposite the representation of Athene’s birth.

BOOK II.--CORINTH.

Poseidon was said to have received the Isthmus and the land toward the sea after Briareus arbitrated his dispute with the Sun. Corinth contained temples, statues, altars, and other representations of Poseidon, including a temple at Lechaeum, a statue at Cenchreae, and a sanctuary at the Isthmus.

CHAPTER XIX.

Poseidon flooded much of the Argive region after Inachus and the other arbitrators decided that the land belonged to Hera rather than to him. Hera later persuaded him to draw off the water, and the Argives built a temple to Poseidon the Flood-god where the stream receded.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Poseidon was said to have had an intrigue with Æthra on the island of Sphæria. The people of Trœzen said that Calaurea was once sacred to Apollo while Delphi belonged to Poseidon, and that the gods exchanged these places. Poseidon also threatened to make Trœzen fruitless by casting brine on its seeds and roots, but after sacrifices and prayers he was mollified and ceased to do so. He had temples at Calaurea, the old town of Hermione, and elsewhere in the region.

CHAPTER XV.

A temple of Tutelary Poseidon stood near the theatre at Sparta. Hipposthenes, who won many wrestling prizes, was worshipped according to an oracle as if honors were being awarded to Poseidon. On the throne of Amyclæan Apollo, Poseidon is represented carrying off Taygetes, the daughter of Atlas.

Poseidon in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Poseidon is a deity associated above all with the sea, horses, riding, temples, oracles, and far-reaching parentage. He fathers Ancæus, Chios, Agelus, Melas, and Œoclus, and is called Pelagæus, Asphalius, and Hippius; Hippius is linked to horses, riding, Arion, and his pursuit of Demeter. He receives horses, was said to have been fed lambs as a child, and has temples, an altar, a statue, worship, and an ancient oracle across many places. He punishes the Achæans with an earthquake after violence against suppliants at Helice, blinds Æpytus with seawater, and reportedly aids the Mantineans in battle. His unions or intrigues include Thyia, Ascra, and Demeter, by whom Despoena is born. In the Eumolpia, he shares the oracle at Delphi with Earth, employs Pyrcon, and later exchanges that oracle for Calauria near Troezen.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXI.

Poseidon has a temple near the harbour at Patræ and an erect statue made of white stone. His universal titles include Pelagæus, Asphalius, and Hippius; he is probably called Hippius because he was believed to have invented riding. Pamphus calls him the giver of horses and ships with sails.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Poseidon has a temple near the sea at Ægium. He is said to have quickly punished the Achæans after they drove suppliants from the temple at Helice and killed them, sending an earthquake that destroyed the city.

CHAPTER X.

Poseidon Hippius was worshipped at the temple near Mantinea. According to the account, he struck Æpytus blind with seawater after Æpytus broke through the thread guarding the temple, and he was said to have appeared to help the Mantineans in battle.

CHAPTER XXV.

Poseidon pursued Demeter with amatory intent while she searched for Proserpine. He changed himself into a horse after discovering her transformation into a mare and united with her; he was consequently called Hippius there first in Arcadia.

CHAPTER XLII.

According to the people of Phigalia, Poseidon had an amour with Demeter, after which Demeter gave birth to Despoena.

CHAPTER V.

Poseidon is represented in the Eumolpia as sharing the oracle at Delphi with Earth and employing Pyrcon as his interpreter. He later receives Calauria near Troezen as his share in exchange for the oracle.

Neptune in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Neptune, also called Poseidon, is Jupiter and Pluto’s brother who receives dominion over the ocean after Saturn’s dethronement. As chief of the water deities, he rules the sea and bears a trident able to shatter rocks, summon or calm storms, and shake shores. He creates the horse, patronizes horse races, marries Amphitrite, and is Orion’s father. During a conflagration, heat drives him beneath the sea whenever he tries to rise. He aids Jupiter by releasing rivers and waters, competes for Athens, and later favors and rescues Greeks, though Jupiter orders him from the field. He also protects Trojans by calming Juno’s storm and freeing ships, then permits Aeneas to reach Italy for Palinurus’s life.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter I

Neptune, or Poseidon, was the brother of Jupiter and Pluto. After Saturn's dethronement, Neptune received dominion over the ocean.

LATONA AND THE RUSTICS

Neptune tried three times to raise his head above the sea during the conflagration. Each time the heat drove him back beneath the surface.

Chapter XV

Neptune is the chief of the water deities and wields a trident that can shatter rocks, summon or calm storms, and shake shores. He created the horse and patronizes horse races, traveling in a chariot drawn by horses with brazen hoofs and golden manes. He hears Erisichthon’s daughter’s prayer and changes her form, and he wins Amphitrite while riding a dolphin.

THE ILIAD

Neptune favors the Greeks and rescues them when the Trojans are pressing them near the ships. Disguised as Calchas, he encourages the Greek warriors, but Jupiter orders him to leave the field, and he obeys.

Chapter XXIV

Neptune discovers that Juno has stirred a storm against the Trojans without his permission. He reprimands and dismisses the winds, calms the sea, clears the clouds, and helps free the damaged ships. He later agrees to let Aeneas reach Italy on the condition that one life serves as a ransom, and Palinurus becomes the victim.
