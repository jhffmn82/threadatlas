mankind in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Mankind is variously said to have existed eternally or to have first come into being at a particular time. At first it lived rudely in fields on herbs and natural fruits, then gradually developed articulate speech, shelters, fire, useful arts, inventions, and associations that planted nations. History benefits mankind by preserving virtue, exposing cowardice and impiety, and teaching justice and prudence.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

THE PREFACE.

Mankind is said to owe great gratitude to those who have written universal histories. History is presented as a general benefactor to mankind because it preserves virtue, exposes cowardice and impiety, and teaches justice and prudence.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK L

Mankind is said by one opinion to have existed eternally, while another holds that men first came into being at a particular time. The first men lived rudely in the fields, fed on herbs and natural fruits, and gradually acquired language, shelters, fire, and useful arts.

Mankind in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Mankind is a mortal being of extraordinary, inventive skill: through labor it subdues Earth, sails the sea, captures and tames animals, and possesses wise speech and swift thought. It creates cities, shelter, and cures, but its resourcefulness may serve good or evil, and it cannot escape Death.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHORUS.

The Chorus praises man as a creature of extraordinary skill who masters the sea, earth, animals, birds, fish, language, thought, cities, shelter, and medicine. Yet man cannot escape Death, and his resourcefulness may lead either toward good or toward evil.

mankind in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Mankind is an animated human race, the Creator’s last work, formed from earth tempered with water and made in the image of the Gods. More fitted for higher faculties than other creatures, it has an upright countenance turned toward heavens and stars and rules over animals, fish, and birds. It begins with primeval innocence but gradually degenerates into a greatly wicked, universally corrupt, impious race, whose thoughts are continually evil. Condemned by Jupiter and marked to be cut off wherever Nereus roars, it is threatened with destruction so corruption will not spread. Its loss is repaired by Deucalion and Pyrrha: stones become a renewed generation, hardy and able to endure fatigue.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

opening

Man is formed from earth tempered with water and is made after the image of the Gods. Unlike other animals, he stands upright and looks toward the heavens and the stars; Minerva animates him.

notes .. notes

Man is described as the last work of the Creator, formed from divine elements so that he could rule over the other animated works of creation. His animation is compared with God breathing the breath of life into him.

opening

The race of mortals is condemned by Jupiter because guilt and corruption are found throughout the earth. Jupiter declares that it must be cut off so that the sound parts will not be corrupted.

notes .. notes

Mankind is described as broadly corrupt and wicked, with the imagination of its thoughts characterized as continually evil. It is marked for destruction in the divine punishment.

opening

The human race, whose loss Deucalion and Pyrrha seek to repair, is renewed through the transformation of the stones. The resulting generation is described as hardy and able to endure fatigue.

human race in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

The human race, a nobler upright animal made in the gods’ image, descends from Aske and Embla; Prometheus gave fire for tools and survival, before violence and corruption ended in flood destruction.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER II

Man was made in the image of the gods with an upright stature and was given fire by Prometheus. Fire enabled man to make weapons and tools, cultivate the earth, warm his dwelling, and resist the cold; later, mankind became violent and corrupt before being destroyed by the flood.

the human race in Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879

The human race is presented as a conscious organismic species whose members differ in mental climate, solar energy, and the scale of their feelings and tendencies, yet can recognize one another through shared possibilities. It produces ideas and combinations as a sum total, but is marked by absurdity, self-delusion, incompetence, and condemning in others what it practices itself. Currently working with slovenly approximativeness and self-defeating inaccuracy, it may decline into weakness and degeneracy as machines take over, or become the nervous centre of increasingly refined mechanical processes.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

I.

Human beings are liable to laugh, shriek, or curl the lip at folly in others while remaining unaware that the same folly resembles themselves. They share a common susceptibility to absurdity, self-delusion, and incompetence.

XIII.

Human beings differ greatly in the strength and form that their feelings and tendencies take, but they show enough correspondence for one person’s behavior to illuminate human nature in another. They commonly condemn in others the very tricks or blemishes they practice themselves.

XVII.

The human race is described as currently performing work with slovenly approximativeness and self-defeating inaccuracy. The narrator imagines it declining into weakness and degeneracy as machines take over its work, while Trost expects it to act as the nervous centre of increasingly refined mechanical processes.

humanity in The Life and Adventures of Santa Claus

L. Frank Baum · 1902

Humanity is a numerous, diverse group of mortals, born into wealth or poverty and varied conditions and occupations. Its members live brief lives of toil, worry, aging, and death, yet each has a mission to leave the world better than it was found. Over generations, humanity increases and spreads across the earth seeking new homes. It clears forests, builds cities, fields, and pasture, makes ships from tree trunks, crosses oceans, and settles distant lands. Claus recognizes this lot, accepts it as his own, and leaves the Forest to join humanity's struggle. Ak teaches that helpful people are sure to live again.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

6. Claus Discovers Humanity

Humanity is numerous and includes people with many different natures, conditions, and occupations. Mortals must struggle and worry for wealth, eventually perish, and are born in both cottages and palaces. Ak teaches that helpful people are sure to live again.

7. Claus Leaves the Forest

Humanity is described as living briefly, working for its needs, growing old, and dying, while each person has a mission to leave the world better than they found it. Claus accepts humanity's lot as his own and leaves the Forest to take his part in its struggle.

2. When the World Grew Old

People become more numerous over the generations and spread across the earth in search of new homes. They cut down forests, build cities and fields, make ships from tree trunks, and cross oceans to settle in distant lands.
