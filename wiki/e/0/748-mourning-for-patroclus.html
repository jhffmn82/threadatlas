Abstract written by a model

Mourning for Patroclus is a collective grief centered on Achilles after Patroclus’s death. Achilles expresses it with dust, tears, torn hair, lamentation, refusal of food, and thoughts of slaughter and blood. The Myrmidons, Achaeans, captive bondswomen, and Briseis join the mourning, with Briseis and the women also lamenting their own losses. Achilles leads all-night lamentation, circles Patroclus’s body in chariots, cuts his hair, pours wine onto the ground, and calls on Patroclus until the pyre burns down. Later, grief over other dead drives Priam to seek Hector’s body.

BOOK XVIII.

Achilles expresses overwhelming grief by covering himself with dust, tearing his hair, and lamenting that he could not save Patroclus. The Myrmidons and the captive bondswomen also mourn, and Achilles leads the lament throughout the night.

Grief dominates Achilles, Briseis, and the Achaeans after Patroclus's death. Achilles refuses food and can think only of slaughter and blood, while Briseis and the other women lament their own losses through mourning for Patroclus.

BOOK XXIII.

Achilles and the Myrmidons mourn Patroclus by circling his body in chariots, weeping, cutting their hair, and lamenting through the night. Achilles continues pouring wine onto the ground and calling on Patroclus until the pyre burns down.

BOOK XXIV.

Achilles grieves intensely for Patroclus, while Priam, Hecuba, their household, and the Trojans mourn Hector and the other dead. Priam’s grief drives him to risk visiting Achilles so that he may take Hector in his arms and mourn him.
