Naso in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

Naso is a male poet and lover, born among the watery Peligni, staying at Sulmo, and associated with the Amours and Elegies. His defining condition is service to Cupid and Love: Cupid diverts him from warfare and mighty subjects, wounds and captures him, and makes him compose elegiac love poetry in six feet followed by five. Naso repeatedly casts himself as Cupid’s soldier, servant, slave, and captive, yet also as a poet whose verses, gentle words, fidelity, and praise are what he can offer. His life in these records unfolds through volatile erotic pursuits. He loves a mistress, especially Corinna, with constancy and longing, seeks secret meetings, communicates covertly at a banquet beside her husband, courts other women, and admits love for two mistresses at once. He is easily inflamed by many forms of beauty, lacks self-control, and compares himself to a ship driven by a tide. Desire brings pleasure, jealousy, grief, separation, and humiliation: he suspects infidelity, suffers for his mistress’s perjury, fears for Corinna’s safety, and experiences sexual impotence despite recounting recent successes. He also confesses a violent assault on his mistress, regrets it, condemns his hands, and asks to be punished. Naso’s poetry and love continually compete with more elevated ambitions. He begins with intended arms and warfare, but Love redirects him to tender subjects; later he considers Tragedy, receives its sceptre and lofty buskin, yet accepts a reprieve to continue elegiac love. He identifies himself as the poet of his own failings, values lasting fame for his loves, and ends his Elegies to undertake a more extended work. The surviving work is linked with three remaining books, though five formerly constituted it. By the end, he presents his sportive lays as honorable, claims inherited knightly rank and Pelignian glory, and turns from Elegy toward a larger undertaking.

AN EPIGRAM ON THE AMOURS.

Naso is associated with the five books that formerly constituted the work; only three remain in the present version.

ELEGY I.

The poet prepares to write about arms and warfare but complains when Cupid alters his verse and gives him a subject of love. After Cupid shoots him, he burns with love and begins composing in six feet followed by five.

ELEGY II.

The narrator suffers sleeplessness and bodily pain before realizing that Love has attacked him. He yields to Cupid, calls himself Cupid's newly made captive, and asks his beloved mistress to return his affection; he also offers lifelong fidelity and lasting poetic praise.

ELEGY IV.

The speaker is jealous and distressed that the mistress's husband will attend the same banquet. He gives the mistress detailed instructions for secretly communicating with him, avoiding her husband, and arranging an opportunity after the husband is asleep. He expects the mistress to tell him the next day that she did not dally with her husband.

ELEGY V.

The narrator rests on a couch at midday and draws aside Corinna's tunic when she enters. He observes and praises her body, presses her to himself, and later reclines with her in weariness.

ELEGY VII.

The speaker admits that frenzy raised his arms against his mistress and that he injured her by tearing her hair and marking her cheeks with his nails. He regrets his violence, calls his hands impious, wishes to be chained, and invites his mistress to punish him in return.

ELEGY X.

The speaker says that he formerly loved his mistress's mind as well as her person, but that her requests for presents have cured his passion. He offers duty, zeal, fidelity, and poetic praise as what he can give her.

BOOK THE SECOND ELEGY I.

Naso identifies himself as born among the watery Peligni and as the Poet of his own failings. He says he began to sing of the battles of heaven and the Giants, but turned to love poetry after his mistress shut her door against him; his gentle words and Elegies then prevailed.

ELEGY II.

Ovid has seen a young lady walking in the portico of the temple of Apollo and has sent her a request to visit her. He addresses Bagous, urging him to relax his surveillance and help the lovers meet safely. He argues that accusations harm either an indifferent husband or a husband who dotes on his wife.

ELEGY III.

The poet confesses that he cannot control his attraction to women and compares himself to a ship carried along by a rapid tide. He admits that he is pleased by women who praise or abuse him and that he is excited on every occasion.

ELEGY V.

The speaker witnesses his mistress communicating secretly with another person and later kissing him. He becomes angry and jealous, but her beauty weakens his resolve, so he begs her for kisses and remains tormented by the possibility that she gave the rival equally good ones.

ELEGY IX.

The speaker presents himself as Cupid’s loyal soldier, wounded in Cupid’s own camp despite never deserting his standards. He says he is exhausted from serving love and ought to be discharged, but he would refuse a life without love because the fair are a sweet evil. He asks Cupid to pierce him while he is unarmed and exposed, and he hopes for alternating caresses, reproaches, favor, and rejection from his mistress.

ELEGY X.

The speaker admits to Græcinus that he is simultaneously in love with two mistresses and is tormented by choosing between them. He wishes to remain devoted to Love, to die in Love's service, and to be remembered as having met a fitting death. He urges Corinna not to sail, promises to welcome her return, and claims sole credit for overcoming the obstacles that guarded her.

ELEGY XIII.

Naso is angry that Corinna undertook the risk without his knowledge, but his anger gives way to apprehension. He prays to Isis and Lucina for Corinna's life and promises frankincense, vowed gifts, and an inscription in their honor.

ELEGY XIV.

The speaker condemns the mistress's attempted abortion and compares it to unnatural acts of maternal violence. He says that he himself would never have lived if his mother had killed him before his birth, and he prays for the mistress's safety.

ELEGY XV.

The speaker envies the ring because it can touch and remain close to his mistress. He wishes to be transformed into the ring and imagines escaping from her finger into her bosom, sealing her tablets, and refusing to leave her desk.

ELEGY XVI.

The speaker is staying at Sulmo and admires the healthfulness and beauty of the surrounding Pelignian fields. Without his mistress, he feels as though he is not in his native place but in distant and hostile lands. He asks his mistress to join him and says he would endure any dangerous journey with his love.

ELEGY XVIII.

The speaker-poet rests in the shade of Venus while Love interrupts his attempts to undertake a mighty subject. He is drawn away from tragedy and other ambitious writing, and instead composes works about tender love, letters, and abandoned lovers.

---- BOOK THE THIRD. / ELEGY I.

The Poet deliberates in a sacred grove about whether to continue writing Elegies or begin a greater work of Tragedy. He asks Elegy and Tragedy to hear him without prejudice, acknowledging that Tragedy offers him lofty poetic fame while Elegy offers lasting fame for his loves. He is granted a short respite to write about tender Love before beginning the more weighty undertaking.

ELEGY II.

The speaker attends the Circus Maximus to sit beside his mistress and admire her, though he pretends to be concerned with the race. He tries to protect and assist her, declares his passion, supports her favorite charioteer, and receives a promising look from her.

ELEGY III.

The speaker complains that he has suffered for his mistress’s perjury even though the Gods have witnessed her oaths. He questions the Gods’ justice and says that, if he were a Divinity, he would allow a beautiful woman to swear falsely with impunity.

ELEGY VII.

The male speaker fails to respond sexually while the unnamed girl embraces, kisses, touches, and encourages him. He feels intense shame, recalls his recent sexual successes, and wonders whether magic, poison, exhaustion, or his own embarrassment has caused the failure.

ELEGY X.

The poet says that he composed his Elegies as a foster-child of the Pelignian fields and that his sportive lays did not disgrace him. He claims ancestral rank, predicts that he will be called the glory of the Pelignian race, and announces that he is turning to a more extended work.

Ovid in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Ovid is a Paelignian poet from Sulmo, identified as Naso and author of The Amores, Heroides, and books of verse. His defining work is love elegy: he writes of Corinna and other beloved women, gives women fame through poetry, and regards poetry as both his wealth and a source of enduring reputation. Compelled and guided by Amor or Cupid, he portrays himself as a lover, even a slave to a woman, unable to govern himself or stop desiring what he also hates. Across the poems, his love life moves through pursuit, frustration, jealousy, injury, and reflection. He secretly approaches a door with wine and a fallen chaplet, begs a janitor for entry, threatens the house, and leaves refused, ashamed, and regretful of striking his mistress. He overhears Dipsas coaching a young woman, resents the power of poems as gifts, and curses Dipsas. He sends Corinna tablets through Nape, living on hope for a night with her. He accepts dependence on his mistress and even wants her husband to guard her more closely so that obstacles may sustain desire. He courts a woman at the circus, denounces the gods when a faithless girl breaks an oath without losing her beauty, and is delayed by a swollen river while rushing to his lady-love. He mourns Tibullus and questions piety and the gods, while asserting that poetry endures. Love and hate remain in conflict: he claims release from love but cannot cease loving. Visited by Elegy and Tragedy, he postpones greater work to continue short love poems. At the end, he regrets making Corinna common by praising her beauty, celebrates his standing as a poet, closes the elegies, and turns toward a greater subject associated with Lyaeus.

Ovid in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Ovid is a Roman poet, born in Sulmo in 43 B.C. Educated in Rome under distinguished masters, he completed his studies through visits to Athens and Asia Minor and lived in Sicily for nearly a year. At his father’s urging he briefly practiced law and held minor state offices after attaining his majority, but abandoned the law courts for poetry. He became intimate with distinguished poets and, as a favored poet, received royal-family patronage. His first youthful marriage was unhappy, his second brief, and his third lasting and devoted. He was prosperous and happy until about age fifty. Around 8 A.D. Augustus banished him from Rome to Tomi, where he died in 18 A.D. while still in banishment. He wrote poetry including the Amores, Heroic Epistles, Ars Amatoria, Remedia Amoris, Metamorphoses, Fasti, Tristia, Epistles, and minor poems. Metamorphoses, in fifteen books, is identified as his masterpiece and as a work checked against a Latin original. In its narratives he follows prevailing tradition in presenting a divine decision, deliberated by Jupiter and the Gods, to punish wicked mankind by a deluge. He gives Deucalion the merit belonging to Noah, treats the flood as universal though it was generally said to affect Thessaly only, and includes anachronisms involving the Molossi and frankincense. He alone names Pyreneus, is the earliest writer noted for the Pierides’ challenge to the Muses, and uses Arachne’s contest with Minerva to introduce further myths, including the Pygmies’ warfare with the Cranes.

Ovid in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Ovid is a Poet whose work includes accounts that shape mythic figures and history: he presents the Sibyls as one long-lived person, attributes Pythagoras’s teaching to Numa before Numa’s sovereignty despite an acknowledged anachronism, and follows later accounts of Hippolytus as Virbius. At the end of The Metamorphoses, he praises Augustus, wishes for Augustus’s long reign, completes the work, and expects immortality through poetry.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLE III. [XIV.101-153]

Ovid represents the various Sibyls as one person in order to explain her prolonged existence. In his account, Apollo's gift allows the Sibyl to live for many ages, though without preserving her youth.

EXPLANATION.

Ovid claims that Numa Pompilius went to Crotona to study under Pythagoras, but the explanation identifies this as a considerable anachronism. He also follows later authors in connecting Hippolytus with restoration to life and his appearance in Italy as Virbius.

FABLE VIII. [XV.745-879]

The Poet concludes the work by praising Augustus and asking that Augustus’s life and reign long outlast his own. He declares that the better part of himself will become immortal through poetry.

Ovid in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Ovid, also called Naso, was a Roman poet born in 43 B.C. He chose poetry over public life despite holding dignified offices. His major works include the mythological Metamorphoses and Fasti; the Metamorphoses narrates creation, human decline, the flood, and mankind’s renewal. Banished from Rome at fifty, he spent his last ten years at Tomi in grief and anxiety, writing Tristia and Letters from Pontus.
