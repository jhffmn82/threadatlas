gifts and payment for sexual favors in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · about 24 to 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where gifts and payment for sexual favors first appears

Abstract written by a model

The topic is gifts and payment for sexual favors. Dipsas teaches a young woman to feign love, admit lovers according to their gifts, and use her slaves, sister, mother, and nurse to seek more, including through birthday pretexts, rivals' presents, and unrepaid loans; the narrator's poetry is deemed inadequate. The later account condemns payment as degrading and incompatible with love, distinguishing it from freely given gifts. It values a poor lover's service, devotion, faithfulness, and poetry, whose fame can outlast material gifts.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

VIII

Dipsas teaches the young woman to demand gifts and payment while pretending love, to admit lovers according to what they bring, and to use slaves, a sister, a mother, and a nurse to solicit additional gifts. She recommends birthday pretexts, presents from rivals, and even asking for a loan that will never be repaid; the narrator's poetry is treated as insufficient payment.

IX

Payment for love is condemned as degrading, because paying in full releases the recipient from any remaining obligation. Gifts are treated differently when they are freely given, especially by a wealthy person, while a poor lover is encouraged to give service, devotion, faithfulness, or poetry; such poetry can provide enduring fame after clothes, jewels, and gold have been destroyed.

the presents and payments extracted from lovers in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · 15 BC · the work’s page · read it where the presents and payments extracted from lovers first appears

Abstract written by a model

The courtesan's trade is sought through repeated requests and deception. Dipsas urges the damsel to demand presents, price men's redemption, seek unrepaid loans, and use servants, relatives, birthdays, other men's gifts, and Sacred Street talk to gather further payments.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

ELEGY VII.

Dipsas urges the damsel to demand presents, accept men redeemed at a price, solicit loans that will never be returned, and enlist servants, relatives, and a nurse in gathering money. She recommends using birthdays, gifts from other men, and talk of the Sacred Street to encourage further payments.
