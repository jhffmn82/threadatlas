  • In Hecuba and other plays, Ajax is a Greek chief and Teucer’s brother who dies by his own sword after another warrior receives Achilles’ arms.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Aias is a Greek and Argive warrior whose madness leads him to slaughter livestock before he dies by falling on his sword.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Aias is Telamon’s son and a formidable Trojan War warrior who helps defend Achilles’ body, loses Achilles’ arms to Odysseus, and dies by his own sword.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Aias carries Achilles’ body to the ships, disputes the armour with Odysseus, goes mad after losing it, destroys the Achaean herd, and kills himself.
  • In The Iliad, Ajax son of Telamon is a foremost Achaean warrior and defender who commands troops, fights Hector, protects the ships and Patroclus’ body, and competes in wrestling.
  • In The Library, Ajax is a Greek hero who fights Hector, defends and carries the bodies of Greek champions, loses Achilles’ arms to Odysseus, goes mad, and dies by suicide or at sea according to different accounts.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Ajax is Achilles’ cousin and the lord of the seven-fold shield who loses Achilles’ armour to Ulysses, goes mad, and kills himself with Hector’s sword.
  • In Description of Greece, Ajax is associated with Salamis, where he has a temple and an ebony statue and receives divine honours alongside Eurysaces.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Aias is the greatest Greek defender after Achilles, a mighty warrior who goes mad after losing Achilles’ arms to Odysseus and kills himself with Hector’s sword.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Ajax is a gigantic and courageous Greek warrior who defends the ships, helps defend Patroclus’ body, loses Achilles’ armour to Ulysses, and kills himself.

Renowned as the greatest or most valiant surviving Greek, he rescues the Greek ships from Hector, fights Hector in single combat, and bears a seven-hided shield and a sword received from Hector. Menelaus and Agamemnon oppose his burial, but Teucer defends his service and Odysseus calls him, apart from Achilles, the noblest Argive at Troy. He aids Menestheus, kills Epicles, presses Hector from the battlement, receives renewed strength from Neptune, and helps establish the Achaean defensive line. He returns to defend Patroclus with Menelaus, kills Hippothous, and calls for united hand-to-hand resistance. Aias in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Aias is presented as a mighty Greek, Argive, Achaean, and Danaan warrior in the war against the Trojans, chiefly identified as Telamon’s son and as the Danaans’ greatest defence after Achilles falls. Fate makes him invulnerable to foes’ blades; he bears a sevenfold shield, exceptional strength, and prizes won in athletic contests. Fighting beside Achilles, he urges him to defend the ships, kills enemies, and survives Penthesileia’s lance. After Achilles dies, Aias guards the corpse, drives back Trojan attackers, mourns his kinsman, and argues for holding the ships. He defeats Diomedes in wrestling, receives handmaids, wins an iron bar and silver, and is given Memnon’s arms. His central reversal comes when he claims Achilles’ arms for carrying the body and defending the ships, but Odysseus receives them. Overcome by grief, wrath, and madness inflicted by Athena or the gods, Aias mistakes sheep for enemies, slaughtering them before recognizing the deception. He curses Odysseus, Agamemnon, and the Argives, then kills himself with Hector’s sword; the Danaans honor him by burning and burying him near Rhoeteium. The records also describe Aias as Oileus’ son, a Locrian king with a fourfold buckler: struck down by Aeneas’ stone, he is rescued to the ships, later survives a shipwreck and clings to the Gyraean Rock, until Poseidon destroys the rock and crushes him.

Ajax in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Ajax is a gigantic, courageous Greek warrior, famed for prodigious valor but dull intellect. He bravely defends the Greek ships, strikes down Hector with a stone, helps Menelaus defend Patroclus’s body, and sends Antilochus to Achilles. After competing with Ulysses for Achilles’s armor and losing it, Ajax kills himself; a flower associated with his grief grows from his blood.

THE ILIAD

Ajax fights bravely when the Greeks defend their ships and knocks Hector down with a huge stone. He later helps defend Patroclus's body and prays for daylight during Jupiter's storm-darkened battle.

Chapter XXI

Ajax and Ulysses were the only claimants to Achilles's armor, but the prize was awarded to Ulysses. Ajax then killed himself, and a flower associated with his name and grief was said to have grown from his blood.
