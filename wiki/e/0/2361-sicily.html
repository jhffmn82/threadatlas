Abstract written by a model

In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Sicily is a fruitful land given by Zeus to Persephone and becomes Ergoteles’s adopted homeland at Himera. In Hecuba and other plays, Sicily contains Mount Ætna, where the Cyclops live, and its coast is reached by Ulysses and his storm-driven crew. In Hecuba and other plays, Sicilia contains the Cyclops’ cave, and Ulysses says he will steer across its waves toward his country. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Sicily is portrayed as a wealthy and strategically vital island repeatedly contested by tyrants, cities, Carthage, and Rome. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Sicily is described as a powerful island whose history, peoples, cities, wars, and eventual subjugation by Rome are recounted. In The Aeneid, Sicily is a hospitable island whose shores receive the Trojan fleet, contain Anchises’s tomb, and become a settlement under Acestes for those Aeneas leaves behind. In The Library, Sicily is an island containing Trapani, where oral traditions about Ulysses and Polyphemus survive. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Sicily, or Trinacria, is the setting of Ceres’s rule, Proserpine’s abduction, and the failure and restoration of fertility.

Sicily in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Sicily first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sicily is a rich, strategically valuable island, described as among the finest in the world, containing Syracuse and many other cities and territories. Its power could aid the acquisition and expansion of a larger dominion, making it repeatedly contested for sovereign command. Under Dionysius the tyrant and then Dionysius the younger, it is ruled through tyranny; Dion returns with mercenaries to restore liberty, travels through the island, gains communities, and fights amid invasions, sea battles, and the capture, sack, and relief of Syracuse. Seditions and tyranny continue until Timoleon gains Syracuse, protects Messana, removes tyrant garrisons, establishes democratic institutions, defeats rivals, and draws many cities and peoples into alliance. After the Carthaginian invasion, his victory brings peace: cities and lands become populated, cultivated, and prosperous. The island then becomes the territory of Agathocles's continuing affairs. His seizure of Syracuse is associated with calamity, bloodshed, and oppression; he fights Carthaginians, Agrigentines, exiles, and other Sicilian forces, while cities revolt, submit, or change sides. Although the Carthaginians control almost all Sicily except Syracuse, Agathocles seeks to reduce the whole island to obedience, leaves for Africa, returns, commits a massacre at Aegesta, defeats Dinocrates, and ultimately gains sovereign power over all Sicily. Later, independent rulers and cities wage frequent wars and plunder one another until Pyrrhus gains much of Sicily and drives the Carthaginians from all but Lilybaeum. Sicily then becomes the principal theater of war between Romans, Carthaginians, and allies, marked by the siege of Lilybaeum and naval and land conflicts. After Carthage's overthrow, sixty years of prosperity and richness give way to slave rebellion. Large numbers of harshly treated slaves, weak jurisdiction, and the influence of powerful Roman slaveholders contribute to devastation, cities taken, Roman armies defeated, and island-wide anarchy, robbery, murder, and violence. Roman forces eventually restore control, but the records end with Sicily still marked by conflict, disorder, and struggles for command.

Sicily in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Sicily first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sicily is an island, described as the greatest and most powerful, large and triangular, with a circumference of four thousand three hundred and sixty furlongs. Some accounts say it was formerly a peninsula connected to the continent by a narrow isthmus, then separated by the sea or an earthquake; another account differs. It was formerly called Trinacria for its shape and Sicania for the Sicanians. Dedicated to Ceres and Proserpine, it is linked with the first growth of wheat and the rape of Proserpine. Its peoples and settlements include Sicani, Sicilians, Greeks, Syracusans, and cities including Syracuse, Gela, Agrigentum, and Motya. Hercules travelled through it with the oxen, fought the Sicani, received honours, and inspired observances; Daedalus, Minos, Cocalus, Cretans, Aristaeus, Venus at Eryx, and Daphnis also figure in its early associations. After royal government was overthrown, Sicily flourished in freedom, peace, and wealth, but civil wars and seditions followed. Much of its later course is defined by contests for its cities and liberty: wars among Sicilian communities, the disastrous Athenian invasion against Syracuse, repeated Carthaginian campaigns and captures, and conflicts involving Dionysius. Cities shifted sides, suffered destruction, fled, or sought restored freedom, while Syracuse remained central. Dionysius expanded power from Syracuse, fought Carthage, brought cities and rulers under influence, and used Sicily as the base for expeditions into Italy. Eventually, Dioclean laws gave way when the Romans brought all Sicily under Roman power.
