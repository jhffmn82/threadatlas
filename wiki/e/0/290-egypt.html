Abstract written by a model

In The Odyssey, Egypt is described as a place near the island of Pharos that is raided by Ulysses and a Cretan stranger, and where Menelaus is detained until sacrifices allow him to continue his voyage. In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Egypt is associated with the Nile, Io's restoration and motherhood, and the flight of Danaus and the Danaids from Aegyptus's forced-marriage decree. In Hecuba and other plays: Egypt is the Nile-watered land where Proteus ruled, his son's palace stands, and Hermes placed Helen while Menelaus and the Greek forces fought at Troy. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Egypt is described as a fertile, populous, heavily defended strategic country whose rulers used its Nile, cities, resources, and fortifications in wars and campaigns. In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Egypt is described as an ancient, fertile, populous country centered on the Nile, source of civilization, religion, laws, arts, sciences, and many historical events. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Egypt is described as a fertile land nourished by the Nile, a place associated with the worship of Isis, the reign of Epaphus, and the provision of grain to Greece. In The Library, Egypt is described as a country named after Egyptus, visited by Dionysus, ruled by Proteus and Busiris, and traversed by Hercules. In Description of Greece: Ptolemy entered Egypt, killed Cleomenes, secured the country with garrisons, and defended it against Perdiccas, Antigonus, and Demetrius.

In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Egypt is portrayed as a fertile Nile-fed land of Osiris associated with agriculture, law, marriage, government, worship, and teachings linked to Pythagoras. In Musical Instruments, Egypt is described as possessing ancient instruments resembling those shown on monuments and as an alleged source of Greek musical influence, while Egyptian influence on Greek music is questioned. In Scientific American Supplement No. 360, November 25, 1882, Egypt is identified as a location where soldiers could use an apparatus to obtain good water and cook food in the field. In Vestiges of the Mayas, or, Facts tending to prove that Communications and Intimate Relations must have existed, in very remote times, between the inhabitants of Mayab and those of Asia and Africa, Egypt is presented as an ancient center of monuments, civilization, knowledge, religion, and possible cultural connections with Mayab.

Egypt in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Egypt first appears

Abstract written by a model

Egypt is a country and a heavily defended, fertile, populous strategic place centered on the Nile, with fortified cities, guarded approaches, waterways, flooding, supplies, and yearly revenues of six thousand talents. It contains Alexandria and the Nile, and its natural fortifications and resources repeatedly make it difficult to invade. Under Acoris it aided Evagoras and prepared against the Persians; an early Persian invasion failed against its defenses. It then underwent struggles over kingship between Tachos and Nectanabis, before Artaxerxes renewed the war, captured its cities, plundered temples and records, demolished walls, and placed government under Pherendates. After Egyptian cities rejected Persian rule, Egypt submitted to Alexander, who settled its affairs and began Alexandria. Ptolemy then took and ruled it, using it as a secure base for forces, fleets, diplomacy, and campaigns in Cyrene, Cyprus, Syria, Phoenicia, and Coele-Syria. Perdiccas invaded to defeat Ptolemy, but the Nile, a repulsed assault, and losses during a crossing caused the expedition to fail. Egypt also resisted Antigonus, whose invasion withdrew in the face of its river defenses and supplies. Later it was defeated by Antiochus III, whose restraint toward defeated Egyptians helped him gain Pelusium and all Egypt. Its rulers and inhabitants remained vulnerable to internal conflict, including hostility toward Ptolemy Physcon, while a Roman delegation judged that its population, fertility, Nile, cities, defenses, and strategic position could support a vast empire under suitable rulers.

Egypt in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · 59 to 29 BC · the work’s page · read it where Egypt first appears

Abstract written by a model

Egypt is a country, long, low, flat, densely inhabited, fertile, and highly productive, with a happy, warm, and temperate climate, though its outer parts are described as intensely hot and its upper region as wilderness. It lies mostly toward the south and is said to be under the south pole. It is bounded south by the Nile’s cataracts and mountains, north by the Mediterranean Sea, east by Lake Serbon, deserts, and marshes, and also borders Libya, Arabia, and Ethiopia. Its sea coast is two thousand furlongs; it had three million people and formerly more than eighteen thousand cities. Memphis is its most famous city, and it contains the Delta and Thebes. The Nile is central: Egypt is said to have become land from Nile-borne deposits of slime and mud from Ethiopia, while its floods cover much of the low country and alter its boundaries. The river, its crocodiles, canals, embankments, and waterworks support protection, fertility, and prosperity. Egypt is also represented as protected by deserts, mountains, marshes, lake, sea, and cataracts. Accounts portray it as an exceptionally ancient source of humans, living things, gods, rites, colonies, laws, arts, sciences, medicine, and social divisions. Egyptians claim inventions including letters, astronomy, and geometry; priests claim Greek rites and practices originated there. Its worship of Osiris, Isis, and sacred animals, its temples, sepulchres, funerary institutions, and laws are prominent. It is credited with civilization and education, yet records also report famine, serpents that depopulated much of it, and harsh mine labor. Its narrated rule begins with Osiris and Isis, followed by many native and foreign rulers. A fable names it after Egyptus; Meris built a lake and waterworks; Sesostris organized thirty-six provinces and strengthened wealth, trade, peace, and security. Ethiopian rule gave way to native kings, twelve nobles, and Psammetichus. It later became a Persian province, revolted and sought Athenian help, but was subdued by a Persian army. In later episodes it is ruled by Ammosis, Mendes, Orus, and Psammetichus, and its king supplies Lacedaemonians with naval equipment and wheat instead of soldiers.
