Abstract written by a model

In The Iliad, the Achaean wall is built as a high defensive structure to protect the Achaeans and their ships, withstands Trojan assaults, and is ultimately destroyed by Apollo, allowing the Trojans to attack the ships and tents. In The Fall of Troy, the Achaean defensive wall protects the Achaeans’ ships and men until Eurypylus’s forces damage it, after which Odysseus, Diomede, Leonteus, and Neoptolemus drive the attackers back.

Achaean wall in The Iliad

Homer · 749 to 724 BC · the work’s page · read it where Achaean wall first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Achaean wall is a high defensive structure built around the Achaeans’ ships, intended as an impregnable bulwark for the army and fleet. Nestor proposes it with strong gates, battlements, stakes, and a deep, wide trench close outside, so that it may shelter the Achaeans and ships and keep Trojan horsemen and foot soldiers away. It is built without hecatombs or divine consent; Neptune objects, while Jove says Neptune may later shatter it, cast it into the sea, and cover the beach with sand. The wall’s fame reaches as far as dawn, and it stands firm while Hector lives, but its lowest point, near the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, becomes a focus of fighting. Under sustained Trojan and Lycian assault, its timbers, breastworks, battlements, and buttresses are attacked. Sarpedon pulls away a battlement, Hector leads the Trojans through the broken gates, and the Achaeans continue defending the breach. The wall is overthrown, freeing the Trojans to attack the ships. Finally, Apollo destroys it with supernatural ease, and Trojans swarm through the breach toward the ships and tents.
