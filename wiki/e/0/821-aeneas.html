  • In Heroides and Amores, Aeneas is the Trojan and Dardanian leader who carries his father toward Italy while leaving Dido despite her love and Carthage's offers.
  • In The Aeneid, Aeneas is a pious Trojan prince, hero, and leader destined to establish a Trojan line in Italy and become an ancestor of Rome.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Aeneas is the pious Trojan survivor who rules the Latin kingdom for three years and is succeeded by Ascanius.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Aeneas is the Trojan warrior who warns Hector against a suspected stratagem and advises reconnaissance before a night attack.
  • In The Rhesus of Euripides, Aeneas is the Trojan prince and Hector's brother who opposes a risky night attack, recommends reconnaissance, and calms the allies.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Aeneas is the son of Aphrodite and Anchises who is destined to rule the Trojans and found a lasting line.
  • In The Iliad, Aeneas is a Trojan warrior, prince, and leader who fights for Troy, receives divine protection, and survives an encounter with Achilles.
  • In The Library, Aeneas is the pious son of Anchises and Aphrodite who flees Troy carrying his father and is spared by the Greeks.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Æneas is the Trojan hero who escapes Troy, reaches Latium, marries into the royal house, and becomes Indiges, ancestor of Rome through his descendants.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Aeneas is a god-aided Trojan warrior and prince who defends Troy before escaping its destruction with Anchises and his son.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Aeneas is the Trojan leader who guides the fugitives from Troy to Italy and triumphs over his foes.

Aeneas in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Aeneas is a pious Trojan prince, hero, and leader: son of Venus and Anchises, descended from Jupiter, father of Ascanius, husband of Creusa, and commander of Trojan survivors. Forced from Troy by fate and protected and aided by Venus, he carries the household gods and is bound for Italy and Latium, where he is destined to establish a lasting Trojan line, rule, and become ancestor of Rome. After Troy falls, he escapes with Anchises and Ascanius, loses Creusa, and leads the exiles through long wanderings. Shipwrecked in Libya, he is welcomed by Dido and loves her, but Jove's command compels him to leave for Latium despite pity and pain. In Sicily he honors Anchises through funeral rites and games; at Cumae he descends alive into the underworld, meets the dead, finds Anchises, and is shown the future Italian race and his destiny. Reaching the destined shore, he founds a Trojan camp, seeks allies, receives divine armor, and leads Trojans and allies in war against Turnus and other foes. He kills Lausus reluctantly and Mezentius in combat, mourns and honors Pallas, offers peace, and agrees to decide the conflict by single combat. Wounded when the truce is broken, he is healed by Venus, returns to battle, defeats Turnus, and kills him after seeing Pallas's golden belt.

BOOK I

Aeneas leads the Trojans toward Italy after years of wandering, but Juno’s storm scatters and damages his fleet. He reaches the Libyan shore with six ships, hunts seven stags for his men, encourages them to endure, and mourns the absent Orontes, Gyas, Lycus, and Amycus. Jupiter declares that Aeneas will wage successful war in Italy, build cities, and eventually be taken up to heaven.

Meantime, in shades of night Aeneas lies:

Aeneas surveys the unknown coast after his fleet takes shelter beneath rocks. He encounters Venus disguised as a huntress and asks what land he has reached and who rules it.

To whom, with sorrow streaming from his eyes,

Aeneas tells Venus, while she is disguised as a nymph, that he was driven from ancient Troy and has been tossed across many seas before reaching the Libyan coast. He enters Dido’s city concealed by Venus’s clouds, recognizes the Trojan paintings, and is reunited with his companions when Dido grants the Trojans refuge. Aeneas presents himself to Dido, thanks her for her kindness, embraces his friends, and attends the feast prepared for him.

Cupid meantime assum’d his form and face,

Aeneas is Dido's princely guest and is seated beside her at the feast. His gifts are admired, and Cupid takes his form and face; through Cupid's work, Dido's thoughts become filled with Aeneas.

BOOK II

Aeneas relates the destruction of Troy and the events of its final night. He describes what he witnessed and experienced as the Greeks deceived the Trojans with Sinon and the wooden horse.

“’Twas in the dead of night, when sleep repairs

Aeneas sees Hector’s ghost, receives the Trojan gods from him, and is warned to escape Troy with them. He joins the defense of the city during the Greek assault, fights with the Trojans, and witnesses the destruction of the palace and the death of Priam.

“Then, not before, I felt my curdled blood

Aeneas sees Helen in Vesta’s temple and resolves to punish her, but Venus intervenes and commands him to abandon this fruitless vengeance. He returns to Anchises, prepares the household’s escape, carries his father, leads Ascanius, and tries to guide Creusa through the burning city. After losing Creusa, he searches for her throughout Troy and finally sees her ghost, who tells him to continue toward Latium.

BOOK III

Aeneas leads the Trojan survivors from the ruins of Troy and recounts their voyage. He settles briefly in Thrace and Crete, learns through the household gods that Italy is their true destination, encounters the Harpies, and visits Helenus and Andromache at Buthrotus.

“The prophet first with sacrifice adores

Aeneas consults Helenus, receives instructions for reaching Italy, and continues the Trojan voyage with Anchises, Ascanius, and the Trojans. He rescues Achaemenides from the Cyclopian shores and recounts his wanderings to the queen.

BOOK IV

Aeneas is admired by Dido for his valor, actions, birth, appearance, and suffering at Troy. He joins the hunt, shelters with Dido in the cave, helps raise Carthage's defenses, and is then rebuked by Mercury for lingering in Libya instead of pursuing the future city assigned to him.

The pious prince was seiz’d with sudden fear;

Aeneas secretly orders the fleet repaired and the men embarked, intending to leave the Tyrian court. He tells Dido that the Delphian oracle, fate, Jove, and the appearance of the herald of the gods require him to seek the Latian lands. Though he mourns and feels pity and love for Dido, he obeys the will of Jove and keeps his purpose.

The wretched queen, pursued by cruel fate,

Aeneas sleeps more easily than Dido and prepares the Trojans for an early departure. After Hermes warns him again, he orders the fleet to sail and leaves without knowing that Dido is about to kill herself.

BOOK V

Aeneas is driven by the storm to Sicily, where Acestes welcomes him. He honors Anchises with sacrifices, conducts the funeral games, and awards prizes to the contestants.

The race thus ended, and rewards bestow’d,

Aeneas supplies equal arms for Entellus and Dares, stops the gauntlet fight when Entellus's fury increases, and tells Dares that divine strength has favored Entellus. He later honors Acestes with a goblet, proclaims him victor of the archery contest, and orders Periphantes to bring Ascanius and the boys for the riding game.

Thus far the sacred sports they celebrate:

Aeneas responds to the burning fleet by praying to Jove and offering himself as a sacrifice if the Trojan vessels can be saved. He accepts the counsel of Nautes and Anchises, leaves the old and fearful behind in Sicily, establishes a settlement with Acestes, and resumes the voyage toward Italy. He later mourns Palinurus and takes control of the helm.

BOOK VI

Aeneas reaches Cumae, visits the temple and the Sibyl’s cave, and asks Apollo for guidance about settling the Trojan race in Italy. He requests passage to the underworld so that he may see his dead father Anchises, finds the golden bough with the aid of two doves, and performs the rites for Misenus before beginning the descent.

Ye realms, yet unreveal’d to human sight,

Aeneas enters the Underworld with the Sibyl to seek his father, Anchises, in the Elysian grove. He meets Palinurus, Dido, and Deiphobus, crosses the Styx, and proceeds toward Pluto’s palace.

Where long extended plains of pleasure lay:

Aeneas enters the Elysian fields with the Sibyl and asks about the souls gathered at Lethe. He meets Anchises, tries three times to embrace him, views the future Italian race, and receives instruction about Rome’s destiny, future wars, and customs before returning to his ships.

BOOK VII

Aeneas leads the Trojans past the shores of Circe and into the Tiber. After the Trojans consume their cakes and realize the omen, he identifies the place as the destined end of their wandering and orders them to explore the land while preparing to establish a town.

BOOK VIII

Aeneas is troubled by the preparations of his enemies but receives guidance from Tiber. He travels by ship to Evander, asks for assistance, and is welcomed as a friend.

Now night had shed her silver dews around,

Aeneas receives heavenly armor from Venus and recognizes the thunder, lightning, and trumpets as a divine sign of promised aid. He interprets the omen as a summons to war and declares that Turnus will pay for his broken faith.

BOOK IX

Aeneas is absent from the Trojan camp while seeking the Arcadian prince and raising Tuscan forces. His absence leaves the camp vulnerable, and Nisus and Euryalus plan to reach him and urge his return.

BOOK X

Aeneas leaves the Trojan camp, visits the Tuscan camp, and asks Tarchon for aid against Mezentius and Turnus. He leads the Tuscan and Trojan forces back by sea, then lands and kills several enemies, including Theron, Lichas, Gyas, Cisseus, Pharos, Maeon, and Dryops.

But, in another part, th’ Arcadian horse

Aeneas learns of Pallas’s death and, remembering Evander, Pallas, and his hospitable ties, cuts through the enemy ranks. He captures four sons of Sulmo for sacrifice, rejects Magus’s offer of ransom, and kills him. Aeneas also kills Emonides, Anxur, Tarquitus, several other warriors, Lucagus, and Liger before the Trojan forces break out of their camp.

Meantime, by Jove’s impulse, Mezentius arm’d,

Aeneas confronts Mezentius and wounds him in the thigh after his spear pierces Mezentius’s shield and armor. When Lausus intervenes, Aeneas kills the young man, then honors him by leaving his arms untouched and offering his body to his parents. He later defeats Mezentius and kills him after hearing his request for burial.

BOOK XI

Aeneas erects a trophy from Mezentius’s arms and urges his troops to press toward the trembling town. He mourns Pallas, orders the young prince’s body sent to Evander, grants the Latins a truce for burying their dead, and says that Turnus must fight him for Lavinia and peace.

Thus, full of anxious thought, he summons all

Aeneas is praised by Diomede as a warrior equal to Hector and especially excellent in reverence toward the gods. Turnus calls him a Phrygian pirate and accepts his challenge to single combat.

While they debate, nor these nor those will yield,

Aeneas draws his forces into the field, moves his camp, and leads the Trojans toward the city along the river side. He sends his light horse ahead while taking a larger route through steep ascents and thorny brakes.

BOOK XII

Aeneas agrees to settle the war through single combat and swears that, if he wins, the Trojans will join the Italians under Latinus’s rule while he asks only for altars for his gods. When the peace is broken, he enters the fighting unarmed and calls on both sides to stop, but an unknown arrow strikes and wounds him.

When Turnus saw the Trojan quit the plain,

Aeneas is wounded and returns to battle after Venus heals him. He leads the Trojans against the Latins, kills several warriors, seeks Turnus, and finally orders an assault on the city when Turnus cannot be found.

Now Turnus rolls aloof o’er empty plains,

Aeneas attacks the town and hastens to meet Turnus when he hears the challenge. Though his recent wound slows him, he pursues Turnus, wounds him with a spear, and kills him after seeing Pallas's golden belt.

Aeneas in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Aeneas is a Trojan warrior, prince, and leader: son of Anchises and Venus, loved by Venus, and a mainstay of the Trojans and Lycians in fighting and counsel. He is among the best Trojan warriors with Hector, leads the Dardanians, owns Trojan horses, and is fated, with his descendants, to reign over the Trojans. Although aggrieved because Priam son of Dardanus did not give him his due honour, he repeatedly serves the Trojan cause. He joins Pandarus against Diomed, drives their chariot, and is struck in the hip by a stone. Near death, he is carried from battle by Venus, hidden by Apollo, healed at Pergamus by Latona and Diana, and returns to kill Crethon and Orsilochus. He is urged by Helenus to rally the Trojans, fights with Deiphobus against Idomeneus's group, and kills Aphareus. Later he joins Hector in pursuing the retreating Achaeans and seeking Achilles' horses. Finally, encouraged by Apollo, he confronts Achilles, declares his ancestry, casts a spear, and lifts a stone; Neptune rescues him and tells him to avoid Achilles until Achilles is dead. His course is marked by command, battle, wounds, divine protection, recovery, and survival.

At Delos, Anius receives him; he consults Apollo’s oracle, exchanges gifts, and receives a goblet. Aeneas in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Aeneas is a Trojan leader, chief, hero, and warrior, son of Anchises, who commands and guides the Trojans in their search for a new home. During Troy’s destruction, he escapes carrying Anchises and leading his son, while losing his wife. He leads the fugitives through many lands and dangers toward Italy, briefly accepts Dido’s hospitality and affection, then leaves when Jupiter commands him to continue. Seeking Anchises, he follows the Sibyl into the infernal regions and receives a revelation about souls and his people’s future in Italy. After reaching the Tiber, he accepts Italy as home through the hunger omen, seeks Latinus’s alliance, follows Tiber’s guidance to Evander, and becomes the fated leader of the Etruscans. Protected by Vulcan’s armor, he returns with allies, rescues the Trojan camp, kills Lausus, Mezentius, and Turnus, triumphs over his foes, and marries Lavinia.

Chapter XXIV

Aeneas escapes burning Troy carrying his father Anchises, leading his son, and losing his wife in the confusion. He leads the Trojan fugitives through Thrace, Delos, Crete, Epirus, Sicily, and Carthage toward Italy, surviving the Harpies, Polyphemus, and storms. He accepts Dido’s hospitality and affection for a time, but leaves her when Jupiter commands him to resume his voyage; later, he takes charge of the ship after Palinurus falls overboard and seeks the Sibyl’s help in reaching the dead.

Chapter XXV

Aeneas follows the Sibyl into the infernal regions to see his father, Anchises. He encounters Palinurus, Dido, Trojan and Grecian warriors, the condemned, and the blessed, and receives from Anchises a revelation about souls and the future establishment of his people in Italy.

Chapter XXVI

Aeneas reaches the mouth of the Tiber after parting from the Sibyl and accepts the omen of eating the biscuit crusts as a sign that he has reached his promised land. He sends messengers to Latinus, receives his alliance, and later follows Tiber's guidance to Evander. Aeneas allies with Evander and Pallas and travels with them to the Etruscan camp, where Tarchon receives him as the destined leader.

NISUS AND EURYALUS

Aeneas returns with Etrurian allies and rescues the beleaguered Trojan camp. He kills Lausus and Mezentius in battle and finally kills Turnus after seeing Pallas's belt.
