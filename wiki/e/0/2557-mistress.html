mistress in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

The mistress is a peerless beauty, beloved by the speaker and desired by wealthy young men, yet presented as a courtesan or prostitute. Dipsas urges her to profit from several lovers through calculated favors, refusals, and emotional manipulation, despite her lack of fitting wealth and dress. She shares blissful nighttime union with the speaker, whose longing delays his wish for dawn and whose exclusion from her door redirects his poetry to love. But she also brings continual jealousy and grief: she deceives him, signals and kisses a rival, and her newly artful kisses imply infidelity.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

ELEGY VII.

The damsel is praised as beautiful but told that she lacks suitable wealth and clothing. Dipsas instructs her to entertain multiple lovers, manipulate their emotions, refuse or grant favors strategically, and secure presents and payments from them.

ELEGY XIII.

The mistress lies beside the speaker in the soft arms of sleep and is deliciously united with him. Her early rising is the chief reason he begs Aurora not to hasten.

BOOK THE SECOND ELEGY I.

Naso’s mistress shut her door against him while he was preparing to sing of the battles of heaven. Her rejection caused him to abandon Jove and resume writing affectionate Elegies, whose gentle words eventually prevailed.

ELEGY V.

The mistress is accused of acting falsely toward the speaker. She communicates with the rival through gestures, coded words, and glances, kisses him passionately, blushes when confronted, and then gives the speaker unusually skillful kisses.
