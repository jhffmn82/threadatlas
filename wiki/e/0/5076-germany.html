Germany in Laurence Sterne in Germany

Harvey Waterman Thayer · 1905

Germany is presented as a literary setting whose reception and transformation of Sterne and Yorick form the central story. It was described as dependent on foreign literary models while gradually seeking independent national ideals; political, social, and intellectual conditions made its literature less favorable than England’s to humor and vigorous narrative. Yet it proved highly receptive to British literature in the 1760s and gave Sterne unusually serious consideration and recognized merit, even more than England in its reception of spurious Sterne works. Before the Sentimental Journey, knowledge of Sterne spread slowly and unevenly through translations, journals, reviews, and individual readers. A German translation of Tristram Shandy appeared in Berlin and Stralsund in 1763, but early interest in that work remained limited. Its distinctly British humor was difficult to transfer, whereas intelligent lovers of English literature knew and appreciated Yorick. The decisive change came when the Sentimental Journey, published in London on February 27, 1768, was received almost immediately. It won broad popularity and commanding influence because its sentimental experience suited German literary and emotional developments. Translations, including Bode’s continuation, and related treatments of animals, servants, and human relations reinforced this welcome. Germany also received translations, reviews, imitations, letters, sermons, novels, and questionable writings attributed to Sterne; sympathetic acceptance of doubtful works helped create a distinctive image of Yorick. It learned of Ferriar’s investigations chiefly through Friedrich Nicolai’s 1795 résumé. Ultimately Germany treated Sterne as a foreign prophet, mentor, and authority on emotional and humane conduct. It developed sentimentalism in response to Yorick’s sentiments and adopted Sterne-associated forms and habits: discontinuity, typographical eccentricity, direct address to readers, hobby-horse theory, traveler classifications, and the belief that trifles determine major events. Writers, critics, translators, and readers repeatedly invoked Sterne and Yorick, produced sentimental travel narratives, and debated imitation, originality, and value. This influence spread through German life and letters and reached Denmark and Livonia.
