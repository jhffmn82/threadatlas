Atlantides in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

is_a daughters of Atlas [collective name] The Atlantides were Atlas’s seven daughters: Maia, Electra, Taygeta, Asterope, Merope, Halcyone, and Celseno. They bore sons by heroic princes and gods, becoming ancestors of nations and cities; after their deaths they were worshipped as goddesses and placed among the stars as the Pleiades. Nymphs were also commonly called Atlantides because nympha was a general term for women in that country.

Pleiades in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Pleiades are stars, the daughters of Atlas, also called Peleiades in The Astronomy though mortals call them Pleiades. Their movements govern agricultural timing: rising begins harvest, setting marks ploughing, reappearance after forty nights and days signals sharpening the sickle, and their climb ends vineyard digging. They are stormy, disappear or hide, and plunge into the misty sea to escape Orion’s strength.

The Pleiades signal the seasons of agricultural work. Their rising marks the beginning of harvest, their setting marks ploughing, and their reappearance after forty nights and days indicates when the sickle should be sharpened; when they climb up the plants, vineyard digging should cease.

THE ASTRONOMY

The Pleiades are called Peleiades in The Astronomy, although mortals call them Pleiades. They are described as stormy and as going down or hiding away.

Pleiades in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Pleiades are a group of seven daughters, generally identified as Alcyone, Merope, Celaeno, Electra, Sterope, Taygete, and Maia. They were born at Cyllene in Arcadia and are described as daughters of Atlas and Pleione; Atlas was commonly said to be their father, giving them the name Atlantides. One account instead makes them daughters of the queen of the Amazons, and the origin of the name Pleiades was disputed among the ancients. Their names were generally agreed upon, though the Scholiast on Theocritus offered a different list except for Maia. They are discussed across numerous works, from Phaenomena and Catasterismi to Astronomica, Fasti, and later commentaries.

the Pleiads in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

The Pleiads are seven daughters of Atlas and nymphs in Diana’s train. Pursued by Orion, they are changed by Jupiter into pigeons and made a constellation; Electra leaves, so only six remain visible.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter XIX

The Pleiads were seven daughters of Atlas and nymphs in Diana’s train. When Orion pursued them, Jupiter changed them into pigeons and made them a constellation. Only six are visible because Electra left her place rather than witness the ruin of Troy, and the remaining sisters have looked pale ever since.
