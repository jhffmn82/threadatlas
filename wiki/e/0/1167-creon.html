  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Creon is the king of Corinth who accepts Jason’s marriage to Glauce, orders Medea to leave with one day to prepare, and is consumed by the fire she sets in the palace.
  • In Medea, Creon is the king of Corinth and father of Jason’s intended bride who orders Medea and her children into exile, grants them one final day, and dies embracing his daughter after her poisoned robes kill her.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Creon is a ruler and family figure in Thebes and Corinth who offers the Theban throne to whoever solves the Sphinx’s riddle, refuses to sacrifice his son Menoeceus, mourns his death, banishes Oedipus, forbids Polynices’ burial, orders Antigone to marry Haemon, and, as king of Corinth, expels Medea before dying with Glauce from her poisoned robes.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Creon rules Corinth and is poisoned by Medea; after his death, his relatives kill Medea’s sons and spread the story that she killed both them and Creon.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Creon is the king of Corinth and father of Glauce or Creüsa who is consumed by fire with his daughter when Medea burns his palace.
  • In The Library, Creon is the son of Menoeceus, father of Glauce, king of Corinth, and successor to Laius in Thebes who offers the kingdom and Jocasta to whoever solves the Sphinx’s riddle, aids Amphitryon, forbids burial of the Argive dead, has Antigone interred alive, and dies rescuing Glauce from her poisoned robe’s fire.

Creon in Medea

Euripides · -0430

Creon is the ruler and king of Corinth, father of the princess who is to marry Jason. Fearing Medea will harm his daughter, Jason, or himself, he orders Medea and her two children into exile, yet grants them one final day in Corinth under threat of death after sunrise. As the bride’s father and one of Medea’s intended victims, he is caught in her revenge: he finds and embraces his dead daughter, is trapped by her poisoned robes, and dies with her.

MEDEA

Creon orders Medea and her two children to leave Corinth because he fears that Medea will harm his daughter, Jason, or himself. Although he distrusts her, he grants her one day to remain, warning that she or either child will die if found in Corinth after the next sunrise.

MEDEA.

The bride's father is included among the three people Medea intends to kill. He is also associated with the house from which Medea has been hunted away.

The king finds his dead daughter and embraces her. The poisoned robes cling to him as he tries to rise, and he dies with her.

Creon in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Creon is a ruler and family figure whose actions center on Thebes and Corinth. He is Jocasta’s brother, Menoeceus’s son, maternal uncle of Eteocles, and father of Menoeceus. After Laius’s death, he offers the throne and Jocasta’s marriage to whoever solves the Sphinx’s riddle. In Thebes, he advises Eteocles to defend the gates prudently and seeks Tiresias’s counsel to preserve the city. When Tiresias declares that Menoeceus must die for Thebes, Creon refuses to sacrifice his son and tries to send him away, but Menoeceus chooses death for the city. Creon mourns him, prepares his body, learns of Jocasta’s and her sons’ deaths, banishes Oedipus, and forbids burial for Polynices under threat of death, despite Antigone’s appeals. He also orders Antigone to prepare to marry Haemon. In Corinth, Creon is king and father of Glauce, the princess offered to Jason as a new bride. Fearing Medea will harm Glauce, he expels Medea and her children, though he grants them one final day. Medea’s poisoned robes kill Glauce; Creon embraces her and dies with her.

Narrative, section by section

8 sections; each heading opens the text

THE PHŒNICIAN VIRGINS.

Creon is Jocasta's brother and the son of Menoeceus. After Laius's death, he proclaimed that the throne and Jocasta's marriage would be awarded to whoever solved the Sphinx's riddle.

ETEOCLES, CHORUS, MESSENGER.

Creon advises Eteocles to rely on prudence and to appoint seven commanders to defend the gates. After hearing Tiresias' prophecy that Menoeceus must die to save Thebes, Creon refuses to sacrifice his son and attempts to send him away to Ætolia and Thesprotia. He is left lamenting when Menoeceus deceives him and chooses to die for the city.

MESSENGER, JOCASTA, CHORUS.

Creon mourns his son, who has died for Thebes, and comes to have the body washed and laid out. He learns that Jocasta and both sons are dead and asks the Messenger how the brothers' combat took place.

ANTIGONE, CREON, CHORUS.

Creon orders Oedipus to leave Thebes and declares that Polynices must remain unburied beyond the borders. He threatens death for anyone who crowns or covers Polynices' corpse, refuses Antigone's requests to honor the body, and orders her to prepare for marriage to Haemon.

MEDEA.

Creon, king of Corinth, orders Medea and her children to leave the country because he fears that Medea will harm Glauce. He grants Medea one additional day to remain, warning that she and her children will die if they are still in Corinth after the following dawn.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Creon, the monarch of Corinth, gives his daughter in marriage to Jason and orders Medea to leave Corinth.

JASON, MEDEA, CHORUS.

Creon is the father of Jason's new bride and has ordered Medea and her children to leave the country. He rushes to his dying daughter, embraces her, and dies after being caught and torn by the poisoned robes.

JASON, CHORUS.

Creon is described as the one who proposed the princess’s match with Jason and expected to drive Medea from the land with impunity. Medea holds him responsible, along with Jason and the princess, for the circumstances leading to her revenge.

Apollodorus · 0070~/0200~

Creon is a ruler and father: son of Menoeceus, father of Glauce, king of Corinth, and successor to Laius in Thebes. After Laius’s burial, he ruled Thebes, offered its kingdom and Laius’s wife Jocasta to whoever solved the Sphinx’s riddle, and lost Haemon to the Sphinx. He purified and aided Amphitryon after the Cadmea was freed of the vixen, joining the attack on the Tafian islands. Later he forbade burial of Argive dead, set watchmen, and had Antigone interred alive for burying Polynices. In Corinth he betrothed Glauce to Jason and died rescuing her from her poisoned robe’s fire.

τῶν ταύρων οὐκ ἐδίδου τὸ δέρας Αἰήτης, ἐβούλετο

Creon is the king of Corinth and betroths his daughter Glauce to Jason. He dies in the fierce fire caused by Glauce’s poisoned robe when he goes to help her.

THE LIBRARY, II. tv. 6-7

Creon purified Amphitryon at Thebes and agreed to assist his expedition against the Teleboans if Amphitryon first rid the Cadmea of the vixen. He joined Amphitryon in the attack on the Tafian islands.

Γαμεῖ δὲ Ζῆθος μὲν Θήβην, ad ἧς ἡ πόλιες

Creon, son of Menoeceus, succeeded to the kingdom after Laius’s burial. During his rule, he proclaimed that whoever solved the Sphinx’s riddle would receive the kingdom and Laius’s wife, and he lost his son Haemon to the Sphinx.

VII. Κρέων δὲ τὴν Θηβαίων βασιλείαν παραλα-

After succeeding to the kingdom of Thebes, Creon cast out the Argive dead unburied and ordered that no one bury them. He set watchmen, and after Antigone buried Polynices, he had her interred alive.
