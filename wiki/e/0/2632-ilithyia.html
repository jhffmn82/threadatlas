Ilithyia

deity · 4 works · 14 quoted facts · 9 connections
Also called: Eulinus, Ilithyias, Ilithyïa, Lucina, the Goddess who presides over births
  • In The Amores, or Amours, Lucina compassionately aids females in labor and is invoked to favor Corinna’s childbirth.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Lucina is a goddess presiding over births who delays Alcmena’s labor at Juno’s urging and transforms Galanthis into a weasel after Galanthis deceives her.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Ilithyia is a deity of childbirth honored in temples and represented by several statues; Lycian Olen calls her Eulinus, identifies her with Fate, and says she predates Cronos.
  • In Description of Greece, Ilithyia, surnamed the Olympian, receives votive offerings at her temple on Mount Cronos and is jointly worshipped with Sosipolis for introducing him into the world.

Lucina in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Lucina is a goddess presiding over births who, influenced by Juno, delays Alcmena’s labor with charms until Galanthis deceives her, after which she transforms Galanthis into a weasel.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

FABLE III. [IX.273-323]

Ilithyïa, the Goddess who presides over childbirth, comes to Alcmena but acts on Juno’s behalf. She sits before Alcmena’s door, uses a magic spell to impede the delivery, and later transforms Galanthis into a weasel for deceiving her.

CHAPTER XVII.

Ilithyia was honored in a temple at the northern end of Mount Cronos and was surnamed the Olympian. She was worshipped jointly with Sosipolis because the Eleans believed that she had introduced the child who became Sosipolis into the world.

Ilithyia in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Ilithyia is a deity of childbirth, represented in the Iliad and honored by a notable temple at Clitor and a market-place temple linked to Auge’s birth. Her images include a veiled wooden statue with marble features, a marble statue by Euclides, and a statue at Ægium holding a torch and extending one hand. The torch may signify childbirth’s fiery pains or bringing children to light. Lycian Olen calls her Eulinus, identifies her with Fate, and says she predates Cronos.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIII.

Ilithyia's statue at Ægium held a torch in one hand while the other hand was stretched out straight. The narrator suggests that the torches may symbolize the fiery pains of childbirth or Ilithyia's bringing children to the light.

CHAPTER XXI.

The town of Clitor has a notable temple to Ilithyia. Homer says there are several Ilithyias without specifying their number, while the Lycian Olen identifies her with Fate, says she is older than Cronos, and calls her Eulinus.

CHAPTER XLVIII.

Ilithyia is associated with the temple in the market-place where Auge is represented on her knees. Auge is said to have given birth there, linking the place with Ilithyia.
