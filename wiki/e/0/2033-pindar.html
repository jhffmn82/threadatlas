Pindar in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Pindar is a poet, author, composer, and singer of victory odes: a friend of winners in the games whose songs illuminate their cities, recall their achievements, and seek the Muses’ power to celebrate them. He praises Zeus and rejects the story of Pelops’ ivory shoulder because it would accuse the gods of an infamous act; he warns that beautiful but untrue tales do not endure. He also expresses ideas about future life and seems influenced by Pythagoreanism. His work unfolds through praise and promised repayment. He celebrates Epharmostos’ wrestling victories. After admitting he had forgotten a promised strain for Agesidamos, he pledges a longer celebration in Agesidamos’ Italian home and composes a victory song for Agesidamos and the Lokrians. He sings for Hieron of Syracuse, praising Hieron’s victories and city and comparing his own praise to a javelin thrown beyond rivals. From Thebes he sends an ode to Syracuse, honoring Hieron’s valor, wealth, wisdom, and directness while condemning flattery, slander, bitterness, and crooked conduct. He composes for Arkesilas’s chariot victory. At twenty, in what is identified as his earliest entire surviving poem, he praises Hippokleas, his family, and Thessaly. He writes for Herodotos of Thebes, postponing an intended song for Apollo to celebrate Herodotos, Asopodoros, Thebes, and hoped-for future crowns. Finally, he comes from the house of Zeus with a bright song for Bromios, attends the Nemean games in winter, and foresees approaching spring and the vernal Dionysia from tokens of a branch.

10 sections; each heading opens the text

notes

Pindar refuses to accept the legend of Pelops' ivory shoulder because it would make the gods guilty of an infamous act. He says that people may believe untrue tales for the beauty of their form, but such tales will not stand the test of time.

notes

Pindar expressed ideas about future life, particularly in the fragments of his Dirges that remain. He seems to have been influenced by Pythagoreanism.

IX.

Pindar presents himself as sending song to illuminate the city of his friends and to praise Epharmostos. He recalls Epharmostos' victories and invokes the Muses' power to celebrate them.

XI.

Pindar admits that he had forgotten his promise to compose a sweet strain for Agesidamos. He seeks to repay the debt with usury by composing a victory song for Agesidamos and the Lokrians.

I.

Pindar speaks as the singer who praises Hieron, his victories, and his city. He compares his own praise to throwing a bronze-tipped javelin beyond his rivals and asks the Muse to honor the house of Deinomenes.

II.

Pindar presents himself as sending the ode from bright Thebes across the sea to Syracuse. He praises Hieron's victory, protection of the Lokrians, military valor, wealth, wisdom, and directness, while condemning bitter speech, flattery, slander, and crooked conduct.

X.

Pindar composed the ode for Hippokleas, at the request of Thorax. He praises Hippokleas, his family, and Thessaly, and the text identifies this as the earliest entire surviving poem by Pindar, written when he was twenty years old.

Isthmian Ode I

Pindar postpones a song intended for Apollo at Delos in order first to praise Herodotos of Thebes and his victory. He celebrates Herodotos, his father Asopodoros, Thebes, Isthmus, and the prospect of Herodotos winning further crowns.

FRAGMENT OF A DITHYRAMB,

The singer (speaker) has come from the house of Zeus with a bright song a second time for Bromios. The singer (speaker) has also come to sing the offspring of the Highest and of Kadmeän mothers.

notes

Pindar had been at the Nemean games in winter. He meant that he, like the priest of the temple, could foresee from the tokens of the branch that spring and the vernal Dionysia were approaching.

Pindar in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Pindar was a poem-writer who composed in Doric, visited Thespiæ when young, and was said to begin writing after bees left honey on his lips. Though defeated by Corinna at Thebes, reportedly because she used Aeolic, he became famous throughout Greece. The Pythian Priestess at Delphi granted him firstfruits with Apollo; he also dedicated a statue of Hermes Agoræus and sent a Hymn honoring Ammon. In old age Proserpine appeared in his dream; he died before its tenth day, and afterward a related woman recorded his Hymn to Proserpine from a dream.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXII.

Pindar was defeated by Corinna at Thebes. The text suggests that Corinna’s use of Aeolic, rather than the Doric used by Pindar, helped her win.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Pindar began writing poems after bees settled on him while he slept and made honey on his lips. When he became famous throughout Greece, the Pythian Priestess proclaimed at Delphi that he should receive an equal share of the firstfruits with Apollo. In old age he dreamed that Proserpine told him he had not celebrated her, and he died before the tenth day after the dream; afterward, a woman related to him dreamed that he recited a Hymn to Proserpine and wrote it down when she awoke.
