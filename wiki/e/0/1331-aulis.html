Aulis

Also called: Bœotian land, Euboean Aulis, Eubœan Aulis, bay-like wing of Eubœa, fishy Aulis, ports, ports of this land, sands of Aulis, this new Aulis, this place, Αὐλίδα, Αὐλίδι, Αὐλίδος

Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Aulis is where the Greek leaders meet and sacrifice before the expedition and where Artemis’s stormy winds later prevent the forces from sailing. In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, Aulis is the harbor where the Greek army and fleet gather, where Iphigenia is brought for sacrifice, and where the windbound expedition awaits release toward Troy. In Iphigenia in Tauris, Aulis is the site where Agamemnon gathers the fleet, vows Iphigenia to Artemis, and attempts to sacrifice her before Artemis rescues her. In The Library, Aulis is where the Greek army assembles, sacrifices, and encounters a serpent omen, and where the windless fleet nearly sacrifices Iphigenia before Artemis carries her away. In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Euboean Aulis is the Bœotian port where contrary winds detain the Greek fleet, and where the Greeks witness an omen and bring Iphigenia to the altar. In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Aulis is a place near the Euripus in Bœotia, containing a temple of Artemis and the remains of an ancient settlement.

Aulis in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · 437 BC · the work’s page · read it where Aulis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Aulis is a Greek harbor and the setting of the Greek expedition against Troy. The Greek army and fleet gather there: ships fill its narrow harbors, while soldiers occupy Diana’s grove, flowery meads, and tents. Separated from Euripus by a narrow strait, it is marked by stillness: the Euripus is silent and calm, the winds fail, and the fleet cannot sail. This detention becomes bound to Iphigenia. She is brought to Aulis under the pretense of marriage to Achilles, while Agamemnon plans her sacrifice so that the ships may depart. At Diana’s altar, the sacrifice is prepared; accounts describe Iphigenia as threatened, intended or attempted to be sacrificed, and nearly killed like a calf. Calchas declares that the army should leave Aulis and cross the Ægean once this condition is met. In later recollection, Aulis gains fame through the intended sacrifice, though Iphigenia is said to have been conveyed away from it. Thus Aulis unfolds from a crowded but windbound military harbor into the place of Iphigenia’s danger and the expedition’s hoped-for release toward Troy.

Aulis in Iphigenia in Tauris

Euripides · 413 to 411 BC · the work’s page · read it where Aulis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Aulis is the site where Agamemnon gathers the Greek fleet for Troy, but weather holds it there. He vows Iphigenia to Artemis and attempts to sacrifice her; she is believed slain, yet Artemis rescues her before the sacrifice is completed. Later, messages and Orestes recall Aulis as the place of her supposed death.

Aulis in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · about AD 155 to 175 · the work’s page · read it where Aulis first appears

Abstract written by a model

Aulis is a place near the Euripus, where Eubœa separates from Bœotia, near the temple of Mycalessian Demeter. Said to be named after Ogygus’s daughter, it has a temple of Artemis and notable remains; few potters live there, while Tanagra’s people inhabit its district.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIX.

Aulis lies near the place where the Euripus separates Eubœa from Bœotia and is said to have been named after the daughter of Ogygus. It contains a temple of Artemis, the remains of the plane-tree mentioned by Homer, a well, a brazen threshold associated with Agamemnon's tent, and palm trees. Aulis has few inhabitants, who are potters, and its district is inhabited by the people of Tanagra.
