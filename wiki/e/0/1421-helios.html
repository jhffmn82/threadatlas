Abstract written by a model

In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Helios is a bright, tireless, deathless god, son of Hyperion and Euryphaessa, watchman of gods and men, charioteer across Heaven, and witness who tells Demeter of Persephone’s abduction. In The Odyssey, Sun-god Hyperion owns the Sun’s cattle and is identified as the father of Phaethusa and Lampetie by Neaera. In The Odyssey, the sun-god Hyperion owns cattle and sheep on Thrinacian island, becomes enraged when Ulysses’s men kill them, and asks Jove to punish the men. In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Helios is the god who gives light and the divine lord of Rhodes, receiving the island and fathering seven sons with Rhodos. In The Library, the Sun is a deity who owns cattle on Thrinacia, rides a daily chariot, reverses its course to establish Atreus’s rule, reports the slaughter of its cattle to Zeus, and acknowledges Phaethon as its son. In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, the Sun, identified with Phoebus, acknowledges Phaeton as his son, grants his requested gift, warns him against driving the chariot, and prepares him to do so.

Helios in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · about 699 BC · the work’s page · read it where Helios first appears

Abstract written by a model

Helios is a bright, tireless, deathless god: the son of Hyperion and Euryphaessa, a watchman of gods and men. He shines upon mortals and immortals, with dazzling rays, streaming bright locks, a far-seen face, and a golden helmet. Resting at Heaven’s highest point, he rides a golden-yoked chariot drawn by stallions down through Heaven to Ocean. From the bright upper air, he hears Persephone’s cries, witnesses her abduction, and tells Demeter that Zeus gave her to Hades.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

II. TO DEMETER

Helios, the bright son of Hyperion and watcher of gods and mortals, hears Persephone’s cries and sees what happened. He tells Demeter that Zeus gave Persephone to Hades and that Hades carried her away.

XXXI. TO HELIOS

Helios is the tireless, godlike son of Hyperion and Euryphaessa. He rides a golden-yoked chariot drawn by stallions, shines upon mortals and immortals, and gazes piercingly from beneath his golden helmet. After resting at heaven’s highest point, he drives his chariot and horses down through heaven to Ocean.

Sun in The Library

Apollodorus · AD 1 to 200 · the work’s page · read it where Sun first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sun is a deity with cattle on Thrinacia, a palace, and a chariot used daily. It reverses its course and sets in the east, establishing Atreus’s claim to rule after Thyestes’s agreement. When Ulysses’s companions slaughter its cattle, it reports them to Zeus. It acknowledges Phaethon as its son and reluctantly lets him drive the chariot; a similar son in Indian accounts causes destructive heat.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

ιῖιο Ὅτι viol Πέλοπος Πιτθεὺς ᾿Ατρεὺς Θυέστης

The sun reverses its course and sets in the east after Thyestes agrees that Atreus should rule if such a reversal occurs. This reversal establishes Atreus’s claim to the kingdom.

TOY Σειρήνων. αἱ δὲ Σειρῆνες ἦσαν Αχελῴου

The Sun owns the cattle grazing on Thrinacia. After Ulysses’s companions slaughter and eat some of them, the Sun reports the deed to Zeus.

XI.—PHAETHON AND THE CHARIOT OF THE SUN

The Sun acknowledges Phaethon as his son and reluctantly grants his request to drive the chariot. In the Indian accounts, the Sun is also the father of a boy who attempts to take his place and causes destructive heat.
