Olympia in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Olympia is a gracious dwelling place of Oinomaos and Pelops at Pisa by the Alpheos, containing Altis, the sacred grove. It is the sacred site of Olympic games, Zeus’s altar, Pelops’s tomb, golden olive-leaves, and crowns for athletic victory. Called the mother of gold-crowned contests and queen of truth, it receives and celebrates triumphs: Psaumis brings honor from it to Kamarina; Agesias, Alkimedon, Epharmostos, Agesidamos, and Ergoteles win there. Its contests glorify Opous and give winners ample glory. The records culminate in its games, founded by Herakles, the first Olympiad, and Pindar’s sight of Agesidamos victorious beside the altar.

Olympia in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Olympia is a place beyond Scillus across the Alpheus, immediately associated with the river, the Olympic games, sacred rites, athletic training, and a dense collection of temples, groves, monuments, and offerings. Its central sacred area is the Altis, the grove of Zeus, containing the Temple of Zeus, the temple of Hera, the grove of Pelops, a great altar, many lesser altars, treasuries, statues, paintings, and votive gifts. Outside Altis stood the workshop of Phidias; oil protected the ivory of the Zeus statue from dampness. A wild olive behind Hera’s temple supplied the crowns awarded to victors. Its traditions begin with contests for power and divine competition: Endymion’s sons competed there for a kingdom, Pelops established control, and early races were connected with the Idæan Dactyli and Zeus, Cronos, Apollo, Hermes, and Ares. Clymenus established games and an altar to Hercules and the Curetes; Iphitus later renewed the contests, which expanded from running to wrestling, boxing, horse-races, the pentathlum, and the pancratium. The restoration and control of the contests caused disputes among Elis, Pisa, and the Arcadians. Exclusion from Olympia’s contests and privileges led the Lacedæmonians to fight Elis; after submitting, Elis permitted them to sacrifice and compete. The quoit of Iphitus records an armistice between Elis and the Olympians. Olympia’s games were supported by training grounds for races, the pentathlum, and wrestling, as well as horse-racing facilities. Its rules had force: Mount Typæum was the threatened punishment site for women entering forbidden contests, and statues warned against cheating and bribery. Victories were proclaimed and preserved through statues, inscriptions, chariots, horses, and monuments, though not every victor was represented. These commemorated athletes such as Euthymus, Diagoras and his family, Theagenes, and others, alongside horse-breeders, rulers, seers, and military figures. The place also accumulated dedications from individuals, cities, and foreign communities: treasuries, bronze monuments, trophies, and offerings from many named peoples and places. Thus Olympia unfolds from sacred and contest traditions into an enduring venue where worship, competition, political conflict, commemoration, and public offerings continually gather.

Narrative, section by section

15 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VI.

The Lacedæmonians fought Elis partly because they were excluded from contests and privileges at Olympia. After Elis submitted, it allowed the Lacedæmonians to sacrifice to the god at Olympia and to compete in the games.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Olympia was the site where Endymion made his sons compete for the kingdom and where Pelops later established control. It was also the center of the contests whose restoration caused disputes between Elis, Pisa, and the Arcadians.

CHAPTER V.

Olympia lay beyond Scillus across the Alpheus and was associated with the Olympian Games and contests. Its traditions included the temple of Cronos, the early races of the Idæan Dactyli, and contests involving Zeus, Cronos, Apollo, Hermes, and Ares. Near Olympia, Mount Typæum was used as the place from which women detected entering forbidden contests were to be thrown.

CHAPTER VIII.

Olympia is described as the site where Clymenus established games and erected an altar to Hercules and the other Curetes. Its contests were later renewed by Iphitus and gradually expanded to include running, wrestling, boxing, horse-races, the pentathlum, and the pancratium.

CHAPTER XI.

Olympia contains the Temple of Zeus, the grove of Pelops, the great altar, numerous other altars, monuments, paintings, and votive offerings. Its rituals include sacrifices by the people of Elis, the use of white poplar wood, and observances connected with the Olympic contests.

CHAPTER XV.

Olympia has the workshop of Phidias outside Altis and the temple of Hera within the sanctuary. Its victors receive crowns made from the wild olive tree growing behind the temple.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Olympia is the setting for the contests, the temple buildings, and the monuments commemorating athletic victories and offenses. The quoit of Iphitus records an armistice between Elis and the Olympians, while statues at Olympia warn athletes against cheating and bribery.

CHAPTER XXII.

Olympia contains a large collection of statues and votive offerings dedicated by Greek cities, foreign communities, and private individuals. The unit records offerings there from places including Apollonia, Metapontum, Phlius, Leontini, the Messenians at the Sicilian Strait, Agrigentum, the Achæans, and Thasos.

CHAPTER XXVI.

It contained numerous votive offerings, including offerings of Micythus, the Victory of the Dorian Messenians, Phormis’s horses and charioteers, and other dedications by communities and individuals. Its Altis also contained a trophy made from Lacedæmonian spoils and other bronze monuments.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

Olympia contains numerous statues, chariots, votive offerings, and inscriptions commemorating athletes, horse-breeders, rulers, seers, and military figures. Its games and records are repeatedly used to establish the victories and origins of the people represented.

CHAPTER VI.

Olympia contains the statues and votive monuments described in this unit, including those of Euthymus, Diagoras and his family, and many other athletes. It is also the place where the athletes' Olympic victories were proclaimed and commemorated.

CHAPTER XI.

At Olympia, Theagenes had notable athletic exploits, including defeating Euthymus in boxing and later defeating Dromeus of Mantinea in the pancratium. The text states that his most notable exploits at Olympia had already been recorded.

CHAPTER XII.

Olympia contained monuments and statues honoring Hiero the son of Dinomenes, Hiero the son of Hierocles, Areus, Aratus, and other victors. The monuments included chariots, horses, and statues made or dedicated by various individuals and communities.

CHAPTER XVII.

Olympia is presented as the site of the Altis, the treasuries, athletic statues, the Olympian games, and the horse-racing facilities. It contains dedications from cities and peoples including the Sicyonians, Carthaginians, Epidamnus, Sybaris, Cyrene, Metapontum, Megara, and Gela.

CHAPTER XXI.

At Olympia, athletes practise for the pentathlum and races in the gymnasium, and a smaller enclosure is used for wrestling. The route from Olympia passes the tomb of Oinomaus, the ruins of Pylos, Heraclea, and other sites before reaching Elis.

Olympia in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Olympia is a place of Olympian games, victories, and honors. When Hercules the Theban established the games, Iasius reportedly won the horserace and victors received wild-olive crowns. After Iphitus restored the games, Corœbus was first victor; Damaretus first won the heavy-armour race; Arrhachion won twice before the 54th. It also held Œbotas’s statue, honored by competitors and victors.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XVII.

Olympia had a statue of Œbotas, who was later declared worthy of one by the oracle at Delphi. Achæans who intended to compete there sacrificed to Œbotas, and victorious competitors crowned his statue.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Damaretus of Heræa was the first victor at Olympia in the race in heavy armour. Corœbus was the first victor at Olympia after Iphitus restored the games, according to the inscription on his tomb.

CHAPTER XL.

Olympia was the site of two of Arrhachion's victories in the two Olympiads before the 54th.

CHAPTER XLVIII.

At Olympia, Iasius is said to have won the horserace when Hercules the Theban established the Olympian games. A crown of wild olive was given to the victor there.
