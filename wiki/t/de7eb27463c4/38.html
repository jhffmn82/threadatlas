This section, in brief. Hercules captures Troy, Cos, Elis, and Pylus, defeats several enemies, and establishes or celebrates religious rites at Olympia. He later attacks Lacedaemon to avenge the death of Licymnius’s son, restores Tyndareus to power, and encounters Auge and her infant son Telephus. At Calydon, Hercules wins Deianira as his wife after defeating Achelous and receives the horn of Amaltheia in exchange for Achelous’s broken horn.

As Greek homicides were supposed to be haunted by the ghosts of their victims until a ceremony of purification was performed which rid them of their invisible, but dangerous, pursuers, we may conjecture that the rule of silence had to be observed by hen until the accomplishment of the purifica- tory rite released them from the restrictions under which they laboured during their uncleanness, and permitted them once more to associate freely with their fellows. As to the restrictions imposed on homicides in ancient Greece, see Psyche’s Task, 2nd ed. pp. 113 sqq.; Folk-Lore tn the Old Testament, i. 80, 83 sg. The motive of the homicide’s silence may have been a fear lest by speaking he should attract the attention, and draw down on himself the vengeance, of his victim’s ghost. Similarly, among certain peoples, a widow is bound to observe silence for some time after her husband’s death, and the rule appears to be based on a like dread of exciting the angry or amorous passions of her departed spouse by the sound of the familiar voice.
