This section, in brief written by a model
The proposed model is a retrieve-generate framework for knowledge-intensive generation that adds Retriever as Answer Classifier and Dense Knowledge Similarity objectives to a RAG-based backbone. The retrieve-generate backbone uses a Dense Passage Retriever to select passages and a BART encoder-decoder to generate a label sentence. Retriever as Answer Classifier trains the retriever to identify whether retrieved passages contain knowledge expressed in an answer, while Dense Knowledge Similarity aligns question-passage representations with knowledge representations extracted from answers. The model jointly optimizes generation, answer-classification, and knowledge-similarity losses with balancing weights.

Methodology In order to focus on retrieving knowledge-relevant passages in KIG tasks, we introduce a new training framework with two new objectives: Retriever as Answer Classifier (RAC), and Dense Knowledge Similarity (DKS). Both objectives aim to mitigate the problem discussed in Section 3, that the re- triever solely trained with NLL loss prefers label- relevant passages rather than question-relevant pas- sages. Figure 1 shows the overall architecture of the proposed model. 4.1 Retrieve-Generate Backbone Transformer-based retrieve-generate systems are widely used in knowledge-intensive generation tasks like generative QA and conversational search (Cooper Stickland et al., 2021; Adiwardana et al., 2020). To apply our knowledge-constrained objec- tives, we need a retrieve-generate architecture as the backbone. Here we choose to use the architec- ture from the RAG paper (Lewis et al., 2020b) as the retrieve-generate backbone. Given a question q and passage corpus P, we first use a Dense Passage Retriever (DPR; Karpukhin et al. (2020)) to com- pute the similarity of each passage pi ∈P with q: sim(pi, q) = encq(q)⊤encp(pi), (1) where encq is the question encoder and encp is the passage encoder. To facilitate easier comparison with prior works, we follow the settings in Lewis et al. (2020b) to fix the parameters of the passage encoder. Based on the similarity, we select top- n passages with Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) and calculate the probability p(pi|q) = exp (sim(pi, q)) P pj∈Top-n(P|q) exp (sim(pj, q)), (2) where Top-n(P|q) is the selected top-n passages with given q. Then these passages are concatenated with the question and generate the probability dis- tribution of response tokens through a sequence-to- sequence encoder-decoder model: p(yt|y:t−1, q, pi) = BART([q; pi], y:t−1) (3) ppi(yt|q, y:t−1) = p(yt|y:t−1, q, pi)p(pi|q) (4) pgen(yt|q, y:t−1) = X pi∈Top-n(P|q) ppi(yt|q, y:t−1) (5) pgen(y|q) = T Y t=1 pgen(yt|q, y:t−1), (6) where BART([q; pi], y:t−1) is the BART generator (Lewis et al., 2020a) that takes the concatenation 1016 of q and pi, and y:t−1 as input. The goal of the backbone is to maximize the probability of label sentence ˆy, which is equal to minimizing the nega- tive log-likelihood of ˆy: Lgen = −log pgen(ˆy|q) (7) 4.2 Retriever as Answer Classifier Since we would like our retriever to retrieve knowledge-relevant passages instead of only re- trieving label-relevant passages, we add a new an- swer classification objective, which makes use of rich answer encoding to capture knowledge inside the answer. The answer classifier outputs whether the retrieved top-n passages contain the knowledge inside the answer. Here we reuse the DPR retriever (sharing parameters) in the retrieve-generate back- bone to generate answer encoding and use it for classifying the matched answer. We apply in-batch negative sampling (Karpukhin et al., 2020) for the RAC objective. Given a question-answer pair (ˆq, ˆy), we use m answers in the same batch as negative answers from the rest of the training set: Neg = {y(1), y(2), . . . , y(m)}, (8) where the batch size is m + 1. We then compute the probability of the answer (both positive and negative) y given question ˆq as: sim(y, pˆq i ) = encp(pˆq i )⊤encq(y) (9) S(y, ˆq) = X pˆq i ∈Top-n(P|ˆq) p(pˆq i |ˆq)sim(y, pˆq i ) (10) pRAC(y|ˆq) = σ(S(y, ˆq)), (11) where p(pˆq i |ˆq) is computed with Eq. (2), encp and encq are the same as described in Section 4.1, and σ(·) is the sigmoid function for generating proba- bility. We then use binary cross-entropy loss to get the training signal: LRAC = log(pRAC(ˆy|ˆq)) + X y′∈Neg log(1 −pRAC(y′|ˆq)) (12) 4.3 Dense Knowledge Similarity Another way of focusing on knowledge-relevant passages is to further marginalize the generation process. This objective directly focuses on knowl- edge instead of the answer label, which changes the final goal from generating a natural language response to answer the question p(y|q) to generat- ing knowledge k required to answer the question p(k|q): p(k|q) = X y p(k|y)p(y|q) (13) Thus the optimization process becomes maximiz- ing p(ˆk|q). To accomplish that, we need to get the gold knowledge ˆk for training. We choose to use the sentence bottleneck auto- encoder3 introduced by Montero et al. (2021) to get answer knowledge representations. The original goal of sentence bottleneck auto-encoder is to re- construct the input sequence through a bottleneck representation between encoder and decoder (as shown in Figure 2): AE(y) = decAE(β(encAE(y))) = y′, (14) where β(·) is the knowledge extractor, a multihead attention mechanism that aggregates encoder states H to a single bottleneck representation: H = encAE(y) (15) z = β(H) (16) β(H) = MultiHead(q, K, V ) (17) where q is the encoder states of [CLS] token, K and V are the encoder states H. Here we treat the bottleneck representation as the knowledge repre- sentation of the input sequence: k = z (18) k is a rich answer encoding containing knowledge needed to rebuild the sentence itself, and thus, im- plicitly guarantees that the knowledge represented by a wrong answer is different from the one by a right answer. We, subsequently, train another knowledge extractor α to extract knowledge repre- sentation from question-passage pairs: k′ = X pi∈Top-n(P|q) α(encAE([q; pi]))p(pi|q) (19) We expect k′ to be similar to k in the knowledge representation space, thus we use MSE loss to ob- tain the training signal: LDKS = ||ˆk −k′||2 2 (20) 3Appendix B provides more details about our reasoning behind selecting sentence bottleneck auto-encoder. 1017 encAE decAE Figure 2: Sentence Bottleneck Auto-endoer. By fitting the dense knowledge representations (rich answer encoding) instead of the original label answers, we change the goal from retrieving label- relevant passages to retrieving knowledge-relevant passages. 4.4 Overall Architecture Using the retrieve-generate backbone and the two objectives with rich answer encoding, we can train the model with all these objectives in a multi-task setting. In training, we optimize the summation of all the different losses: L = Lgen + w(LRAC, LDKS)⊤, (21) where Lgen, LRAC and LDKS are computed in Eq. (7), (12) and (20) respectively. w ∈R2 is the weight vector for balancing different losses. We compute w in each step to make sure that all the losses will have the same value. 5
