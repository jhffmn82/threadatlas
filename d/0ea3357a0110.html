Impressions of Theophrastus Such

George Eliot · 1879 · 27 sections

The document is a sequence of reflective essays and character sketches concerned with self-knowledge, vanity, morality, intellectual life, public conduct, national feeling, and prejudice. It opens with a narrator candidly examining his own loneliness, wounded vanity, failed influence, and difficulty in knowing himself, arguing that sympathy and attention to other people correct egoism. Writing, he says, encourages confessional garrulousness by imagining a distant approving audience. His memories of his father, a beloved country parson, and of childhood journeys through England explain his affection for the past and rural landscape, even while he recognizes that present England is changing and that London now claims him most strongly. A series of portraits then exposes particular moral and intellectual failures. Merman’s scholarly dispute with Grampus ruins his prospects, finances, and peace, showing the cruelty surrounding the worship of Truth. Lentulus embodies baseless self-confidence; Hinze empty deference; Touchwood destructive bad temper; Spike the way private commercial interests can become practical public politics; Mordax vanity and cruelty under contradiction; Mixtus the confusion of former ideals with wealth and fashionable life; Ganymede the enduring illusion of being “young”; Vorticella the self-importance of minor authorship; and Pepin and other prolific writers the dangers of writing faster than knowledge or judgment permit. The essays repeatedly distinguish real moral seriousness from appearances. They condemn universal burlesque, plagiarism and careless claims of originality, self-deception, inaccurate personal testimony, and a narrow morality that excuses public dishonesty so long as private family conduct seems respectable. Genuine morality requires knowledge and sympathy in every relation, including politics, commerce, journalism, and workmanship. The closing sections widen from individual character to society and history. Trost disputes the narrator’s speculation that self-supplying, self-repairing machines might supersede humanity. The discussion then turns to national memory, the treatment of Jews, and European exclusion. Rejecting the idea that Jews are an inexplicable exception, the text traces their endurance through foreign rule, dispersion, and persecution; condemns Christian persecution as contrary to Christ’s teachings; recognizes Jewish national pride and capacities; and considers whether preserved national feeling and favorable conditions could support renewed Jewish national life. Throughout, the document urges clearer perception, humility, sympathy, and resistance to vanity, cruelty, and inherited prejudice.

I. body

The narrator examines the limits of self-knowledge, recognizing that other people may perceive weaknesses in him that he cannot see. He reflects on his loneliness, desire for approbation and love, physical awkwardness, failed public influence, and tendency to seek consolations for wounded vanity. He concludes that self-partiality and egoism distort moral judgment, while attention to the world and sympathy with other people can correct self-occupation. Although he remains imperfect and dependent on the common lot, he has developed a stronger habit of listening to and taking interest in the experiences of others.

My conversational reticences about myself turn into garrulousness on paper--as t body

The speaker says that conversational reticence about personal matters becomes garrulousness through writing. The act of writing creates an illusion of a distant, approving audience, while a distinct critical auditor would inhibit the speaker. The speaker leaves judgment of the manuscripts to others but refuses to solicit an opinion from the friend during life, fearing a verdict that they are flat rather than subtle. The speaker connects this self-awareness to a loving laughter at the weaknesses of acquaintances, measured against an ideal self or God within.

II. body

The narrator argues that people should judge the present age with as much fairness and affection as the past, since every period contains both beauty and ugliness. He explains that his own attachment to the past comes chiefly from memories of his father and childhood, not from a belief that earlier societies were better. The narrator describes his father, a country parson who knew people of every social rank and was loved by his parishioners despite collecting tithes. He also recalls riding with his father through the English countryside and reflects on how the national landscape shaped his early imagination and continuing affection for England.

That part of my father's prime to which he oftenest referred had fallen on the d body

The narrator recalls his father's conservative view of England during the political reaction following the French Revolution and the rise of Napoleon. He contrasts his father's idealized England with the changing England of the present, while describing how railways, canals, and other labor reshape the countryside. The narrator admits that his affection for rural England is partly visionary but still values the ideals formed by childhood. He now identifies himself chiefly with London, though he retains his early attachment to home and familiar landscapes.

III. body

The unit concerns Merman's attempt to challenge Grampus's epoch-making work through a theory about the connection of symbolic monuments associated with the Magicodumbras and Zuzumotzis. After Merman publishes his book, Grampus writes a devastating review attacking Merman's learning and competence, and the controversy makes Merman notorious across Britain, America, Germany, and France. Julia Merman supports her husband's ideas and tries to protect him from further trouble. The unit presents this public humiliation as an example of the cruelty that can accompany worship of Truth.

Meanwhile, how did he like it? body

Merman devotes months to defending his counter-theory against Grampus, producing a sixty-page rejoinder and gathering learning to support it. Grampus and his followers turn Merman, the Magicodumbras, and the Zuzumotzis into objects of public ridicule, while Merman becomes increasingly isolated and obsessed with answering his critics. His controversy damages his legal and editorial prospects, exhausts his household finances, and leaves Julia struggling to maintain the household. Merman eventually takes a post far below his earlier prospects, and although he later recognizes some of his former errors and sees his central idea accepted, he remains embittered and prematurely worn down.

IV. body

The narrator reflects on Lentulus, a respectable and benevolent man whose reserved manner suggests immense but undisclosed originality. Lentulus disparages celebrated poets and philosophers without possessing detailed knowledge of their work, believing that he could produce better poetry and correct the errors of all previous thinking. The narrator concludes that Lentulus's confidence is conceit: his apparent neutrality conceals expansive ignorance and self-estimation unsupported by achievement. Lentulus's lack of words prevents his ideas from causing much harm, and the narrator ends by treating his silence as a blessing.

V. body

The unit contrasts ordinary, socially motivated insincerity with Hinze’s deliberate and habitual excessive deference. Hinze treats Felicia’s conventional observations as profound revelations, embarrassing her with reverent attention and elaborate compliments. He similarly venerates Tulpian and repeats the opinions of distinguished people, not for a clear personal advantage but from a formed habit and lack of independent judgments. The narrator concludes that Hinze is not exactly a hypocrite, but an ill-gifted conversationalist whose make-believe reverence lacks any deep motive or genuine admiration.

VI. body

The unit examines bad temper as a habitual disposition that is often excused even when it produces persistently offensive conduct. Touchwood is presented as a fundamentally good and capable man whose quarrelsome, capricious temper makes intimacy difficult and undermines the value of his apologies and kindnesses. The text argues that high character requires dependable principles and that uncontrolled temper can distort judgment, especially in public life and partisanship. It therefore doubts that Touchwood should enter Parliament and instead wishes that he would write the great book of which he is thought capable.

VII. body

The unit concerns Spike, a cotton manufacturer and elector whose private interests gradually make him a representative of wider political needs. Although he is not inwardly attached to Progress as an abstraction, his long involvement in spinning and weaving has made him a devotee of it in practice. He supports the Reform Bill and the Repeal of the Corn Laws because expanded political representation and foreign markets benefit trade. His objections to legislation affecting his own trade become public-spirited because the same problems affect his trading neighbours.

VIII. body

The unit examines Mordax, an intellectually gifted and generally kind man whose vanity makes him deny error and respond cruelly to contradiction. Acer argues that true fairness and goodwill are shown by defending overlooked claims, while the narrator maintains that good qualities can coexist with serious defects in particular situations. The discussion contrasts intellect with moral nature, emphasizing that both can be uneven and that sympathy for those immediately affected should restrain grand justifications for cruelty. Mordax's arrogance is illustrated alongside Pummel's self-satisfied refusal to admit surprise or ignorance.

IX. body

The unit concerns Mixtus, whose youthful religious, philanthropic, democratic, and intellectual commitments have been displaced and complicated by his marriage to Scintilla, increasing wealth, and integration into fashionable London society. Scintilla’s prettiness, liveliness, music, and worldly associations captivated Mixtus, while her indifference to religious and theoretical ideas made his former habits socially impractical. Mixtus has become a wealthy commercial man and lavish host, but he feels personally submerged and uncertain because his present identity conflicts with his earlier ideals. His kindness and intellectual interests remain, yet he alternates between defending money and affirming the superior value of mind, genius, religion, and social benevolence.

X. body

The unit argues that indiscriminate burlesque debases the moral currency by turning sacred, heroic, and pathetic subjects into objects of laughter. It distinguishes La Bruyere’s refined use of ridicule from the dull habit of forcing jokes onto every serious topic, warning that this damages education, art, and human sentiments. The unit predicts that Shakspere may increasingly be known through burlesques rather than his original plays and cites Sainte Beuve’s warning that civilisation can rapidly decline during crises. It concludes that a society habituated to mocking everything, like the Tirynthians, may become incapable of seriousness when serious action is required.

XI. body

The unit examines Euphorion’s inconsistent attitude toward intellectual property: he recognizes ancient and well-known sources but treats many recent borrowings as belonging to everybody or nobody. It argues that knowingly presenting another person’s philosophical or scientific ideas as original is morally comparable to more obvious forms of plagiarism, even when it cannot be legally proved. The text also warns that accusations of plagiarism are easily mistaken because ideas may arise independently, memories fail, and people often assume that prominent or fluent individuals must be the originators. Aquila illustrates the conversational appropriation of other people’s information, while the discussion of original authorship emphasizes the misleading influence of reputation and public expectations.

Such guesses remind one of a fabulist's imaginary council of animals assembled t body

An imaginary council of animals debates which creature constructed a honeycomb that Bruin and other epicures had found and tasted. The animals variously suspect birds, the Musk-rat, and finally the Wasp, after the Fox reports finding honey on the Wasp and being stung on the nose. The council accepts the Fox's swollen snout as evidence, despite objections from several animals. A complimentary deputation to the Wasp is approved in hopes of encouraging further honey production.

XII. body

The unit concerns Ganymede, a precocious writer whose family, admirers, critics, and colleagues repeatedly treat him as remarkably young. His book A Comparative Estimate of European Nations and his literary organisation establish and reinforce his public reputation for youthful brilliance. Although he grows older, marries, becomes heavier, and takes a government post, Ganymede continues to believe that youthfulness defines him. The narrator observes that this illusion is sustained both by Ganymede's own manner and by others' continued use of the epithet “young.”

XIII. body

The unit examines how human beings notice folly and absurdity in others but often fail to recognize the same traits in themselves, producing false testimonials about their own conduct. It presents comparison as the chief means by which a person uses observations of others to investigate human nature and self-knowledge. Judgment of others by oneself can either provide a method of understanding mankind or reduce everyone to the judge’s own limitations, while judgment of oneself by others can weaken indignation against wrongdoing. The unit concludes that people often preserve an outdated tradition about their own character and trust their verbal self-description more than plainly contradictory behavior.

opening body

The unit contrasts self-deception and inaccurate testimony with genuine imagination grounded in precise perception. Bovis falsely believes himself moderate in his sugar consumption, while Callista confuses invention with observation and misremembers ordinary facts. It argues that the sane mind in the sane body distinguishes intentions from actions, past feelings from present ones, and imagined events from witnessed facts. Dante is presented as an example of a powerful imaginative seer who can experience an intense vision yet recognize the difference between that vision and external reality.

XIV. body

The unit criticizes the too ready writer, comparing him to the excessive talker who monopolizes conversation and prevents others from contributing. It contrasts Adrastus's celebrated, wide-ranging productivity with Theron's neglected specialized research, and presents Pepin as a general writer whose early self-confidence hardened into habitual, overconfident, poorly grounded judgment. The narrator argues that incessant writing for publication can damage perception and veracity, making writers invested in their own errors and inclined to seem authoritative on every subject. Although Pepin's private character is more amiable and modest than his writing suggests, the public conscience is blamed for treating authorship as a vocation requiring little moral or intellectual scruple.

XV. body

The unit examines the moral ailments associated with small authorship, especially the disease of magnified self-importance. It focuses on Vorticella, a woman in Pumpiter whose single book, "The Channel Islands, with Notes and an Appendix," becomes the center of her social identity. Vorticella preserves and displays an album of highly flattering critical opinions, insists on the importance and correctness of her work, and tries to make visitors read both the reviews and the book. The discussion broadens to male and female vanity, arguing that vanity is common to both sexes but differs in expression and manner.

XVI. body

The unit criticizes the narrowed popular meaning of morality and morals, which often refers only to sexual propriety, family life, and private charity while excluding duties toward society. Melissa pities Sir Gavial Mantrap for his disgrace and calls him moral because he is a good family man and charitable, although his commercial schemes impoverished others; the narrator regards him as a serious scoundrel. The unit argues that public dishonesty, political partiality, journalistic malice, and careless workmanship are moral failures because they damage the commonwealth. It also rejects the supposed opposition between intellect and morality, defining genuine morality as conduct shaped by full knowledge and sympathy in every human relation.

XVII. body

In “XVII. SHADOWS OF THE COMING RACE,” Trost argues that machines will remain dependent on the human race and free people for higher intellectual work, while the narrator imagines machines becoming self-supplying, self-repairing, and self-reproducing, eventually superseding humanity and consciousness. The narrator presents this as a logical consequence of mechanical progress, though Trost rejects the premises. In “XVIII. THE MODERN HEP! HEP! HEP!,” the text argues that national memories and national consciousness can inspire peoples to greatness, using Greece, Italy, and the English as examples. It then criticizes Europe for treating the Jews as exceptional and excluding them from the justice and mercy ordinarily based on human likeness.

But to consider a people whose ideas have determined the religion of half the wo body

The unit argues that the Jews should not be treated as an inexplicable exception, because their intense national and religious identity arose through historical circumstances comparable in some respects to those of other peoples. It describes the Jewish struggle to preserve national life against foreign rule, especially through the Maccabees and the resistance to Rome, and connects Jewish dispersion and persecution with both moral dangers and remarkable endurance. The text condemns Christians who persecuted Jews in the name of Christianity and argues that such conduct contradicted Christ's teachings. It concludes that, despite centuries of oppression and alienation, Jews retained national pride, family-centered kindliness, practical ability, and scientific and artistic aptitude.

The unit examines prejudice against Jews, immigration, national identity, and the possibility of a renewed Jewish national life. It argues that racial fusion cannot be stopped, but that its effects should be moderated through stronger national excellence and the preservation of national traditions. The text defends Jewish separateness and considers whether widespread Jewish feeling and favorable political conditions might support the restoration of a Jewish state. It also emphasizes Christianity’s Jewish origin and urges Jews to draw upon the inherited glories and sufferings of their people.
