  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, the Thebans are a leading Boeotian power that resists Lacedaemonian control, expands its authority, defeats Sparta, and is ultimately destroyed by Alexander.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Thebans are described as ancient philosophers and astrologers, participants in mythic and Persian-era conflicts, and a formerly dominant but later weakened Boeotian power.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, the Cadmeans follow Tiresias’s advice and flee to Tilphossus, bury Tiresias, and later invade and settle in Doria, with some returning to Thebes.
  • In The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I, the Thebans defend Thebes and its seven gates, then arm and defeat the Argives after Eteocles and Polynices fall.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, the Cadmeans are the people of Thebes who depend on Oedipus for victory and safety and are urged to help expose Laius’s murderer.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, the Cadmeans defend the Cadmea against an Argive army while Eteocles urges their men to protect the city’s walls, shrines, altars, and gates.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, the Chorus prays for the city’s safety, fears for the returning champions, warns Eteocles against meeting Polynices, and laments the curse upon Oedipus’s house.
  • In The Library, the Thebans expose sons to the Teumessian vixen, pay tribute to Erginus, consult Tiresias, resist the Argives, and abandon Thebes to found Hestiaea.
  • In The Library, the Thebans prepare for battle under Eteocles, consult Tiresias, and learn that Menoeceus must sacrifice himself to Ares for their victory.
  • In The Library, the Cadmeans are driven in battle as far as the walls of Thebes before their opponents flee after Zeus strikes Capaneus.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Thebans are a people of Thebes whose traditions, religious practices, political history, wars, defeats, recoveries, and later decline are recounted.
  • In Description of Greece, the Thebans refuse to join Agesilaus in Asia, oppose the Lacedæmonians, suffer defeat at Coronea, and later help restore Messenia after Leuctra.

Thebans in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Thebans are the people of Thebes and a leading Boeotian power, described as a warlike nation with many valiant commanders. Their rise centers on resistance to Lacedaemonian control: the whole city supported Theban exiles, helped recover Thebes by killing Lacedaemonian supporters and besieging the Cadmea, and, with Athenian and Boeotian aid, forced its garrison to surrender. They joined the Athenian senate on equal terms and received Athenian aid, while defending themselves through difficult ground, raids, and open battle. They pursued control over Boeotia, subduing the Oropians and rejecting peace leagues and proposed terms because they claimed all Boeotia under Theban government. This policy accompanied campaigns against Orchomenos, Plataea, and Thespiae: they routed a larger Spartan force near Orchomenos, expelled Plataean citizens and razed their city, took Thespiae, and later destroyed Orchomenus after a conspiracy. Under Epaminondas and Pelopidas, they defeated the Lacedaemonians at Leuctra and later at Mantinea, becoming chief commanders and sovereigns of Greece. They also entered the Peloponnesus, aided in rebuilding Messene, broke through defenses near Corinth before being repelled by Chabrias, and sent forces to Thessaly after Pelopidas and Ismenias were imprisoned. At their height, they sought dominion at sea as well as on land, decreeing one hundred galleys and matching docks and bringing Rhodes, Chios, and Byzantium under obedience. They aided the Megalopolitans with troops under Cephision, fought in several campaigns, relieved Chaeronea, and invaded and plundered Phocis. The Phocian war depleted their treasury, leading them to accept three hundred talents from Artaxerxes. Their final crisis came against Alexander. Resolving to defend Theban liberty, they besieged the Macedonian garrison in the Cadmea and fortified Thebes. Though outnumbered, they resisted resolutely until Macedonians entered through an unguarded portal. More than six thousand were killed and about three thousand became captives sold into slavery; resistance continued even after the city was taken. Later records also note Thebans supporting Macedonians because they held territory allotted after Thebes was destroyed, while Athenians contemplated restoring Thebes and its land.

Thebans in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

The Thebans are a people of Thebes, associated with Cadmea and with civic traditions, sanctuaries, monuments, sacrifices, and burial places. They maintain accounts of Cadmus’s settlement, altered their sacrifice to Apollo Spodius from bulls to working oxen, and honor figures including Pronomus, Epaminondas, Iolaus, Zethus, and Amphion. Their earlier traditions include defeat and tribute after killing Clymenus, remission through Hercules, conflict with Orchomenians, flight after Glisas, settlement at Homole, and restoration to Thebes by Thersander. Their political and military story centers on changing alliances, severe reversals, and repeated recovery. They were first friendly with the Lacedæmonians, later joined the Corinthians against them, captured Platæa, fought Athenians with both defeats and victory at Delium, and faced wars involving Orchomenians, Phocians, and Peloponnesians. Epaminondas is credited with raising them from obedience to other Greek states into a foremost power. Their decisive victory at Leuctra, fought for their country, wives, and children, enabled them to expel Lacedæmonian Harmosts and decemvirates; they also aided Arcadian colonization and the founding of Megalopolis. Yet they fought the Phocians for ten years with mixed success, suffered most heavily among the Greeks at Chæronea, and buried those who fell against Philip in a common sepulchre. After Philip’s death they ejected the Macedonian garrison, but Alexander expelled them from Thebes. Survivors escaped chiefly to Athens, while some reached Acræphnium; Cassander later restored them with allies, and they rebuilt their walls. In later affairs they joined Mithridates against Rome under Pytheas, were punished for earlier actions against several peoples, and afterward sought Roman friendship. Although Roman favor eventually restored territory taken after Sulla’s invasion of Bœotia, the lower city was largely deserted in the narrator’s time.

Narrative, section by section

19 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIV.

The Thebans, encouraged by Pytheas, undertook to take an eager part in the war against the Romans. They had been heavily punished by Metellus and ordered to pay fines for invading Phocis, ravaging Eubœa, and destroying the harvest of the people of Amphissa.

CHAPTER XI.

The Thebans supported the Athenian account that Gryllus mortally wounded Epaminondas. Epaminondas is credited with transforming the Thebans from a people accustomed to obeying other Greek states into a foremost power.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The Thebans supported the founding of Megalopolis by sending 1,000 picked men under Parmenes to protect the Arcadians. Megalopolis was enrolled in alliance with Thebes, and the city initially believed that this alliance removed its fear of the Lacedæmonians.

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I.

The Thebans argued that the Lacedæmonians had brought about the peace and later violated it. Under Neocles, they approached Platæa by way of Hysiæ and Eleutheræ, captured the town, and required its inhabitants to leave before sunset.

CHAPTER VI.

The Thebans were defeated by the Athenians in two battles, including one near Platæa, but later defeated the Athenians at Delium. They were first friendly with the Lacedæmonians, then joined the Corinthians against them, and won the battle of Leuctra, after which they removed Lacedæmonian decemvirates and Harmosts from the towns. They fought for ten years in the Sacred War, suffered especially heavily at Chæronea, and later expelled the Macedonian garrison from Thebes after Philip's death.

CHAPTER VII.

The Thebans were expelled from Thebes by Alexander, escaped to Athens, and were restored by Cassander the son of Antipater with assistance from the Athenians, Messenians, and Arcadians of Megalopolis. They rebuilt their old walls, later joined Mithridates in his war against Rome, and sought renewed Roman friendship after Sulla invaded Bœotia. Sulla took half their territory, though they later recovered it through Roman favor; in the narrator's time, the lower part of their city was largely deserted.

CHAPTER VIII.

After being defeated in battle by the Argives at Glisas, most of the Thebans fled with Laodamas, while others avoided going to the Illyrii and settled in Homole. Thersander later restored them to Thebes, and they commemorated Homole by naming a gate Homolois.

CHAPTER IX.

The Thebans were initially beaten at Ismenius but defended themselves from the walls of Thebes and then destroyed the remaining Peloponnesian army. They later fought the Epigoni at Glisas, were defeated, and were either driven into flight or reduced after a blockade.

CHAPTER XII.

The Thebans formerly sacrificed bulls to Apollo Spodius, but after sacrificing an ox employed in labor when the intended sacrificial bull failed to arrive, they continued sacrificing working oxen. They say that Cadmus was guided by a marked cow to the place where he and his army settled, and they erected statues there to Pronomus and Epaminondas.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Thebans fought the Lacedæmonians at Leuctra, staking the safety of their country, wives, and children on the battle. They won the engagement, with only 47 Thebans and Bœotians who remained in the battle killed.

CHAPTER XIV.

The Thebans were taken back into Bœotia by Epaminondas after their allies had overrun and plundered Laconia.

CHAPTER XV.

The Thebans marched against Alexander after he imprisoned Pelopidas, putting Cleomenes and Hypatus at their head. During Epaminondas's absence, they drove the Orchomenians from their country, an act Epaminondas regarded as a misfortune.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Thebans say that Xenophon the Athenian made the hands and face of the statue of Fortune carrying Wealth, while Callistonicus made the rest. They also say that their ancient wooden statues of Aphrodite were votive offerings of Harmonia and that the shields at the temple of Demeter belonged to Lacedæmonian notables who fell at Leuctra. The Thebans say that Dionysus freed some of their people who had been captured by Thracians.

CHAPTER XVII.

The Thebans were going to battle against the Orchomenians with Hercules. They believed they would win if a prominent citizen willingly committed suicide, and they later guarded the sepulchre of Zethus and Amphion when people from Tithorea sought to remove its earth.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Thebans have the gymnasium of Iolaus, a race-course, and the hero-chapel of Iolaus near the gate Prœtis. They admit that Iolaus died in Sardinia with the Athenians and Thespians who crossed over with him. Some Thebans who escaped the destruction of Thebes by Alexander fled to Acræphnium because old age and weakness prevented them from reaching Attica.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

The Thebans killed Clymenus over a trifling matter at the festival of Onchestian Poseidon. They were defeated by Erginus and his brothers and agreed to pay a yearly tax, which Hercules later caused to be remitted; they eventually drove the Orchomenians from their country.

CHAPTER XL.

The Thebans who fell in the battle against Philip were buried in a common sepulchre near Chæronea. Their sepulchre bore a lion but no inscription.

CHAPTER II.

The Thebans had previously been at variance with the Phocians and openly declared war on them after the temple at Delphi was seized. They fought the Phocians for ten years, sometimes prevailing and sometimes being defeated, and later received the support of Philip the son of Amyntas.

CHAPTER III.

The Thebans helped restore the Phocian towns before the fatal defeat of the Greeks at Chæronea.
