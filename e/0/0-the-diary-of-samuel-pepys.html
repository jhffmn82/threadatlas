The period ends with Pepys conscious of debt, pleasure-seeking, Court disorder, and clerical conduct.

JUNE, JULY & AUGUST 1661 June 1st.

From June 1 to June 5, the diarist and Sir W. Pen inspect ships at Woolwich and Deptford and conduct business connected with an impending voyage. The diarist discusses Wardrobe responsibilities with my Lord, who expresses confidence in him and promises him first refusal of Mr. Young’s place for his father if it becomes vacant. The diarist and Mr. Creed obtain financial imprests and seek ammunition for my Lord’s ships, while the Navy Office conducts its regular business. The diarist also spends time with family and colleagues, attends church and the theatre, and takes part in social and musical activities.

opening

The diarist spends these days visiting Greenwich, Whitehall, the Wardrobe, the office, and the Theatre, while dining and drinking with various acquaintances. Lieutenant Lambert, now Captain of the Norwich, explains ships to him during a water journey to Greenwich. The diarist learns from my Lord that Mr. Townsend is to reveal the whole mystery of the Wardrobe and that he may become Townsend's deputy. He sees Bartholomew Faire at the Theatre, visits the Wardrobe with his wife, and discusses ecclesiastical matters with Dean Fuller.

opening

The narrator's lord is to go to Algier to settle business, put the fleet in order, and return to Lisbone with three ships to meet the following fleet. He entrusts the narrator with overseeing the great preparation during his absence under orders from my Lord Chancellor and Edward Montagu. The office agrees to write to the Duke of York about its severe shortage of money and inability to obtain supplies without personal security. The office's credit has fallen so low that suppliers will no longer sell to it without such security.

opening

The narrator spends time with Captain Rolt, Ferrers, and others before arranging a cloth purchase for my Lord and obtaining an advance through Alderman Backwell. On the 13th, the narrator accompanies my Lord from Whitehall to Deptford and boards the Dutch yacht with him. My Lord becomes angry when W. Howe delays bringing his things, but they eventually sail, and the narrator takes leave of my Lord with expressions of respect and a five-gun salute. The narrator then returns toward London by boat with Captain Ferrers.

opening

The diarist manages the purchase and shipment of cloth for my Lord, arranging scarlet, purple, and black cloth and linen, and eventually sending it by a Margate Hoy after other plans fail. He recovers from a painful cold and spends several days overseeing painters who finish work on his stairs. He visits friends and relatives, including my father, my Lady, Captain Lambert's household, and Jack Cole, and attends to office and financial business. By the end of the unit, he is pleased with the completed stairs and relieved of his illness and household troubles.

opening

The narrator met uncle Wight at the Exchequer and went home with him to dinner. At dinner, the narrator and Mr. Norbury discussed Mr. Norbury's wife's house and land at Brampton, which the narrator found too much to buy. The narrator later went to the office and was pleased to see the house beginning to be clean. On the 22nd, the narrator dined with Lord Crew and was very much made of by Lord Crew and his lady.

opening

Pepys spent time with Luellin and Blirton, attended church, socialized with Sir W. Batten and others, and visited Islington. He was pleased that his house had become very clean and neat, and his father came to see it on Midsummer-day. Elizabeth Pepys was unwell, with a sore belly that she believed had become dangerous. At Graye’s Inn Walks, Pepys considered Mrs. Frances Butler the greatest beauty he had ever seen.

opening

The diarist begins singing lessons with Mr. Mage and spends time with family and visitors. He learns from a letter from his aunt that Robert has become ill with dizziness, prompting his father to travel to attend to Robert's business. The diarist discusses with his father how to present a proposed land purchase to Robert, offering £600 immediately and £150 annually for land yielding £50 per year. He later attends a performance of "Bartholomew Fayre" with Lady Batten, Mrs. Rebecca Allen, Mrs. Thompson, and others.

opening

The writer spent the 28th practising singing, visiting his Lady, working at the office, and going with Sir W. Pen to Moorefields to watch wrestling. On the 29th, the writer and both Sir Williams examined delays in preparing ships at Deptford, then met Mr. Chetwind, Gregory, Hartlibb, and Mr. Kipps at the Bell at the Maypole in the Strand. Mr. Chetwind, formerly consumptive, had become very fat and sallow from chewing tobacco and praised Hooker's Ecclesiastical Polity as the book that made him a Christian. On the 30th, the writer observed that the trade of briefs had become so constant that he and others resolved to give no more to them.

opening

The speaker attended a sermon, had dinner with his wife, and went to Whitehall with Sir Williams to report to the Duke of York about why the ships had not been sent out. The ships could not leave the river because the wind was against them. The speaker later walked alone in Graye's Inn Walk, returned home tired, and found his wife out of order but not sick. The Portuguese Embassador came to White Hall to take leave of the King before ending matters with the Queen and sending her over.

JULY 1661 July 1st.

The diarist bought household goods, including a chest of drawers and an Indian gown, attended to office and domestic affairs, and continued singing lessons with Theodore Goodgroome. He visited Sir William Davenant's Opera, where the second part of "The Siege of Rhodes" was performed for the King and the Queen of Bohemia, though the Eunuch was hissed off the stage. He also learned of the serious illness of his uncle and the death of Mr. Saml. Crew from spotted fever. On July 4th, he saw "Claracilla" at the Theatre and noted that the house was unusually empty because of the Opera.

opening

The narrator travels from London to Brampton after learning that his uncle Robert has died, finding his father well and the corpse awaiting burial. He and his father read uncle Robert's will, arranged the burial, and spent the following week sorting his papers and investigating the estate, which proved less substantial and more disorganized than expected. Tom Trice entered a caveat on behalf of his mother, while the missing surrenders of the copyhold land created further legal trouble. The narrator later visits Cambridge and travels on to Impington to see his elderly uncle Talbot Pepys.

opening

Samuel Pepys spends 16–31 October arranging his uncle’s will and estate with his father, the Trices, Sir Robert Bernard, Mr. Moore, and others. An agreement is reached for Pepys’s aunt to leave the house in return for £10, while other disputes are left to the law, but Pepys remains worried that debts and legal claims will reduce the inheritance. He returns from Brampton to London, resumes work at the Navy Office, manages family and household affairs, and visits theatres, friends, and relatives. In London he follows disputes between Parliament’s Houses and hears reports about King Charles II, the Lord Chancellor, and national politics.

AUGUST 1661 August 1st.

On August 1, Sir Williams both, the narrator, the narrator’s wife, and Mrs. Margarett Pen went by coach to Walthamstow to visit Mrs. Browne. The narrator gave Mrs. Browne six silver spoons for her boy, and the party ate a venison pasty and were merry. They heard an allegation that Lady Batten had been a man’s whore and tried unsuccessfully to reconcile the matter. On August 2, Dr. Thos. Pepys dined with the narrator, and Tom visited before the narrator prepared to travel on horseback to Cambridge.

opening

Samuel Pepys travels through Cambridge, Huntingdon, Brampton, and surrounding places to consult Roger Pepys, his father, relatives, tenants, and others about his uncle's will and related family business. Roger Pepys explains his concerns about young men's conduct in Parliament and the risk of renewed war. After returning to London, Pepys attends to Privy Seal business, visits Whitehall, and works with Mr. Moore and Lord Roberts while waiting for delayed proceedings. He also manages social and family matters, including his wife's assistance to Mrs. Hater, his father's affairs, and an outing to the theatre with my Lady's household.

opening

Samuel Pepys records concern over Lord Hinchingbroke’s illness and Lord Sandwich’s sickness at Alicante, along with the effects on Lord Sandwich’s family. Lord Hinchingbroke develops a severe illness feared to be smallpox but improves, while Lord Sandwich is reported to have recovered from pain and fever and expects to go aboard. Pepys and other Navy officials report the Navy’s lack of money to the Duke of York, as the Navy Office’s bills are being offered at a 20 percent loss. The unit also describes widespread sickness, anxiety about the Court, and Pepys’s visits to plays, taverns, and family and official gatherings.

opening

The narrator attends to sealing business, office affairs, family matters, and social visits from the 19th through the 24th. He helps negotiate the addition of Sir R. Ford’s house to the office, agreeing to pay him £200 per year, and spends time arranging a possible marriage between his brother Tom and Mrs. Whately’s daughter. He visits relatives and associates, takes his wife to the Opera, and observes family tensions, mourning, and the death of his aunt Fenner. He also sees a baboon brought from Guiny and receives a visit from Captain Isham before his voyage to Portugal.

opening

The diarist manages family, household, legal, and financial affairs while attending theatres, taverns, and social gatherings. He arranges for Pall to go into the country, bids farewell to Jane as she leaves for her mother, and worries about his father’s finances, Tom’s future, and his wife’s brother Balty. Lord Sandwich has recovered from a severe fever at Alicante, while Lord Hinchingbrooke and his brother depart for France. The diarist ends the month concerned about his borrowing, indulgence in plays and pleasure, court disorder, and clerical conduct.

opening

The writer considers subscribing L20 and reports that the Office is quiet because of a lack of money. The Office's bills have been offered for sale on the Exchange at a 10 per cent. loss. The Office may add Sir R. Ford's house, but difficulties over dividing it and personally guaranteeing the L200 annual rent may prevent this. The season is described as sickly everywhere because of strange and fatal fevers.
