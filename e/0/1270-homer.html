  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Homer is presented as a traveler to Egypt and a poet who drew ideas from Egyptian customs and stories.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Homer sings sweet lays enlarging Odysseus' fame, honors Aias through song, and persuades listeners to accept his stories.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Homer is presented as a divine poet and singer whose Iliad and Odyssey attained supreme perfection and shaped later Greek epic.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Homer is associated with the Iliad, Odyssey, Poems, and catalogues, while traditions dispute his birthplace, age, parentage, and other details.
  • In Description of Greece, Homer is cited as the author of the Iliad and as a source for accounts of Mecisteus, Cyllene, Achilles' journey to Troy, Hades traditions, Cardamyle, and Enope.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Homer is associated with writing the Iliad and Odyssey, whose work Quintus Smyrnaeus echoes while differing in incidents.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Homer is described as an almost mythical blind, old, wandering minstrel traditionally associated with Scio, to whom the Iliad and Odyssey are attributed.

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Homer is presented as a traveler to Egypt and a poet who drew ideas from Egyptian customs and stories. Egyptian accounts connect his poem of the dead, Jupiter and Juno’s movement into Ethiopia, Helen’s potion, and Nepenthes to Egyptian traditions; he also mentions wealthy, hundred-gated Thebes and Orion.

Homer in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Homer is presented as a divine poet and singer whose Iliad and Odyssey, produced during the second period of early Greek epic, attained supreme perfection and universality. These poems eclipsed pre-Homeric poets, became the central works around which later Cyclic poems were written, and strongly shaped later diction, dialect, style, and epic composition, sometimes paralysing later poets’ individuality. His elevated scenes of pathos are said to reach greater literary heights than Hesiod. He is also linked to the Hymns to the Gods, Hymn to Apollo, Thebais, Epigoni, Margites, Lesser Iliad, Phocais, Expedition of Amphiarus to Thebes, The Taking of Oechalia, and other works, though several attributions are disputed. Accounts of his origin conflict. He is variously associated with Smyrna, Chios, Colophon, Ithaca, and other places; called Melesigenes, Meles, or Altes; and given Cretheis, Metis, Themista, Eugnetho, Calliope, Polycasta, Epicasta, or others as mother, with Meles River or Telemachus as father. Some accounts make him kin and contemporary of Hesiod. One account begins with Margites as his first work; another has him first recite The Thebaid. He sings and performs at Delos, including at its great assembly, recites in a tanner’s yard, travels among Greek cities, and receives honors and gifts. While living with Thestorides, he composes the Lesser Iliad and Phocais. The Argives erect a brazen statue of him. He dies at Ios after falling in a clayey place, with another account connecting his death there to a riddle about lice, and is buried at Ios.

Narrative, section by section

10 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Homer’s Iliad and Odyssey achieved such perfection and universality that they eclipsed earlier epic and exerted a paralysing influence on later poets. Later poets who treated romantic subjects were drawn into Homer’s style, while only exceptional figures such as Vergil and Milton are said to have used the Homeric medium without losing individuality.

The Hesiodic Poems

Homer strongly influenced the diction, dialect, and style of the Works and Days. The Shield of Heracles is criticized for its servile dependence on Homeric models, especially the description of Heracles’ shield in imitation of the shield of Achilles.

The Ionic School

Homer’s poems were treated as the central works around which the later Cyclic poems were written. The text places Homer and the Homeric poems before the authors and dated works of the Cyclic poets.

BIBLIOGRAPHY

Homer is mentioned in connection with the early editions of the Homeric Hymns, the Epic Cycle, and related poems. Demetrius Chalcondyles’s 1488 edition included the Hymns, Epigrams, and Battle of the Frogs and Mice in the editio princeps of Homer.

HOMER’S EPIGRAMS2601

Homer asks fishermen whether they have caught anything. He responds to their claim that they left behind everything they caught and carried home everything they did not catch by observing that they descend from fathers who possess neither rich lands nor countless sheep.

HOMERICA / THE EXPEDITION OF AMPHIARAUS

Homer sat in a tanner’s yard and recited his poetry to those present. He had composed the Hymns to the Gods.

THE TAKING OF OECHALIA

Homer is associated with The Taking of Oechalia, although the poem's attribution is disputed. One account says that Homer gave the poem's attribution to Creophylus after being hosted by him; another says that Homer lent his name to Creophylus's poem.

THE PHOCAIS

While living with Thestorides, Homer composed the Lesser Iliad and the Phocais.

THE MARGITES

The Margites is attributed to Homer in the account of Pigres.

OF THE ORIGIN OF HOMER AND HESIOD, AND OF THEIR CONTEST

Homer is associated with Smyrna, Chios, Colophon, Ithaca, and other places, and differing accounts give him various parents and names, including Meles, Melesigenes, and Altes. He composed or performed the Iliad, Odyssey, Thebais, Epigoni, Margites, and Hymn to Apollo, traveled among Greek cities, received honors and gifts, and died at Ios after failing to understand a riddle about lice.

Homer in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Homer is a poet associated with the Iliad, the Odyssey, the Poems, and catalogues within the Iliad, especially the Catalogue of Agamemnon’s forces, the Catalogue of the ships, and the Catalogue of the Phocians. His work ranges across places, peoples, divine figures, myths, and natural features: it names Ægialus, Helice, Poseidon of Helice, Ægæ, Graia, Copæ, the palm in Delos, Great Bear, the lake Cephisis, and the tomb of Æpytus; describes Arcadians, Ilithyia, the Graces, Styx, and Achelous; and preserves such matters as the song of Linus, Sardonic laughter, and the friendship of Theseus and Pirithous. The poems distinguish what they do and do not say: the Iliad does not mention giants, while the Odyssey calls the Læstrygones giantlike and presents giants as mortal. Homer also depicts the Phæacians as near the gods, tells of the orphaned daughters of Pandareus, and represents the slaying of Otus and Ephialtes. Tradition surrounds the poet himself. He was said to have written his Poems in a cave near the springs of the Meles at Smyrna; a brazen statue of him stood at Delphi beside an oracle concerning his fatherland, death, and a riddle. Ios and Cyprus claimed him, Ios was said to contain his tomb, and Clymene or Themisto were named as his mother. Yet his fatherland and age are explicitly unknown, and blindness is associated with him. His standing is marked by the claim that the Hymns of Orpheus were second only to the Hymns of Homer in composition, while debate over his period was left unresolved.

Narrative, section by section

8 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER V.

Homer was traditionally said to have written his Poems in a cave near the springs of the river Meles at Smyrna.

CHAPTER XVI.

Homer mentioned the tomb of Æpytus in his lines about the Arcadians. Pausanias thought Homer may have regarded it as remarkable because he had seen no more notable tomb.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Homer frequently introduces Styx into his poetry. He depicts its water in Hera’s oath, has it flow into the Titaresius in a catalogue, and presents it as a river of Hades in Athene’s account of Hercules.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Homer does not mention the giants in the Iliad, but in the Odyssey he compares the Læstrygones to giants and has the king of the Phæacians describe the Phæacians as near the gods, like the Cyclopes and the race of giants. The text also cites Homer as showing that the giants were mortal.

CHAPTER XXX.

The text declines to decide the period of Hesiod and Homer because of the disputatiousness of those who discuss poetic subjects. The Hymns of Orpheus are said to be second only to the Hymns of Homer in composition.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Homer mentioned the Graces, calling one Charis and saying that she was the wife of Hephæstus. He also wrote that Sleep desired Pasithea, one of the younger Graces.

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

Homer mentions the lake Cephisis as an existing feature but does not say that Hercules made it. He also indicates that much wealth came to Orchomenus in the period of the Trojan War.

CHAPTER XXIV.

A brazen statue of Homer stands on a pillar at the temple at Delphi, beside an oracle concerning his fatherland, death, and the riddle of young boys. Ios and Cyprus each claim Homer, and traditions identify Clymene or Themisto as his mother; the text gives no private account of his country or age.

Homer in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Homer is an almost mythical poet, traditionally a blind, old, wandering minstrel associated with Scio and Elysium. The Iliad and Odyssey are traditionally attributed to him, though scholars question a single author; the prevailing view credits him with their framework and much structure, later enlarged by interpolations, additions, or rhapsodists. Herodotus assigns him to 850 B.C., while seven cities claim his birthplace.
