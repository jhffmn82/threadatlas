  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Nestor is the youngest son of Neleus and Chloris and one of the Grecian commanders in the Trojan War.
  • In The Odyssey, Nestor rules Pylos, receives Telemachus and Minerva hospitably, recounts the Greeks’ returns, and sends Telemachus onward with provisions and Pisistratus.
  • In The Iliad, Nestor is an aged Achaean and Pylian king, commander, counselor, warrior, and leader who advises the Greeks, arranges their forces, tends the wounded, and teaches chariot racing.
  • In The Library, Nestor sails with Diomedes and Menelaus and shares a prosperous voyage with Diomedes.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Nestor is an aged Lapith warrior and ruler of Pylos who recounts the battle between the Lapiths and Centaurs and defends Hercules’s bravery.
  • In The Library, Nestor alone survives Heracles’s attack on Pylus and, after being raised among the Gerenians, marries Anaxibia and fathers several children.
  • In Description of Greece, Nestor is associated with Pylos, where traditions place his house, image, oxen’s stall, and tomb, and he is said to have recovered Machaon’s bones and ruled Messenia.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Nestor sails with Menelaus, praises Theseus and Pirithous, and appears depicted with a hat and spear beside a horse.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Nestor is an aged Achaean leader and counselor who mourns Antilochus, urges Achilles to avenge him, advises the Argives, and supports the Wooden Horse plan.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Nestor is an old Greek chief who urges Agamemnon’s embassy to Achilles, advises Patroclus, and carries Machaon from battle.

Nestor in The Odyssey

Homer · -0632~/-0599~

Nestor, son of Neleus, rules Pylos and has reigned for three generations as guardian of the public weal. Husband of Eurydice and father of several sons, including Pisistratus, he is known for counsel and generous hospitality. When Telemachus and Minerva arrive during a Pylian sacrifice, Nestor receives them, recounts the Achaeans’ troubled return and deaths, advises a visit to Menelaus, recognizes Minerva, sacrifices to her, and sends Telemachus onward with horses, provisions, and Pisistratus.

Nestor in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Nestor is an aged Achaean and Pylian king: son of Neleus, ruler of Pylos and the Pylian people in his third generation of rule, knight of Gerene, warrior, commander, and father of Thrasymedes. Though old age restricts his physical strength and prevents him from boxing or wrestling, his judgment, eloquence, and counsel make him the foremost councillor in Agamemnon’s esteem and a leader whom the Achaeans hear sooner than any other. He commands ninety ships’ worth of Pylian and neighboring forces, rivals Menestheus in marshalling troops, and owns notable possessions including a gold shield and a richly worked cup brought from home. At the outset, he supports arming the Achaeans, advises Agamemnon to organize them by tribes and clans, recalls signs concerning Troy, and presses for prompt action. He arranges the Pylians for battle, instructing charioteers to hold formation and fight with spears rather than separately. He rebukes fear of Hector, invokes his youthful defeat of Ereuthalion, and proposes both a chosen champion and defenses for the ships. When his horse is disabled and Hector threatens him, Diomed rescues him; Nestor then urges retreat when Jove favors Hector. As the crisis deepens, Nestor recommends watchfulness, feasting, counsel, gifts, and conciliatory words to address Agamemnon’s conflict with Achilles. He helps inspect the guards, proposes reconnaissance in the Trojan camp, and warns sentinels against sleep. He also removes and tends the wounded Machaon, reports the wounded leaders to Patroclus, and urges Patroclus to persuade Achilles to send aid. After the wall falls, he joins deliberation among the wounded chiefs and later restores courage by appealing to the Achaeans’ families, property, and honor. At the games, he teaches Antilochus chariot-racing skill and receives an urn from Achilles, recalling earlier victories in boxing, wrestling, running, and spear-throwing, while acknowledging that the sons of Actor surpassed him in chariot races.

Nestor in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV

Ovid · 0008~

Nestor is an eloquent, long-lived aged man: a Lapith warrior, Greek leader, son of Neleus, and ruler of Pylos after his family’s destruction. In earlier years he witnessed Cæneus, fought Centaurs with a sword, received a scar from Teleboas’s javelin, and later was not sent to Troy when he believed he should have been. With his third century in progress and age failing, he narrates the Lapith–Centaur battle, Cænis’s transformation and Cæneus’s death, and his own killings. Challenged by Tlepolemus, he acknowledges Hercules’s killing of eleven brothers and Periclymenus without denying Hercules’s bravery.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

FABLES III. AND IV. [XII.146-535]

Nestor tells the Grecian chiefs that he once saw Cæneus endure a thousand blows without injury. He recounts Cænis’s transformation, the battle at Pirithoüs’s wedding, and Cæneus’s death.

Nestor narrates the fighting and claims to have killed or helped kill several Centaurs, including Phlegræus, Hyles, Hiphinoüs, Clanis, Dorylas, Phæocomes, Chthonius, and Teleboas. He says that he should have been sent to Troy, but that his age is now failing, and he recalls the deeds and names of many other warriors.

FABLES V. AND VI. [XII.536-628]

Nestor relates the defeat of the Lapithae and Centaurs and is challenged by Tlepolemus for not mentioning Hercules's exploits. He explains that Hercules killed eleven of his brothers and Periclymenus, while declaring that he will not avenge them further by denying Hercules's brave deeds.

Nestor in Description of Greece

Nestor, son of Neleus, is associated with Pylos, where traditions place his house, image, oxen’s stall, and tomb. Some said he was raised in Gerenia or fled there after Hercules took Pylos. He recovered Machaon’s bones and, after Aphareus’s sons died, received and ruled Messenia; his descendants were later expelled by the Dorians and returning Heraclidæ.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIII.

Nestor was said by some to have been brought up in Gerenia, while another tradition said that he fled there after Hercules took Pylos. The text also says that Nestor recovered the bones of Machaon.

BOOK IV.--MESSENIA.

After the deaths of the sons of Aphareus, the kingdom of Messenia devolved upon Nestor, the son of Neleus. His descendants were later driven from Messenia by the Dorians and the return of the Heraclidæ.

CHAPTER XXXIV.

Pylos contains a house called Nestor’s house, a painting of Nestor, and his tomb inside the city. A cave there is said to have been the stall of Nestor’s oxen and, earlier, of Neleus’s oxen.

Nestor in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Nestor, son of Neleus, is an aged Achaean leader, father, warrior, counsellor, and unmatched speaker, valued for ancient wisdom and eloquence even as age limits his fighting. His grief is central: Memnon kills his son Antilochus, and Nestor, unable to face Memnon himself, calls for help to protect the body and urges Achilles to recover and avenge the dead. Later he advises Agamemnon to end lamentation and helps arrange Achilles’ body for care and burial. He also sings of Thetis, Peleus’ wedding, and Achilles’ victories, receives horses for his song, gives them to comrades, and halts a wrestling contest. He counsels the Argives to obey Zeus, withdraw to the ships, and trust that Fate will bring Troy’s fall. At the end, he supports the Wooden Horse plan but, barred by age, remains at Tenedos with Agamemnon.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK II

Nestor witnesses the death of his son Antilochus and is overcome with anguish. He calls on Thrasymedes to help drive Memnon away from Antilochus's corpse, but his age prevents him from fighting Memnon directly.

Then to the side of Aeacus' mighty son

Nestor comes to Achilles grieving for Antilochus and tells him that Memnon has slain his son. He urges Achilles to help recover the dead and avenge his friend.

So the kings drew him from that stricken field

Nestor, son of Neleus, advises Agamemnon to end the lament for the day and order the care of the dead. He helps direct the washing, dressing, and laying out of Achilles' body.

BOOK IV

Nestor sings of Thetis, Peleus’ wedding, the deeds of Achilles, and Achilles’ victories. He gives the horses awarded to him to his comrades, and later stops the wrestling contest between Aias son of Telamon and Diomedes.

Now had the Argives burst the gates, had breached

Neleus' son tells the Argives that their strength is helpless while Zeus protects Troy and urges them to return to the ships. He recalls Zeus's destruction of the Titans, advises obedience to Zeus's signs, and encourages the Argives with the hope that Fate will soon lead them to destroy Troy.

BOOK XII

Nestor supports the plan and encourages the warriors to enter the Wooden Horse, recalling his former strength and longing to join them. Achilles' son prevents him from entering because of his age, and Nestor remains with the fleet at Tenedos alongside Agamemnon.
