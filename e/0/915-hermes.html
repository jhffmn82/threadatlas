  • In The Aeneid, Mercury is Jupiter’s divine messenger who directs Aeneas, secures welcome for the Trojans, orders Aeneas to leave Carthage, and warns him in a dream that Dido intends to die.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Hermes is praised for inventing speech, names, letters, music, arithmetic, and the arts and sciences, establishing worship, observing the stars, teaching wrestling, and serving Osiris as scribe and adviser.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Mercury, son of Maia and Jupiter, invents arts, sciences, wartime messages, measures, merchandise, and wrestling, gives Jasion a harp, and writes sacred inscriptions.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Hermes carries Helen through the air, conceals her, and leaves her with Proteus in Egypt so she remains undefiled and can return to Sparta.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Hermes is Zeus’s herald and courier who kills Argus, demands information from Prometheus, and warns him of burial and eagle torment.
  • In The Odyssey, Mercury is the gods’ messenger, guide, and guardian who warns Aegisthus, orders Calypso to release Ulysses, protects Ulysses from Circe, aids Hercules, rewards Autolycus, and leads the suitors’ ghosts to the dead.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Hermes is the swift, luck-bringing messenger, guide, and herald of Zeus and the gods; he invents the lyre, steals Apollo’s cattle, slays Argus, frees Persephone, and fathers Pan.
  • In The Iliad, Mercury escorts Priam and Idaeus safely to Achilles, putting the guards to sleep, opening the gates, delivering them and their treasure, and returning to Olympus.
  • In The Library, Hermes is Zeus and Maia’s herald who steals Apollo’s cattle, invents the lyre and shepherd’s pipe, gains Apollo’s wand and prophetic gifts, fathers Cephalus and Abderus, kills Hippolytus and Argus, and aids Perseus, Hercules, and Ulysses.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Mercury is Jupiter’s winged herald and messenger who kills Argus, steals Apollo’s cattle, turns Battus to stone, pursues Herse, and begets Hermaphroditus.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Mercury, disguised as a man with the caduceus, accompanies Jupiter to Philemon and Baucis, rewards their hospitality, punishes their village, and fathers Autolycus through Chione.
  • In The Library, Hermes kills Argus and Hippolytus, gives Perseus a sickle and Amphion a lyre, identifies Medusa as a phantom, purifies Danaus’s daughters, and guides Perseus to the Phorcides.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Hermes is a god worshiped throughout Greece as messenger, psychopomp, lyre-maker, Ram-carrier, and Champion.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Mercury is Jupiter and Maia’s messenger, a god of winged footwear, the caduceus, commerce, wrestling, thievery, and invention who lulls and kills Argus and helps spare Baucis and Philemon.

Hermes in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

Hermes is a deathless god, born on Cyllene to Zeus and Maia, and is chiefly the swift, luck-bringing messenger, guide, and herald of Zeus, the blessed gods, and the deathless gods. He serves Zeus, bears a golden rod or staff of riches and wealth, gives grace, good, and good things, and is asked to aid the wisdom and strength of men. He rules or is lord of Cyllene and Arcadia, is associated with Cyllene’s sacred place, and receives authority over cattle, birds of omen, various animals, and service as messenger to Hades. From his first day, Hermes is defined by inventive speed and cunning. Born on the fourth day of the month, he contrives the lyre from a tortoise shell, invents fire-making and pipes, and has sandals woven from tamarisk and myrtle twigs. While still new-born, he steals fifty of Apollo’s cattle, drives them to the Alpheus, sacrifices two, returns unseen to his cradle, and elaborately denies the theft. Brought before Zeus and the immortals with Apollo, he reveals and gives Apollo the lyre; Apollo promises friendship, while Hermes receives cattle-keeping and further powers. Elsewhere he steals twelve heifers, a hundred unyoked cows, and a bull from herds associated with Apollo and Admetus. He tests Battus in disguise, and when Battus reveals the cattle’s location, strikes him with his staff and changes him into a rock. Hermes is repeatedly a robber, thief, cattle driver, night watcher, dream-bringer, and cunning trickster, but also a worshipful and dear divine helper. He uses stupor and strangling upon dogs connected with a stolen herd, and he slays Argus, giving him the title Slayer of Argus. At Zeus’s command he travels to Erebus, persuades Hades to release Persephone, drives the immortal horses, and returns her to Demeter’s temple. He leads Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite to Alexandrus for judgment; carries Aphrodite to learn she will wed Anchises; and tells Tros what happened to Ganymedes. He is also Pan’s father: after tending sheep for a mortal man and marrying the daughter of Dryops, he carries Pan to the deathless gods.

Hermes in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Hermes is a god, especially regarded by the people of Pheneus, who held Hermæa games and maintained a temple for him. According to Arcadian and local traditions, he was washed after birth by mountain nymphs at the wells of Tricrena, was brought up at Acacesius, and was believed to have been reared under purslane. He bears a title from Cyllen and is associated with Mount Cyllene, where a ruined temple held an eight-foot juniper-wood statue. At Chelydorea he found a tortoise, skinned it, and made a lyre from its shell; he later taught Amphion to play it and appears at Helicon contending with Apollo over a lyre. He is Zeus’s messenger, receives Zeus’s sceptre from Zeus, and conveys departed souls to Hades. Greek tradition makes him father of Myrtilus. His worship and images range from roadside, square, stone, and wooden statues to temples shared with the Muses and Apollo or formerly with Hercules. At Tanagra he was honored as Ram-carrier and Champion: he ended pestilence by carrying a ram around the walls, led the young men against the Eretrians, and chiefly caused the Euboeans’ rout using a scraper. At Pharæ, sacred fish, an oracle, a well, and thirty god-named stones accompanied his statue.

Narrative, section by section

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXII.

Hermes is honored at Pharæ through a statue, an oracle, a sacred well, and thirty square stones near the statue that are each named for one of the gods. The fish in Hermes’ well are not caught because they are considered sacred to him.

CHAPTER XIV.

The people of Pheneus paid special regard to Hermes, held games called Hermæa, and had a temple dedicated to him. His temple contained a stone statue made by the Athenian Euchir, son of Eubulides.

CHAPTER XVI.

Hermes was said to have been washed by mountain nymphs in the three wells at Tricrena after his birth. The wells were consequently regarded as sacred to him.

CHAPTER XVII.

Cyllenian Hermes has a ruined temple on the top of Mount Cyllene. His statue is made from juniper wood rather than ebony, cypress, cedar, oak, yew, or lotus, and is about eight feet high.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Hermes had a joint temple with the Muses and Apollo, of which only a few foundations remained, and a statue of him stood there in the form of a square Hermes. A joint temple to Hercules and Hermes no longer existed except for its altar; Hermes was also represented as a square god among the Workers and was famed as Zeus's messenger who conveyed the souls of the dead to Hades.

CHAPTER XXII.

Hermes is worshipped at Tanagra as the Ram-carrier and the Champion. He is said to have ended a pestilence by carrying a ram around the walls, and Calamis made a statue of him carrying a ram; he is also said to have led Tanagra’s young men against the Eretrians and helped rout them with a scraper. Purslane is preserved in his temple as the Champion because it was believed that he was reared under that tree.

Mercury in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Mercury, or Hermes, is Jupiter and Maia’s son and Jupiter’s messenger, a god marked by winged footwear, the caduceus, and skillful arts including commerce, wrestling, gymnastics, thievery, dexterity, and inventing the lyre. He also uses the Syrinx pipes and aids others with a harpe, moly, guidance, and Jupiter’s commands. In the recorded episodes, he descends disguised as a shepherd to lull and kill Argus, then accompanies Jupiter in human shape, receives Baucis and Philemon’s hospitality, and helps spare them from their village’s punishment.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter I

Mercury, or Hermes, was the son of Jupiter and Maia and the messenger of Jupiter. He presided over commerce, wrestling, gymnastics, thievery, and activities requiring skill and dexterity; he wore winged footwear and carried the caduceus, and he invented the lyre.

Chapter III

Mercury goes to earth at Jupiter's command to dispatch Argus. Disguised as a shepherd, he plays the Syrinx pipes, tells stories, and finally kills Argus when all of Argus's eyes are asleep.

Chapter IV

Mercury accompanied Jupiter in human shape while seeking rest and shelter in Phrygia. He was welcomed and fed by Baucis and Philemon and joined Jupiter in sparing them from the village's punishment.
