  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Sparta is described as the capital of the Lacedaemonians, which protected Sphodrias, directed operations in Corcyra, lost its principality after Leuctra, and resisted Epaminondas’s invasion.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Sparta is described as a Greek power and Peloponnesian bulwark that fought in Greece and Asia, endured invasion and earthquake, and imposed its authority on neighboring peoples.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Lacedaemon is described as sending Gylippus and additional troops to aid Syracuse and breaking its league with Athens at Alcibiades’s instigation.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Sparta is described as a realm and homeland associated with Menelaus, Helen, and Hermione, from which Menelaus returns or brings aid and against which some characters criticize its people and customs.
  • In The Odyssey, Sparta is described as a destination in Telemachus’s planned search for news of Ulysses, if he can obtain a ship and crew.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Sparta is described as the place where Menelaus entertains Alexandrus, who gives gifts to Helen before she leaves with him while Menelaus is away.
  • In The Iliad, Sparta and the surrounding Lacedaemonian settlements send sixty ships under Menelaus, with their contingent drawn up apart from the others.
  • In The Library, Sparta is described as the kingdom ruled by Menelaus after Tyndareus gave it to him, from which Alexander abducted Helen after visiting and persuading her to leave.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Sparta is described as unfortified because Lycurgus trusted the valor and patriotism of its citizens, and as honoring Hyacinthus.
  • In The Library, Lacedaemon is described as the second territory assigned by lot, whose serpent sign foretells rulers terrible in attack.
  • In Description of Greece, Sparta is described as a city of the Lacedæmonians with two royal houses, a civic, sacred, and martial landscape, authority over surrounding territories, and a history of warfare and political change.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Sparta is described as a city marked by warfare, political disputes, civic customs, changing defenses, and restored national training.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Lacedæmon is described as a Peloponnesian region once inhabited by Achæans, later occupied by Dorians and Lacedæmonians, and involved in conflicts with Messene, the Achæans, and Rome.

Sparta in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Sparta is a city, the capital of the Lacedaemonians. It protected Sphodrias before its senate after his attempt to seize the Piraeus, then reorganized its forces and policy toward remaining confederates. Answering supporters in Corcyra, it ordered Alcidas there and sent Mnasippus's fleet to seize the island. Fearing Theban loss of its sovereign command of Greece, it suffered defeat at Leuctra and lost its principality; chief nobles were received by Antipater as hostages. During Epaminondas's invasion, Sparta was besieged and repeatedly threatened or attacked. Agis warned it, Agesilaus organized defense, Dionysius supplied Gauls and Spaniards, and its defenders resisted until the attackers withdrew.

Sparta in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Sparta is a Greek power, militarily active in Greece and Asia, lying close to the sides of Peloponnesus and described as a castle or bulwark defending it. After Hercules defeated Hippocoon and his sons and took Sparta, he restored Tyndarus to its kingdom on condition that he preserve it for his posterity. Sparta sent Leonidas to command Thermopylae, where the expedition was presented as dying for common liberty and advancing Sparta’s fame. It detained Themistocles and other ambassadors during an inspection of Athens’s walls, then released them; it ordered Pausanias to free Greek cities from Persian garrisons but later punished him for treason. Pausanias’s conduct brought contempt, though Hetaemaridas prevented war with Athens for dominion of the sea. A devastating earthquake killed more than twenty thousand people, after which Helots and Messenians attacked. Sparta supported Megara against Athens despite being poorer and weaker at sea, recovered after losses at Sphacteria, defeated Argives and allies at Mantinea, received Alcibiades, opposed Persian power in Asia, and imposed terms on Elis and Messenia. Its tyrannical government caused hatred among confederates.

Sparta in Hecuba and other plays

Euripides · -0429~

Sparta is a realm and homeland associated chiefly with Menelaus, Helen, and Hermione. Menelaus repeatedly seeks or begins a return there, while Helen, calling it her native realm and former home, hopes return with him will restore her standing among the Greeks and recover her former life after a false reputation of guilt. It is also the place from which Menelaus comes to aid Hermione and from which he plans to lead forces against a neighboring enemy. Its stature is invoked by Hermione, but Andromache denounces its inhabitants as treacherous, and Peleus criticizes the customs of Spartan women.

Sparta in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Sparta, also called Lacedæmon, is a city of the Lacedæmonians. Lacedæmon gave his name to the country and named the city Sparta after his wife; later accounts say Sparta was originally the city’s name, while Lacedæmon had named the whole district and then the city. It had two royal houses descended from the twin sons of Aristodemus. The city is defined by its civic, sacred, and martial landscape: a handsome market-place, public buildings, a theatre, royal tombs, monuments, temples, hero-chapels, statues, sacred precincts, fountains, groves, and the Dromus and Platanistas, where young men ran and fought under established customs. Its highest hill was called the citadel, though it lacked a notably high citadel. Its roads led toward Amyclæ, Therapne, Arcadia, and Gythium, and its territory included landmarks and routes around Caryæ, Sellasia, Thornax, and Scias. It had temples of Serapis and Olympian Zeus, and customs of honoring Enyalius in fetters, sacrificing goats to Hera, and entering battle to flutes, lyre, and harp. Sparta exercised authority beyond the city, receiving half the produce of Messenia and controlling coastal cities; it also became authority over Cardamyle. Yet Augustus liberated the Eleutherolacones from the yoke imposed by Sparta. In conflicts with the Messenians, Sparta withheld redress, prepared secretly for war, faced raids, scarcity, and riots, and escaped Aristomenes’ planned capture after warning reached Anaxander. A prophecy later foretold Sparta’s loss of its glorious flower when Messene was rebuilt, after which it resumed hostilities. Its government changed repeatedly. Cleomenes sought autocratic power and attacked the Achæans; after defeat and flight to Egypt, Sparta abandoned kingship for a republican form. Orestes later ruled as king at the Lacedæmonians’ request. Pyrrhus nearly took Sparta, prompting trenches and palisades, but the city prepared a defense. Finally, after Cleomenes’ defeat at Sellasia, Sparta was taken with Messenian, Aratus’, and Achæan help, and later faced Machanidas and Nabis.

Narrative, section by section

15 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XIII.

Pyrrhus conquered the Lacedæmonians and came within an ace of taking Sparta at his first assault. He then ravaged its territory and took much booty, while the Spartans prepared for a siege behind trenches, palisades, and other defenses.

CHAPTER VI.

Sparta was ruled by Cleomenes, who sought autocratic power and attacked the Achæans. After his defeat and flight to Egypt, the Spartans abandoned kingly government and retained a republican form.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Sparta came under the rule of Orestes after the Lacedæmonians requested him as king. The Heraclidæ later turned Tisamenus out of Lacedæmon and Argos.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Lacedæmon gave his own name to the country and named the city Sparta after his wife. Sparta had two royal houses descended from the twin sons of Aristodemus.

CHAPTER X.

Sparta was originally the name of the city and later Lacedæmon, which had also been the name of the whole district. It contained a handsome market-place, numerous public buildings and temples, royal tombs, hero-chapels, and monuments connected with local and Greek traditions. The text describes several roads and landmarks around the city, including Caryæ, Sellasia, Thornax, Scias, and the temples of Artemis and Apollo.

CHAPTER XIV.

Sparta contains the cenotaph of Brasidas, a theatre, the tombs of Leonidas and Pausanias, numerous temples and hero-chapels, and monuments commemorating athletic victories. Its young men practise running at the Dromus and fight at Platanistas according to established customs.

CHAPTER XV.

Sparta contains numerous hero-chapels, temples, tombs, statues, and sacred precincts, including those of Cynisca, Alcman, Hercules, Athene, Lycurgus, Artemis, and Aphrodite. Its highest hill is called the citadel, though it has no citadel rising to a notable height like the Cadmea at Thebes or Larissa among the Argives. The city’s traditions include honoring Enyalius in fetters, sacrificing goats to Hera, and going into battle to the music of flutes, lyre, and harp rather than the trumpet.

CHAPTER XIX.

The roads from Sparta lead to Amyclæ, Therapne, Arcadia, and Gythium, passing numerous temples, statues, tombs, fountains, and sacred groves. Sparta’s Lacedæmonians controlled several coastal cities, while the Emperor Augustus liberated the Eleutherolacones from the yoke imposed by the Lacedæmonians of Sparta.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Sparta was the authority over Cardamyle after Emperor Augustus detached Cardamyle from Messenia. The people of Leuctra worshipped Carnean Apollo in the same way as the people of Sparta.

BOOK IV.--MESSENIA.

Sparta received a Messenian appeal for justice against Euæphnus but did not provide redress. Its authorities later received conflicting proposals concerning Polychares and secretly prepared for war rather than accepting arbitration.

CHAPTER XI.

Sparta was the home of the Lacedæmonians and the place from which they sought Delphic advice and organized attempts to undermine the Messenians. Aristomenes entered Sparta by night and dedicated a shield at the temple of Athene Chalciœcus as a challenge to the Spartans.

CHAPTER XVI.

Sparta is threatened by Aristomenes, who intends to enter it by night and later raids nearby Amyclæ before help can arrive from Sparta. The city receives news of Aristomenes' actions and suffers scarcity and riots when cultivation is suspended during the war. The Lacedæmonians also return to Sparta to celebrate the festival of Hyacinthus.

CHAPTER XXI.

Aristomenes planned to attack and capture Sparta while most of the Lacedæmonians were at Eira or occupied with plundering. The plan failed after Aristocrates informed Anaxander at Sparta.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Sparta was foretold by Bacis to lose its glorious flower when Messene was rebuilt. The Lacedæmonians of Sparta later resumed hostilities against the restored Messenians after the Phocian War drew the Thebans away from the Peloponnese.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Sparta was taken with the help of the Messenians, Aratus, and the Achæans after the battle against Cleomenes at Sellasia. It later faced the tyrants Machanidas and Nabis.

Lacedæmon in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lacedæmon, also called Sparta, was a Peloponnesian region inhabited by Achæans during Agamemnon’s expedition against Ilium and before the Dorians returned. The Dorians expelled its Achæans; later the Lacedæmonians brought Tisamenus’s remains to Sparta by a Delphic oracle. It was connected by a road from Megalopolis and figured in conflicts involving Messene, the Achæans, Romans, Machanidas, Nabis, and Philopœmen. After the reverse at Leuctra, the rise of Megalopolis, and the proximity of the Messenians, it could not recover its former prosperity.

Description of Greece, Volume II / BOOK VII.--ACHAIA. / CHAPTER I.

Lacedæmon was one of the two Peloponnesian regions whose inhabitants were called Achæans before the Dorians returned. The Dorians expelled the Achæans from Lacedæmon, and the Lacedæmonians later removed Tisamenus's remains to Sparta in accordance with a Delphic oracle.

CHAPTER VI.

Lacedæmon was one of the regions inhabited by the Achæans during Agamemnon’s expedition against Ilium. Its later reverse at Leuctra, the rise of Megalopolis, and the proximity of the Messenians prevented it from recovering its former prosperity.

CHAPTER V.

Olæas joined with the Heraclidæ from Lacedæmon and Argos to restore Æpytus to Messene. Aristocrates was later accused of receiving bribes from Lacedæmon and betraying the Messenians.

CHAPTER XXXV.

A road from Megalopolis leads to Lacedæmon, first reaching the Alpheus and then following the river to the Thius and Phalæsiæ.

CHAPTER L.

Lacedæmon was ruled by Machanidas and later by the tyrant Nabis, and it was involved in renewed war with the Achæans. Philopœmen's victories against its forces and the killing of Machanidas affected its political and military fortunes.

CHAPTER LI.

Titus Flaminius and Diophanes marched against Lacedæmon, alleging that the Lacedæmonians were plotting against the Romans. Philopœmen later permitted Lacedæmonian fugitives to return there after Manius had departed.

Sparta in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Sparta is a city marked by warfare, political dispute, civic customs, and repeated changes to its defenses and youth training. It contains the tomb of Tisamenus at Phiditia and performs funeral rites for Talthybius. During the peace of Antalcidas, Epaminondas visited on an embassy and challenged Sparta to have its neighboring towns set an example. Spartans later came to relieve the Lacedæmonians at Leuctra; Epaminondas marched against Sparta, but Agesilaus refused battle. Sparta was then associated with lost glory before Messene’s recovery. Critolaus urged the Achæans to make war on it, and Diæus said he fought for its troubles rather than against it. After defeat, survivors defended the town while nearby places received Achæan garrisons. Its gates were shut to Titus Flaminius and Diophanes; its Nabis-strengthened fortifications and walls were demolished, and Achæan training imposed. Exiles returned, walls were rebuilt, and national training was restored.

Narrative, section by section

9 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VIII.

Sparta’s fortifications, strengthened during the power of Nabis, were destroyed by the Achæans. The Achæans also interfered with the training of its youths.

CHAPTER IX.

Sparta received back Lacedæmonians who had been exiled by the Achæans. The Spartans rebuilt walls around the city from the foundation.

CHAPTER XII.

Sparta was the subject of a dispute involving the Achæans and the Lacedæmonians. Diæus claimed he intended to fight for everything troubling Sparta rather than against Sparta itself.

CHAPTER XIII.

Sparta was defended by the Lacedæmonians after their defeat in battle, as the surviving army fled into the town. Diæus later surrounded it indirectly by securing nearby towns and placing Achæan garrisons in them.

CHAPTER XIV.

Sparta was the target of the war that Critolaus persuaded the Achæans to undertake.

CHAPTER LI.

Sparta’s gates were shut against Titus Flaminius and Diophanes when they marched toward it. Philopœmen later ordered its walls demolished and its youths to train in the Achæan fashion, although the Romans subsequently restored the national training.

CHAPTER XIII.

Sparta was the place where Epaminondas served on an embassy when the Lacedæmonians agreed to ratify the peace of Antalcidas. When Agesilaus asked whether the Bœotian towns would subscribe separately, Epaminondas answered that they would not do so until Sparta’s neighboring towns set the example.

CHAPTER XIV.

Spartans came in full force to relieve the Lacedæmonians at Leuctra. Epaminondas later marched with an army against Sparta, but Agesilaus declined to fight.

CHAPTER XV.

Sparta was said to have cut off the glory from the councils, before sacred Messene recovered her children.
