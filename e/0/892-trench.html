trench in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

The trench is a deep defensive ditch surrounding the Achaean wall, planted with stakes and intended as a bulwark for the Achaeans, their fleet, rich spoils, and ships against the Trojan wall. It first separates the Achaeans from advancing Trojans, though Hector drives them through it and many die on its stakes. Its width, banks, and stakes frighten Trojan horses and delay infantry. Later Apollo breaks its banks and bridges it, enabling Trojan crossing; it then pens in Trojans, wrecking chariots.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK VIII.

The trench separates the Achaeans from the advancing Trojans. Hector drives the Achaeans through it, and many are killed as they pass its stakes.

BOOK XII.

The trench frightens the Trojan horses because of its width, overhanging banks, and sharp stakes. The Trojan infantry hesitate to cross it after seeing the eagle and snake, but Hector orders them onward.

BOOK XV.

The trench and set stakes initially mark the point beyond which the routed Trojans halt after suffering losses. Apollo later kicks down the trench banks and makes a broad bridge so that the Trojan battalions can cross.

The fight then became more scattered, and the chieftains killed

The trench helps pen in the Trojan host against their will. Many yokes of horses snap their chariot poles in it, leaving the chariots behind.
