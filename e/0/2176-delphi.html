  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, Delphi is a city whose temple, oracle, and treasures become central to the Phocian conflict.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Delphi is the sacred city of Apollo's oracle where Achilles' son is ambushed and killed.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Delphi is Apollo's oracle shrine and the host of the Delphian games, consulted by Creon and associated with Oedipus and Orestes.
  • In The House of Atreus; Being the Agamemnon, the Libation-Bearers, and the Furies, Delphi is the site of Apollo's shrine where Orestes seeks purification and refuge.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Pytho is the site of an ancient oracle, Pythian victories, and athletic contests.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Delphi is the earth's centre-stone and the god's home, a sacred site of prophecy, sacrifice, temples, and games where Neoptolemus is killed.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Pytho is Apollo's sanctuary beneath Parnassus, named for the dragoness slain by Apollo.
  • In The Library, Delphi is the site of an oracle, Neoptolemus's death and alleged temple-burning, and Orestes' trial.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Delphi is the central oracle of Phœbus that directs Roman envoys to bring Æsculapius to Rome.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Delphi is the place of Phœbus's oracle and the Pythian games, previously associated with Earth and Themis.
  • In The Library, Delphi is the place where Themis and later Apollo deliver oracles, and where Oedipus and Hercules seek guidance.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Delphi is a town, oracle, sacred precinct, site of temples and monuments, and battlefield against the Galati.
  • In Description of Greece, Delphi is a sanctuary and city whose oracle guides communities and rulers and which resists the Galati.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Pytho is another name for Delphi, explained by genealogical tradition and by the story of Apollo's slain dragon.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Delphi is a city on Parnassus where Apollo's celebrated oracle stands over a prophetic chasm.
  • In Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone, Delphi is Apollo's shrine and the source of pronouncements about Laius and Oedipus's parentage.

Delphi in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Delphi is a city containing Apollo's temple and oracle, with sacred treasures that became central to conflict. Philomelus seized and fortified it, controlled its approaches, and used it against the Locrians. After his death, the Phocians returned and debated renewing war, but later took the treasures; decrees required repayment and restored the oracle's religious and public arrangements.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. VI.

Delphos was the site of the temple and oracle seized by Philomelus. Philomelus fortified the place, controlled its approaches, and made it the base for his operations against the Locrians.

CHAP. VII.

Delphi was the location of Apollo's temple and the sacred treasures at the center of the conflict. The Phocians returned there after Philomelus's death and held a general council to decide whether to renew the war.

CHAP. X.

Delphi was the site of the temple and oracle whose sacred treasures were taken by the Phocians. The Amphictyonic decrees required the Phocians to repay the oracle and restored its religious and public arrangements.

Delphi in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Delphi is an oracle shrine sacred to Apollo and host of the celebrated Delphian games, including foot-races and chariot races. It is sought for prophetic guidance: Creon consults Apollo there about Laius’s murder, and its oracle foretells Oedipus’s acts. The Chorus questions its guidance unless fulfilled, while Orestes wins and competes in its games.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

KING OEDIPUS

Delphi is the place where Creon goes to consult Apollo and where Laius had been traveling when he was killed. Creon returns from its prophetic hall with the command concerning Laius's murder.

CHORUS.

Delphi is the source of the oracle that Oedipus would know his mother's shame and kill the author of his life. The Chorus says it will no longer seek Delphi for divine guidance unless the oracle is visibly fulfilled.

_Enter_ CLYTEMNESTRA.

Orestes is reported to have entered the chariot race at the Delphian games, described as a celebrated contest in Hellas. His earlier victory in the foot-race is also associated with this event.

Delphi, called the earth’s centre-stone and the god’s home, is a sacred place of temple, shrine, prophecy, sacrifice, and games. A victorious chariot was consecrated there and its equipment displayed near the Parnassian shrine; an ode was intended for performance there. Alkmaion showed his house’s prophetic gift there. Finally, Neoptolemos was killed there but remained as a guardian of justice in processions and games.

Delphi is described as the god's home and as the place where Neoptolemos was killed after bringing spoils from Troy. Although the Delphian entertainers were deeply grieved, Neoptolemos remained there as one of the sons of Aiakos who watched over justice during sacrificial processions and games.

Pytho in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Pytho is a place with an old oracle concerning Laïos’ fate, watched over by Leto and her twin children, and a source of Pythian victory crowns and wreaths. It is repeatedly a site of athletic victories: Ergoteles wins twice, Pherenikos wins a race, Telesikrates wins in armour, and Hippokleas wins the boys’ two-stadion race. Apollo is there at sheep-gathering, and Neoptolemos is said to lie beneath its floor.

Hesiod · -0699~

Pytho is a goodly, rich place in a rugged glade beneath Parnassus. Named for the dragoness’s rotting after Apollo slew her, it becomes Apollo’s sanctuary and gives him the name Pythian. At Delphi, its great house receives Artemis after her hunt; later Homer asks and dedicates a silver bowl, while Hesiod consults the oracle and dedicates victory’s first fruits.

Delphi in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Delphi is a sanctuary and city centered on its renowned oracle and priestess, repeatedly consulted by communities and rulers for guidance on wars, succession, restoration, fines, famine, and political disputes. It directs the Athenians to pay Callippus’ fine, restrains Hercules from attacking Pisa, calls Iphitus to restore the Olympian games, and gives messages to the Thasians. Its pronouncements shape accounts of the Dorians, Aristodemus’ sons, Orestes’ bones, Demaratus, Lacedæmonian and Messenian conflict, and Aletes. Yet its authority is vulnerable: Cleomenes bribes its priestess, and the Phocians seize Apollo’s temple and contemplate destroying the city before Archidamus prevents it. Finally, the Galati advance to plunder its treasures, but Delphians, Phocians, and Ætolians resist and win; the Delphians then sacrifice to Pyrrhus.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

Description of Greece / BOOK I.--ATTICA.

The Galati marched toward Delphi intending to plunder the treasures of the god. The Delphians, nearby Phocians, and an Ætolian contingent resisted them, and after the victory the Delphians offered sacrifice to Pyrrhus, whose tomb they had previously dishonoured.

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Delphi was consulted concerning the return of the Dorians, the succession of Aristodemus’ sons, the bones of Orestes, and the status of Demaratus. Aristodemus was said to have died there, and Cleomenes bribed its priestess to speak falsely against Demaratus.

CHAPTER X.

Delphi was the site of the temple of Apollo seized by the Phocians. The Phocians planned to massacre its young men, enslave its women and children, and raze the city, but Archidamus successfully opposed the plan. The text calls its oracle one of the most famous.

CHAPTER XI.

Delphi was consulted by both the Lacedæmonians and the Messenians about the war. Its priestess gave prophecies concerning cunning, hidden things, the tripods of Zeus of Ithome, and the future fortunes of the opposing sides.

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Delphi issued an oracle that prevented Hercules from marching against Pisa. Its priestess later instructed Iphitus to join the people of Elis in restoring the Olympian games.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The oracle at Delphi required the Athenians to pay the fine imposed after Callippus bribed his opponents before it would again provide them with responses. Delphi is also mentioned in connection with the oracle that Aletes initially disobeyed when he refused to admit Melas and his army.

CHAPTER XI.

The Thasians sent envoys to Delphi during the famine. Delphi first instructed them to restore those who had been banished and later gave the message that they had forgotten Theagenes.

Delphi in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Delphi is a hilly town, also called Pytho, said to be named after Delphus. It is above all the location and source of an oracle, served by the Pythian Priestess and protected by the god according to an oracle. From the earliest notices, people and communities come or send messengers there for answers: Alcmæon, the Phigalians, Argives, Erginus, Orchomenians, Bœotians, Phocians, Homer, Themistocles, and Achæans seek guidance on madness, return, water, offspring, pestilence, danger, conquest, and other needs. Its oracle also determines names for the Athenian tribes. The town’s temple and sacred precincts are repeatedly central to conflict and defense. The Phocians seize the temple while Heraclides is President, and the temple is repeatedly plundered. Delphi later becomes a battlefield against the Galati: threatened by Brennus and the Galati, its inhabitants seek the oracle’s protection, Greek forces assemble, and the barbarians are defeated in what is called the greatest struggle between the Galati and the Greeks. Delphi is also a dense setting of temples, monuments, contests, and offerings. Four temples stand in a row at the entrance, including the temple of Athene Pronoia; nearby are the sacred enclosure of Phylacus, gymnasium, river Plistus, and spring of Castalia. Its precincts contain treasuries, statues, military and athletic memorials, the navel regarded by the Delphians as the centre of the world, inscriptions of the Seven Wise Men, and objects associated with Apollo and others. Peoples from many places send offerings, while statues of Antigonus, Demetrius, and Ptolemy arrive later. Delphi hosts the Pythian musical and athletic contests, with a theatre and a high stadium; below it, the road to the plain passes the Hippodrome for horse-races. Cirrha, its seaport, lies about 60 stades away.

Narrative, section by section

28 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIV.

Delphi was consulted by Alcmæon when his madness continued during his residence in Arcadia. The sons of Phegeus were said to have offered the necklace of Eriphyle to Apollo at Delphi.

CHAPTER XXXIX.

The Phigalians who escaped went to Delphi to ask the god whether they should return to Phigalia.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Delphi was the destination to which the Argives and the sons of Polynices were taking Tiresias and the spoil for Apollo. A ruler also went to Delphi to ask how water could be obtained in his district, and the Pythian Priestess gave him an instruction.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

Erginus went to Delphi to consult the oracle about obtaining offspring. The Pythian Priestess told him to put a new top on the old plough, leading him to marry a young wife.

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

The Orchomenians consulted Delphi during a pestilence that destroyed people and cattle and again about where Hesiod’s bones could be found. They also consulted Delphi when a spectre injured their land.

CHAPTER XL.

The Bœotians sent messengers to Delphi, whose Pythian Priestess directed them to Trophonius at Lebadea.

BOOK X.--PHOCIS. / CHAPTER I.

Delphi was the place to which the Phocians sent to learn how they could escape the danger posed by the Thessalians. The Phocians later sent statues of their commanders and local heroes to Apollo at Delphi.

CHAPTER II.

At Delphi, Heraclides was President when the Phocians seized the temple. The site contained, among Apollo's votive offerings, a brass imitation of an emaciated old man that was said to have been given by Hippocrates the doctor.

CHAPTER III.

Delphi was the site of the temple connected with the Phocians' punishment and later the place where they fought the Galati and the Celtic army.

CHAPTER V.

The high road from the Cross-roads to Delphi is very steep and difficult even for a well-equipped traveler. Delphi is associated with many traditions about the oracle of Apollo and the successive temples built there.

CHAPTER VI.

Delphi was said to have received its name from Delphus, whose parentage is given differently in several traditions. Its inhabitants later called the town both Pytho and Delphi, and its priestess Phemonoe delivered an oracle concerning Apollo’s defense of the Delphians.

CHAPTER VII.

Delphi was the site of the musical and athletic contests later known as the Pythian games. Homer went there to ask what was necessary for him, and the temple there was repeatedly plundered.

CHAPTER VIII.

Delphi was the meeting place of the Amphictyonic delegates and contained four temples in a row, including the temple of Athene Pronoia. Near Delphi were the sacred enclosure of Phylacus, the gymnasium, the river Plistus, and the spring of Castalia.

CHAPTER IX.

Delphi is everywhere hilly, and its sacred precincts and other parts of the town share this character. The town contains many votive offerings, including monuments connected with athletic victories, military events, local heroes, and legendary traditions.

CHAPTER X.

An oracle from Delphi determined names for the Athenian tribes. Statues of Antigonus, Demetrius, and Ptolemy were later sent to Delphi.

CHAPTER XI.

Delphi is the site of several treasuries, votive offerings, and dedications described in this unit. The Siphnii were instructed by the god to send a tenth of their gold-mining revenue there.

CHAPTER XII.

Delphi was one of the places visited by the second Herophile, who was said to have chanted while standing on the projecting stone there.

CHAPTER XIII.

Delphi received the brazen head of a Pæonian bison and many other votive offerings from peoples including the Phocians, Thessalians, Macedonians, Cyrenæans, Tarentines, and Greeks generally. Its temple traditions also include the contests of Hercules and Apollo over a tripod.

CHAPTER XIV.

Delphi received a Greek dedication of Apollo after Artemisium and Salamis. Themistocles went there to consult the Pythian Priestess and to offer spoils of the Medes, but the offering was rejected.

CHAPTER XV.

Delphi contains numerous offerings and statues dedicated after military victories, including statues of Apollo, the bronze palm, and the gilt statue of Athene. It is also the place where crows were said to have damaged the Athenian dedication before the expedition to Sicily.

CHAPTER XVI.

Delphi contains the white-stone object called the navel, which the Delphians considered the centre of the world. It also received numerous votive offerings, including the Liparæans’ statues of Apollo and Echecratides of Larissa’s small Apollo, which the Delphians regarded as the first votive offering.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Achæans sent messengers to Delphi to ask how they could capture Phana. Delphi gave them an oracle directing their attention to the water that sustained the town.

CHAPTER XIX.

Delphi contained votive offerings commemorating Scyllis and Hydna, including statues made by the Amphictyones, although Nero later took Hydna’s statue away. Its temple displayed representations of Artemis, Leto, Apollo, the Muses, the setting Sun, Dionysus, and the Thyiades, along with golden shields and Galatian arms. Delphi was the site of the greatest struggle between the Galati and the Greeks.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Delphi was threatened by Brennus and the Galati, and its inhabitants sought protection at the oracle. Greek forces assembled there to defend it, and the battle that followed ended in the barbarians’ defeat.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Delphi contains the temple at Delphi, where the sayings of the Seven Wise Men were inscribed and several objects associated with Apollo, Homer, Poseidon, the Fates, and Neoptolemus the son of Achilles were kept. The people of Delphi offer annual funeral rites at the grave of Neoptolemus.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Delphi has a handsome theatre near its temple precincts, a statue of Dionysus offered by the men of Cnidos, and a stadium in the highest part of the city. The stadium was originally made of Mount Parnassus stone and was later embellished with Pentelican marble by the Athenian Herodes.

CHAPTER XXXVI.

Delphi lies opposite Ambrosus. Medeon is identified as one of the towns that impiously plundered the temple at Delphi.

CHAPTER XXXVII.

Cirrha is the seaport of Delphi and lies about 60 stades from Delphi. The road descending from Delphi to the plain passes an Hippodrome where the Pythian horse-races are held.
