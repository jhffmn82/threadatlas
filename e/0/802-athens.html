Athens in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Athens is a city, described as the metropolis of Greece, whose record centers on leadership, alliances, democracy, war, and changing control. It administers an Athenian confederacy through allied cities and serves as the meeting place for confederate senates in which members have equal voices. It protects democracy in Greek cities, supports democratic parties and displaced communities, aids Theban exiles, and at different points assists or shares power with the Lacedaemonians. Its military activity includes fleets, armies, admirals, and generals such as Chabrias, Iphicrates, Timotheus, Chares, and Leosthenes. The record repeatedly shows Athens responding to larger threats and shifting alliances. It seeks Persian favor and support from Pharnabazus, contests Theban claims, becomes involved in the Social War, and confronts Philip: first backing Argæus, later accepting peace proposals, then judging peace broken and sending a fleet to Byzantium. Philip’s capture of Elatea alarms the city; Athens seeks Boeotian help, makes peace after Chaeronea, and executes Lysicles. The city is portrayed as strong and eminent, but also as vulnerable to treason and bribery. After Philip’s death, Athens considers resistance to Macedonian control but submits to Alexander after his advance into Boeotia. It repairs its walls, seeks pardon through ambassadors, debates surrendering orators, and secures pardon. Harpalus’s money leads it to condemn Demosthenes and other orators; it also sends part of Harpalus’s treasure to Leosthenes. Following Alexander’s death, Athens becomes a center of resistance, declares the Lamian War, equips Leosthenes, and acts for Greek liberty, though it later condemns him and appoints Chares. Athens in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Athens is a city in Attica, described as a democracy for much of its history, with archons and chief magistrates, armed citizens, garrison and colonial forces, a fleet, fortifications, allies, and great wealth. It is also praised as a nursery of learning, honorable education, philosophy, and oratory. Early accounts connect it with Egyptian grain, rites, mysteries, and social divisions; under Aegeus it endured war, drought, famine, and tribute after Androgeus’s murder, while Theseus gathered its scattered inhabitants and promoted its welfare. During the Persian invasion its inhabitants abandoned it, and Xerxes and later Mardonius destroyed the city and temples. After Plataea, Athens restored its people and walls, enlarged its harbor and fleet, and gained leadership among Greek cities. It aided confederate cities, won wealth and fame through victories over Persia, and increasingly exercised power over its confederates while fighting many wars. It brought Delos’s money into the city, had a treasury of eight thousand talents, and used its fleet, allies, and fortifications in war. Athens allied with Corcyra, besieged Potidea, sent Pericles against Samos, and maintained a thirty-year truce with the Lacedaemonians. During later war, countryside refugees crowded the city, contributing to plague; it also suffered famine and sent forces across many campaigns. The Sicilian expedition ended disastrously, after which Athens briefly replaced democracy with rule by four hundred citizens, then restored democracy and continued resistance. Despite victories and Alcibiades’s restoration, continual losses weakened it. After its fleet was destroyed, Athens was blockaded, famished, and forced to surrender to the Lacedaemonians, demolish its walls and Piraeus fortifications, limit its ships, abandon other cities, and become subject to them. Its democracy was abolished, and the Thirty ruled amid anarchy. Exiles and supporters seized the Piraeus, restored Athenians to the city and ancient laws, and Athens later joined others against the Lacedaemonians, helped rebuild its walls and Piraeus, and sent fleets to recover defecting cities.

Athens in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Athens is a place marked by ancient rule, religious monuments, civic acts, conflict, loss, and recovery. After Thymœtes was driven out, Melanthus ruled it; later, descendants of Neleus came there after displacement. Cleomenes first freed the Athenians from the sons of Pisistratus, then fought them as they defended their freedom and ravaged their territory. Athens suffered plague, was linked to traditions of Theseus, the Amazons, Pan, and Harpalus, and made Chalcinus and Dætus citizens after their return. It lost islands and naval supremacy after Philip’s treaties, endured Macedonian occupation, imposed rulers, and Lachares, then was liberated after Demetrius retained the Piraeus and fortified the Museum. Captured by Sulla after conflict with the Romans, it was injured by war but flourished again under Adrian. Its Odeum, Painted Chamber, Acropolis, theatre of Dionysus, temples, statues, tombs, public buildings, and monuments—including those of Olympian Zeus, Aphrodite, Æsculapius, Enyalius, and Wingless Victory—emphasize antiquity, religious practice, Persian victories, Theseus, and Athene.

Athens in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Athens is a city and a recurring center of refuge, military action, worship, and political reversal. It received Xuthus after his flight from Thessaly, joined Ægialus in giving Achæus an army, invited Ion to command against the Eleusinians, and welcomed the Ionians as settlers; it later served as the starting point for the sons of Codrus and for Ionians sailing toward settlement in Asia Minor. Under Codrus it faced a night entry by Lacedæmonians, while its people also received a warning from Zeus at Dodona. Athens sheltered Platæans driven from their country and Thebans expelled by Alexander, and supported restoration of Thebes with great friendliness. It was attacked or threatened by Epaminondas, involved in the plundering of Oropus, and later held Macedonian garrisons after Demades and other traitors influenced Antipater, making Athenians slaves of the Macedonians. Its civic and sacred life included the Panathenaea, the Acropolis, three Graces, and mysteries kept from the multitude.

CHAPTER II.

Athens was the starting point for the expedition led by the sons of Codrus. The Ionians sailed from Athens one generation before the account describes their settlement in Asia Minor.

CHAPTER X.

After the reverse in Bœotia, Athens did not become subject to Philip, although 1,000 were killed and 2,000 taken prisoner. After Lamia, Demades and other traitors helped bring Macedonian garrisons into Athens, and the Athenians became slaves of the Macedonians.

CHAPTER XI.

Athens was the city whose people plundered Oropus and refused to fulfill the agreement after the garrison’s outrage. Menalcidas and Callicrates later tried to persuade the Achæans to invade Attica.

CHAPTER XXV.

Athens was the city whose people received the warning from Zeus at Dodona. During the reign of Codrus, Lacedæmonians entered Athens by night, but some fled to the Areopagus when the Athenians began to muster against them.

BOOK IX.--BŒOTIA. / CHAPTER I.

Athens received the Platæans after they were driven from their country, including after Platæa was captured during the Peloponnesian war.

CHAPTER VII.

Athens received the Thebans after Alexander expelled them from Thebes.

CHAPTER XIV.

Epaminondas pursued Iphicrates and the Athenians as far as Athens. He returned to Thebes when Iphicrates would not allow the Athenians to go out and fight.

CHAPTER XXXV.

Athens traditionally honoured the Graces Auxo and Hegemone, and also honoured the Seasons Carpo and Thallo with Pandrosus. At the entrance to the Acropolis there were three Graces, near which mysteries were celebrated, and Socrates made statues of them there for the Athenians.

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Athens is a place named after Athene and the capital of Attica. It first suffers tribute to Minos, until Theseus frees it, becomes king, unites Attica’s tribes, and establishes the Panathenaea. After victory over Persia, Athens rebuilds and becomes a political and artistic center under Pericles and Pheidias, producing major works including the Parthenon, Caryatids, and Nike Apteros, before later losing artistic significance.

Athens in Plays of Sophocles: Oedipus the King; Oedipus at Colonus; Antigone

Sophocles · 1912

Athens is a city, called the most devout among States, ruled by Theseus and known for piety, hospitality to vexed strangers, law, justice, and protection of suppliants. Antigone recognizes it, and Oedipus appeals for shelter despite the Chorus’s fear that his curse may endanger the land. Theseus grants Oedipus citizen rights and Athens’ protection, then defends his daughters against Creon, insisting that conduct be justified by law.
