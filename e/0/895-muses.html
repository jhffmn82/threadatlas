  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, the Muses are wise daughters associated with song, poetic inspiration, and triumphal glory.

  • In The Odyssey, the Muse is the divine daughter of Olympian Jove who inspires bards and is invoked to tell of Ulysses' travels and sufferings.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, the Muses are Olympian goddesses and singers who inspire poetic song, teach Hesiod, and sing of gods and mortals.

  • In The Iliad, the Muses are Olympian goddesses who dwell on Olympus, sing at the gods' banquet, recount the Danaans, and punish Thamyris.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, the Muses are virgin daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne who inhabit Helicon and defeat the Pierides in song.
  • In The Library, the Muses are daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne who teach the Sphinx's riddle and defeat Thamyris in music.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, the Muses are first honored on Helicon, later numbered as nine, and said to sing at Harmonia's marriage.
  • In The Fall of Troy, the Muses come from Helicon to mourn Achilles and promise that inspired minstrels will preserve his glory and might.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, the Muses are nine daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne who preside over song and memory and are called Camenae in Latin usage.

Muses in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Muses are generally described as nine daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne, though accounts differ on their parentage and number. Associated with liberal arts, they attend Dionysius on expeditions and are credited by some with inventing letters and composing poetry.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

DIODORUS SICULUS- / BOOK IV.

The Muses are generally said to be the nine daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne, although some writers give them a different parentage or number. They are associated with poetry, harmony, dancing, singing, astrology, and other liberal arts, and are said to attend Dionysius on his expeditions.

CHAP. IV.

The Muses were credited by some with inventing letters and composing verses called poetry. Others attributed the invention of letters to the Syrians, saying that the Phoenicians altered their form and transmitted them to the Grecians.

Muses in Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica

Hesiod · -0699~

The Muses are Olympian goddesses, nine gold-crowned daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne, described as bright-eyed, sweet- and clear-voiced divine singers. They dwell on holy Mount Helicon, dance at its spring and at Delphi, join the Graces in song, follow Apollo, and delight in feasts, dances, and music. They sing of Zeus, the gods, divine genealogy, immortals, and mortal men, and can make a man wise and eloquent. They are central to poetic song: singers arise through them, and those they love speak sweetly. They met Hesiod while he tended sheep on Mount Helicon, taught him divine song, and inspired him. Yet they declare that they can tell false tales resembling truth as well as truth when they choose. Across the records, they are invoked to honor song and to tell of women and the Moon.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

The Muses are said to have met Hesiod while he was tending sheep on Mount Helicon and to have taught him a glorious song. The Theogony also presents the Muses as the source of poetic inspiration and as declaring that they can tell both feigned tales resembling truth and truth itself.

THE THEOGONY

The Muses of Helicon dwell on the holy mount, dance near its springs, and sing of Zeus, the gods, and the race of immortals. They told Hesiod that they know how to speak many false things as though they were true, but can utter true things when they choose.

Muses are a group first honored on Helicon by Ephialtes and Otus. Thought by the sons of Aloeus to be three—Melete, Mneme, and Aoide—they were later established as nine at Thespia by Pierus; they are also brought from Trapezus as old wooden statues and sing at Harmonia’s marriage.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIX.

Ephialtes and Otus are said to have first sacrificed to the Muses on Helicon. The sons of Aloeus thought the Muses were three and called them Melete, Mneme, and Aoide, but Pierus later established nine Muses at Thespia and gave them the names they now have. Before sacrificing to the Muses, people performed annual funeral rites for Linus.

Muses in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

The Muses are nine daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne who preside over song and memory, each assigned to literature, art, or science. They sing to Apollo responsively; in Latin usage they are called Camenae, a broader group including fountain nymphs such as Egeria.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter I

The Muses were nine daughters of Jupiter and Mnemosyne, or Memory. They presided over song and memory, with each assigned to a department of literature, art, or science.

Chapter XV

The Camenae are the Latin name for the Muses and also include some other deities, principally nymphs of fountains. Egeria is one of them.
