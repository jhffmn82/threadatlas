  • In The Amores, or Amours, Juno appoints a keeper for horned Io and receives festival honors at Falisci through rites involving goats.
  • In The Aeneid, Juno is Queen of Heaven and Carthage’s guardian who persecutes the Trojans, supports Turnus, foments war in Italy, and ultimately withdraws after securing the Latins’ identity.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Juno is Jupiter’s wife and Hercules’s jealous stepmother who delays his birth, sends serpents against him, and causes his madness.
  • In The Argonautica, Hera is queen of marriage and Jason’s divine protector who guides the Argonauts, aids their quest for the fleece, and helps secure Jason and Medea’s marriage.
  • In Hecuba and other plays, Juno is Jove’s hostile and deceptive consort who drives Hercules mad and opposes Paris while later favoring Menelaus, Helen, and their return.
  • In Four Plays of Aeschylus, Hera is queen of heaven whose jealous hatred of Zeus’s love for Io transforms and torments Io through Argus and the gadfly.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Hera is Zeus’s wife, queen of the gods and of marriage, mother of Eileithuia, and a goddess associated with Argos, sacrifices, and Hebe.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Hera is Zeus’s golden-throned wife and queen who bears several gods, blinds Teiresias, guards Io, seeks a child without Zeus, and attacks Alexandrus and Helen.
  • In The Iliad, Juno is queen of the gods and a fierce supporter of the Greeks who opposes Troy, deceives Jupiter, and repeatedly aids the Argives.
  • In The Library, Hera is Zeus’s daughter, wife, and mother of Hephaestus who persecutes Io, Semele, Hercules, and others, while also helping the Argonauts.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Juno is Jupiter’s sister and wife, queen of the gods, and a jealous goddess who punishes his lovers and their relations.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Juno is Saturn’s daughter and a relentless enemy of Hercules who obstructs his birth, drives him mad, and later supports the Rutulians and Sabines.
  • In The Library, Hera is Zeus’s wife and a jealous goddess who pursues Io and Leto, delays and torments Hercules, opposes rivals, blinds Tiresias, and assists the Argonauts.
  • In Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II, Hera is a goddess whose titles mark her as maiden, married woman, and widow, and whose temples, images, and worship are described.
  • In Description of Greece, Hera is a goddess honored at numerous sanctuaries with statues, sacrifices, festivals, games, and traditions concerning her marriage to Zeus.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Juno is Jupiter’s queen whose jealousy drives her persecution of Io, Callisto, Hercules, Semele, Bacchus, Paris, and the Trojans.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Juno is the female spirit who gives every woman being, protects her throughout life, and receives birthday offerings.

Juno in The Aeneid

Virgil · -0028/-0018

Juno is a goddess, Queen of Heaven, wife and sister of Jupiter, a divine queen who governs marriage and guards the nuptial vow. She requires vows, gifts, and prayers, is invoked as gracious at a feast, and is especially tied to Carthage as its guardian, though she fears the Trojan prophecy that Carthage will be destroyed. Her ancient hatred of the Trojan people—linked to Paris, Ganymede, Electra, injury to her marriage, and fear for Carthage—drives the record’s central conflict. In Book I she persecutes Aeneas’s voyaging Trojans and commands Aeolus to unleash winds against their fleet. Yet Helenus directs the Trojans to honor her with sacrifice, gifts, and prayer for safe passage. In Book IV, Juno sees Dido’s love for Aeneas and proposes their union to Venus as a bond between their peoples. She scatters a hunt with a storm and brings Dido and Aeneas into a cave to complete the intended bridal union, thereby delaying Aeneas’s destiny; later she sends Iris to release Dido from death’s struggle. She also sends Iris to incite Trojan women to burn the fleet. When the Trojans reach Latium and obtain a promise of peace, Juno is enraged and resolves to postpone the marriage of Aeneas and Lavinia. She commands Alecto to destroy peace and foment war, opens the gates of Mars, and sustains war against the Trojan settlement. As protector of Turnus and partisan of the Rutulians, she aids him repeatedly: she sends Iris to find him, seeks to save him from death, warns of Lausus’s danger, and creates a cloud-made Aeneas phantom that draws Turnus away by ship. She denies responsibility for the war and blames Aeneas and Venus. At the end, grieving for Turnus, she orders Juturna to violate the truce and seek a change of fortune. She ultimately admits Juturna acted under her command, then asks Jove to preserve the Latins’ name, language, habits, and customs. After this is granted, she withdraws.

She is an active divine protector and planner whose favor centers on Jason, Aeson’s son, because he once showed her pity while she was disguised as an old woman; she also seeks punishment for Pelias’s insult. She first nurtures the Earthborn as a trial for Heracles, then strengthens Ancaeus after the deaths of Idmon and Tiphys. In the quest for the fleece, she consults Athena, proposes that Cypris make Medea love Jason, hides Jason and his companions in mist on their approach to Aeetes’ palace, and drives Medea to flee. After Apsyrtus falls, she directs the voyage through storms, reversals, warnings, favorable winds, and protective mists, steering the Argonauts away from dangers. She commands Iris and calls on Thetis, whom she tended from infancy and married to Peleus, to help Argo pass Scylla, Charybdis, and the Wandering rocks. At the end, she sends nymphs to honor Jason and Medea’s marriage and inspires Arete’s judgment. Her power also extends to Macris, whom she drove from Euboea.

Euripides · -0429~

Juno, also called Hera, is Jove’s wife and a divine power of hostility, vengeance, and deception. Her inveterate rage against Hercules arises because Jove loved Alcmena; she sends the Fiend with Iris to drive him mad, causing him to kill his family and ruin his household. After losing the judgment of beauty, she opposes Paris by creating Helen’s likeness, sending the real Helen to Egypt, and making Greeks and Trojans fight over the phantom. Though formerly hostile to Menelaus, she later favors him, Helen, and their safe return to Sparta.

Juno in The Iliad

Homer · -0749/-0724

Juno is a goddess, queen of the goddesses and gods, eldest daughter of Saturn, sister and wife of Jove/Jupiter, and mother of Mars and Vulcan. She possesses a gold-bedizened chariot and favors Argos, Sparta, and Mycenae. Her central concern is the war: she supports the Argives, Achaeans, and Danaans, cares for Achilles and Agamemnon, and is fiercely hostile to the Trojans, Ilius, Priam’s people, and their advance. She repeatedly seeks Troy’s destruction, out of anger and a sworn refusal to protect its people. Her course unfolds through conflict with Jove’s Trojan policy. She suspects Thetis has won Jove’s aid for Achilles, reproaches Jove, fears his threat, and temporarily falls silent. She rejects peace, urges Minerva to make the Trojans break their oaths, drives to Troy to encourage the Argives, and presses Neptune and Minerva to aid the failing Greeks. Though Iris delivers Jove’s command that restrains her from battle, Juno continues to offer the Argives counsel and influence Agamemnon. Determined to change events, she deceives Jupiter: she obtains Venus’s girdle, enlists Sleep, and seduces Jupiter on Mount Ida so Neptune may aid the Achaeans. Later she denies causing Neptune’s intervention, carries Jove’s orders to Iris and Apollo, and warns the gods that his plans will trouble mortals and immortals. She secretly sends Iris to Achilles, sends the sun into Oceanus to provide the Achaeans rest, joins the gods defending Achilles, hinders fleeing Trojans with mist, and directs Vulcan against Xanthus before stopping the violence. Still angry, she celebrates Minerva’s victory over Venus and beats Diana when challenged.

Juno in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Juno is a goddess, daughter of Saturn, sister and wife of Jupiter, queen of Jupiter, supreme queen, and queen of the Gods. She possesses a throne and sceptre, has Iris as messenger, and her own bird is the peacock. After Argus’s death, she sets his eyes in the peacock’s tail; she is also possibly associated with Air or Æther. She is said to be the source of the Milky Way milk story. Much of Juno’s record concerns jealousy, anger, punishment, and revenge in response to Jupiter’s relationships and those connected to them. Discovering Jupiter’s intrigue, she obtains Io as a gift, gives her to Argus’s guardianship, later sends a Fury to terrify and drive Io across the earth, and finally is pacified and ends the punishment. Jealous of Calisto, she transforms her into a bear and objects when Calisto is placed among the stars. She seeks revenge on Semele by using a cloud disguise to approach her and persuading her to demand Jupiter’s full divine majesty. Juno disputes Jupiter over sexual pleasure and blinds Tiresias after his judgment. She punishes Echo by restricting her speech after Echo distracts her while Jupiter’s mistresses escape. She opposes Bacchus and sends Tisiphone against Athamas and Ino, rejoicing at the resulting violence and transforming Ino’s reproaching attendants. She persecutes the family sprung from Agenor, has hatred toward the Tyrian mistress and her race, banishes Latona and drives her to wander with her twins, and causes pestilence against Æacus’s people because their country bears a rival’s name.

Juno, also called Hera, is Jupiter’s wife and consort on Olympus, queen of the gods, and a goddess served by Iris as messenger; the peacock is her favorite bird. Much of her story is driven by suspicion of Jupiter’s unfaithfulness and resentment toward those connected to him or to perceived slights. She discovers Io as a heifer, assigns Argus to guard her, then after Argus’s death sets his eyes on the peacock’s tail and sends a gadfly to torment Io. She changes Callisto into a bear and keeps the Great and Little Bear from the waters. Though Halcyone’s prayers reach her repeatedly, she eventually sends Iris to Somnus so a dream can reveal Ceyx’s death. She persecutes Hercules, Semele, and Bacchus, but is eventually reconciled to Hercules. After Paris awards the golden apple to Venus, she becomes hostile to Paris and the Trojans, uses Venus’s Cestus to distract Jupiter during battle, and later renews her opposition when Aeneas is welcomed by Latinus. She summons Alecto to create discord and forcibly opens the temple of Janus, releasing war.
