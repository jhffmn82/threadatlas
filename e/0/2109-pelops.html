Pelops

person · 6 works · 36 quoted facts · 20 connections
Also called: Pelopid, Pelops the Lydian, Pelops the Lydian hero, son of Tantalos, Πέλοπος, Πέλοψ, Πελοπίδην
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Pelops is the son of Tantalus who wins Hippodamia and the kingdom of Pisa by bribing Myrtilus, then conquers much of the Peloponnese and gives it his name.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Pelops is a Phrygian and Agamemnon’s paternal ancestor associated with the chariot-course described by the Chorus.
  • In The Extant Odes of Pindar, Pelops is Poseidon’s beloved who is restored to mortal life, defeats Oinomaos to marry Hippodameia, fathers six sons, and is honored by a tomb beside the Alpheos where the Olympic games are founded.
  • In The Library, Pelops is the son of Tantalus who is restored to life, becomes Poseidon’s beloved, marries Hippodamia, fathers Atreus and Thyestes, rules Mycenae, and gives his name to the Peloponnese.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Pelops mourns and reveals his ivory left shoulder.
  • In Description of Greece, Pelops is a Lydian who conquers Pisa and Olympia, defeats Oenomaus, marries Hippodamia, establishes rites and temples, and is honored as a hero at Olympia.

Pelops in The Extant Odes of Pindar

Pindar · -0497

Pelops, loved by Poseidon and created by Klotho with an ivory shoulder, is returned by the immortals to the short-lived race of men. After reaching youth, he defeats Oinomaos to marry Hippodameia; they have six sons. He is honored by a tomb beside the Alpheos, while the Olympic games are founded beside that tomb by Herakles.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

OLYMPIAN ODES. / I.

Pelops is described as the founder of the Olympian games and as the beloved son of Poseidon. After being restored to mortal life, he wins Hippodameia as his bride, fathers six sons, and receives enduring honor beside the Alpheos.

notes

Pelops in The Library

Apollodorus · 0001/0200

Pelops, son of Tantalus, was slaughtered and boiled at a banquet of the gods, then restored to life fairer than before. His beauty made him Poseidon’s beloved and minion, and Poseidon gave him a winged chariot. He married Hippodamia, fathered Atreus and Thyestes, succeeded to Mycenae, subjugated Apia and Pelasgiotis, and named Peloponnesus after himself. He killed Stymphalus while feigning friendship, scattering his limbs and causing Greece’s infertility. Later, his bones were required before Ilium could be taken.

Pelops in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Pelops is a Lydian from Asia Minor who came to Pisæa and Olympia, deposed Œnomaus, occupied them, and took neighboring territory from Epeus. Before racing Oenomaus, Elis tradition says he sacrificed to Athene Cydonia; after defeating him, he buried Hippodamia’s suitors in a common tomb and gave them annual funeral rites. Elis also credited him with the first temple to Hermes in the Peloponnese and sacrifice to Hermes for Myrtilus’s murder. He made the contest to Olympian Zeus more famous, and his race with Œnomaus was represented at Olympia. Honoured there nearly as highly among heroes as Zeus among gods, he received an annual black-ram sacrifice; his remains and a lost shoulder-blade tradition were associated with Pisa, Elis, Troy, and Euboea.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK V.--ELIS.

Pelops came from Asia Minor, deposed Œnomaus, occupied Pisæa and Olympia, and took territory bordering Pisæa from Epeus. The people of Elis credited him with building the first temple to Hermes in the Peloponnese and sacrificing to the god for the murder of Myrtilus.

CHAPTER VIII.

Pelops made the contest to Olympian Zeus more famous than his predecessors. The temple at Olympia represents his chariot race against Œnomaus, and also depicts Pelops with Hippodamia, his charioteer, horses, and grooms.

CHAPTER XI.

Pelops is honoured at Olympia nearly as highly among heroes as Zeus is among gods. A tradition says that a shoulder-blade of Pelops was brought to Troy, later recovered from the sea near Euboea, and given to Elis, but it had disappeared by the time of the account.

CHAPTER XV.

Pelops appears in the scenes on the chest of Cypselus, where Œnomaus pursues him and Hippodamia. His horses in that scene have wings.

CHAPTER XXI.

Pelops is associated with Pisa, where a building containing his remains stood near the temple of Artemis Cordace. He is said to have offered annual funeral rites to the suitors of Hippodamia and to have built their common tomb after defeating Oinomaus.
