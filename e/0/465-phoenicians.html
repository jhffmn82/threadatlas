Phoenicians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

The Phoenicians were a people and rebellious nation against Persia. After harsh Persian treatment and Egyptian encouragement, they resisted Persian rule until Sidon’s destruction led their cities to surrender. Later, while defending Tyre by sea, they returned to the city, fought Alexander’s fleet, and attacked besiegers with weapons.

The Phoenicians rebelled against Persian rule after severe treatment by Persian officials and were encouraged by the Egyptians to defend their liberty. They initially resisted the Persian forces, but after Sidon was destroyed, the remaining Phoenician cities surrendered.

CHAP. IV.

The Phoenicians serving Tyre returned hurriedly to the city when Alexander attempted to enter its harbor. Some vessels were lost while forcing their way through the Macedonian fleet, and the Phoenicians continued to attack the besiegers with fire-darts, stones, and other weapons.

Phoenicians in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Phoenicians were merchants and colonizers who learned letters and writing from Syrians. They founded Gades and its temple to Hercules, settled Malta and Gaulus, and established colonies in Africa, Sardinia, Sicily, nearby islands, and western Europe. Allegedly buying Iberian silver cheaply, they transported it to Greece, Asia, and other nations, gained great wealth, and some storm-driven merchants discovered Madeira beyond the pillars of Hercules.

Phœnicians in The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII

Ovid · 0008~

Phœnicians, anciently called Achivi even after establishing themselves in Greece, are associated with a proposed Chiva-for-serpent explanation of Cadmus and Hermione. They settled in Sicily and named a willow-surrounded fountain Alphaga, “the fountain of the willows,” or Arith, “a stream.”

The Phoenicians were anciently called Achivi, a name they retained after establishing themselves in Greece. Their possible word Chiva for serpent was proposed as part of the explanation for the story about Cadmus and Hermione.

Phœnicians in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Phœnicians are presented through a Sidonian tradition claiming superior divine knowledge, identifying Apollo with the Sun and as Æsculapius’s father, while expelling Cnidians from Pachynus.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIII.

A Sidonian claimed that the Phœnicians had more accurate general knowledge about divine matters than the Greeks. The Phœnician tradition he reported made Apollo the father of Æsculapius and interpreted Apollo as the Sun.

Phoenicians in Travels in Morocco

James Richardson · 1860

The Phoenicians successively invaded North Africa and established themselves on its shores around the foundation of Carthage, about 820 years before the Christian era. The Romans later received or wrested control of Africa from them.
