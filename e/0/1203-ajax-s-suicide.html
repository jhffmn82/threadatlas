  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Aias contemplates suicide, considering death preferable to living in dishonor; he takes his bright sword into the countryside intending to bury it after purifying himself, while Tecmessa and the sailors try to prevent his death.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Aias’s suicide is a self-inflicted death performed with Hector’s sword, following his despair over disgrace and suffering; he asks for his body to be protected from enemies and scavengers and treats death as a passage to rest.
  • In Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, and Homerica, Aias kills himself about dawn.
  • In The Library, after losing the contest for Achilles’ arms, Ajax becomes disturbed with grief, plans a nighttime attack on the army, is driven mad by Athena, and kills himself after recovering his senses from slaughtering cattle and their herdsmen.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Ajax’s suicide is caused by his loss of the contest for Achilles’ arms after Ulysses receives them; overcome by anger and grief, he plunges his sword into his breast and dies.
  • In The Fall of Troy, Aias’s suicide follows his wrath against Odysseus and his realization that the gods made him slaughter sheep as enemies; after Athena removes his madness, he kills himself with Hector’s sword.

Aias's suicide in The Fall of Troy

Quintus Smyrnaeus · 0200~/0299~

Aias's suicide, caused by wrath against Odysseus, follows his realization that the gods made him slaughter sheep as enemies and his despair over lost honor. After Athena removes his madness, he kills himself with Hector's sword, leaving Teucer, Tecmessa, and the Danaans grieving.

Aias's suicide follows his realization that the gods made him mistake sheep for his enemies and his despair over lost honor. He uses Hector's sword to kill himself, leaving Teucer, Tecmessa, and the Danaans in profound grief. Odysseus connects the act with Aias's wrath, passion, and the working of fate.
