Corinna in The Amores, or Amours

Ovid · -0015

Corinna is a beautiful woman, mistress, wife, and the speaker’s beloved, whose presence captivates, frustrates, and inspires him. Her beauty is described in detail—flowing hair, white neck, and an unblemished, youthful body—and it gives her power over lovers. She is linked to a husband who guards and confines her, a keeper and fastened door that she learns to deceive, attendants including Nape, and rivalry with Cypassis. The speaker repeatedly seeks access to her, sends tablets, fears her refusals and absences, suspects or is accused of rival affairs, and longs for embraces, kisses, and mutual intimacy. Corinna is clever in manipulating desire: she dismisses the lover through feigned ailments or faults, then wins him back with caresses, words, and kisses. Her life also includes anxiety for her parrot, travel by ship toward Baiæ, and a dangerous pregnancy after an attempted abortion; the speaker prays for her survival and uncertainly claims responsibility for her unborn child. She honors Isis, possesses Paphos and wave-beaten Cythera, and is proud or disdainful because of her beauty. By the end, the speaker’s poems have made her famous and public: though he once considered her his alone, he fears that their publication will cause her to be shared with many rivals.

ELEGY II.

The beloved mistress has recently captivated the narrator. He asks her either to love him or to give him a reason to continue loving her, and he promises to remain devoted to her and unite her name with his in song.

ELEGY IV.

The husband attends the banquet and reclines beside the mistress. The speaker fears that he will touch, embrace, kiss, and pursue sexual intimacy with her, and advises her to make him drink until he falls asleep. The husband will later lock the mistress in and may attempt to take kisses and further intimacy from her.

ELEGY V.

Corinna comes to the narrator's chamber wearing a loose tunic, with her flowing hair covering her white neck. She struggles to remain covered but is overcome, and the narrator describes her body as entirely without blemish before embracing her.

ELEGY VI.

The mistress is the woman whose house the lover seeks to enter. The lover recalls having spoken to her in the porter's behalf, and he supposes that she may now be asleep with the porter.

ELEGY XI.

Corinna is Nape's mistress and the recipient of the speaker's tablets. She refuses the speaker's request, sending word that she cannot be seen that day.

ELEGY VI.

Corinna was the speaker's mistress, and the parrot had pleased her since it was given to her. She anxiously prayed for the parrot during its illness, and the dying bird addressed her in its final words. She also repeatedly accused the speaker of finding other women attractive and of having an affair with Cypassis.

ELEGY VIII.

Corinna learns of the speaker’s intrigue with Cypassis and fixes angry eyes upon Cypassis. The speaker tries to persuade Corinna that he gave no guilty sign of their secret amours.

ELEGY X.

Corinna prepares to leave her familiar couch and household gods to travel by sea to Baiæ. The speaker fears for her safety, urges her to remain on land, and later hopes that she will return safely so that he can embrace and kiss her.

ELEGY XIII.

Corinna is exhausted and lies prostrate in danger of her life while trying to disengage the burden of her pregnant womb. Naso says that she conceived by him, or so he thinks, and prays that she will survive.

ELEGY XVII.

Corinna possesses great beauty, which makes her proud and disdainful toward the speaker. She is the only woman whom the speaker promises to celebrate in his poems.

ELEGY XIX.

Corinna is described as clever and as having recognized the lover's attraction to difficulty. She feigned headaches and faults in order to dismiss and torment him, then restored his affection through caresses, delightful words, and kisses. The husband is encouraged to suspect her nighttime movements and possible communications.

---- BOOK THE THIRD. / ELEGY I.

Corinna learned through Elegy how to deceive her keeper, loosen the constancy of a fastened door, leave her couch in a loose tunic, and move through the night without stumbling. She tore apart a birthday message sent to her and threw it into the water near her home.

ELEGY VI.

The mistress is the person to whom the speaker is hastening. The river's obstruction prevents him from reaching her and delays their expected mutual transports.

ELEGY XII.

Corinna is the mistress whom the speaker once loved alone and called his own. His poems have made her public and famous, so he fears that he must now share her with many others.

Corinna in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Corinna is a beautiful, celebrated, and haughty woman who is the speaker’s beloved and mistress, loved by Naso and Ovid and made the subject of verse. Her beauty is described as unmatched, but she lacks apparel or payment worthy of it; she knows the power of her appearance and gains pride from seeing herself in a mirror. She is watched by her husband, keeper, and guards, making access to her difficult, yet she also attracts wealthy lovers and is preferred by a newly rich soldier. Advice given around her urges the strategic use of modesty, rivals, refusals, and gifts. Her relationship with the speaker unfolds through desire, delays, jealousy, and public fame. She visits him at midday in a loose tunic, resists as he removes it, and they have sex before resting. At other times she hesitates to visit, sends tablets refusing him, keeps him out, and can renew his desire by feigning illness or wrongdoing before becoming affectionate. She is jealous of other women, especially Cypassis, and mourns her parrot, whose final word is her name. She suffers distress after losing her once abundant hair through her own treatment. She prepares for a dangerous sea voyage, and later undergoes a dangerous attempt to end a pregnancy, causing the speaker to fear for her life. Though Ovid says she first inspired his poetry, he fears his praise has made her common, known to many, and sexually available to others; another woman even claims the distinguished name Corinna because of that poetry.

P. OVIDI NASONIS AMORES / LIBER PRIMUS

Corinna visits the speaker during the hot midday and appears in a loosely fastened tunic with her hair covering her neck. The speaker removes her tunic; after resisting in a way that appears unwilling, she is overcome, and they have sex before resting together.

VIII

The young woman has attracted the attention of a wealthy young lover and blushes when Dipsas tells her so. Dipsas urges her to exploit her beauty by pretending modesty, refusing and accepting favors strategically, cultivating rivals, displaying signs of other lovers, and securing gifts through herself and her household.

XI

Corinna is Nape’s mistress and the intended recipient of Naso’s tablets. She has previously hesitated about coming to Naso, and Naso asks Nape to watch her face as she reads and to make her write a substantial reply without delay.

XII

The beloved woman sends tablets bearing a refusal, and she once stopped at the threshold after striking her toe. She has lost her formerly abundant, delicate hair through her own treatment of it, and she blushes and weeps while looking at the severed hair.

VI

Corinna mourns the parrot and makes vows for its safety, but the vows are carried away by the South Wind. She becomes jealous when the speaker looks at or praises other women and accuses him of desiring Cypassis. The parrot’s last word is her name.

X

Corinna prepares to leave her familiar bed and household and undertake a dangerous voyage by sea. The speaker hopes she will return safely, welcomes her imagined arrival, and later fears for her life when she undergoes a dangerous attempt to end a pregnancy.

XVII

Corinna’s beauty is said to make her hard and arrogant with the speaker. She recognizes his susceptibility to refusal and jealousy, feigns illness or wrongdoing, and then becomes affectionate when his desire has been renewed. Another woman is said to publicize that she is Corinna because the speaker’s poetry gives the name distinction.

VI

The narrator's lady-love is the person he is trying to reach across the river. Elsewhere, she prefers a newly rich soldier to the narrator and keeps him from entering, while her guard and husband make access to her difficult.

XII

Corinna is the woman whom Ovid alone began to love, but he fears that she must now be shared with many. Ovid says that she alone stirred his poetic genius and that his praise of her beauty has made her known and sexually available to others.
