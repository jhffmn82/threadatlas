Lysander in The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books

Diodorus Siculus · -0059/-0029

Lysander was a Spartan commander and bold, daring admiral of the Lacedaemonians. He assembled fleets and forces at Rhodes, Ephesus, Miletus, and Chios, receiving money from Cyrus for the army and the Lacedaemonian war. After defeating the Athenian fleet at Notium, he was discharged from an earlier command and handed the fleet at Ephesus to Callicratides. Later made high admiral, he raised ships, captured Lampsacus, defeated and captured the Athenian fleet at Aegos Potamos, took Sestos, and blockaded Samos and Athens. Following the war, he helped impose an oligarchic government at Athens, advised the selection of thirty governors, and held authority to establish oligarchies and settle city governments. Prominent at Sparta, he sought to end Heraclid rule and make any Spartan eligible for kingship, unsuccessfully trying to bribe oracles. Summoned to Athens with ships and soldiers, he faced Pausanias's opposition. Finally sent to Phocis, he besieged Haliartus and was killed there in battle.

Narrative, section by section

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAP. IX.

Lysander was appointed admiral of the Lacedaemonians and gathered soldiers, sailors, and ships at Rhodes, Ephesus, Miletus, and Chios. He obtained financial support from Cyrus and defeated the Athenian fleet at Notium after learning that Alcibiades had sailed away.

CHAP. X.

Lysander had previously commanded the Lacedaemonian fleet but was discharged from that command. He delivered the fleet at Ephesus to Callicratides.

CHAP. XV.

Lysander became high admiral of the Lacedaemonian fleet, raised ships, secured money and authority from Cyrus, and fitted out his forces at Ephesus. He captured Lampsacus, defeated and captured the Athenian fleet at Aegos Potamos, took Sestos, and then blockaded Samos and Athens.

DIODORUS SICULUS. / BOOK XIV.

Lysander advised the Athenians to choose thirty governors and threatened Theramenes when he opposed the proposal. He helped impose the oligarchic government after the peace with Athens.

CHAP. III.

After the Lacedaemonians gained supremacy by sea and land, they appointed Lysander high admiral with authority to establish oligarchies in the cities. He later became prominent at Sparta and attempted to abolish the rule of the Heraclidae by promoting the idea that any Spartan should be eligible for kingship. He tried to bribe the oracles at Delphi, Dodona, and Jupiter Ammon, but failed; after his death, a speech advocating this change was found among his papers.

CHAP. VI.

The Athenian decemvirs summoned Lysander from Lacedaemon with forty ships and a thousand soldiers. Pausanias opposed his influence by marching to Athens and arranging a reconciliation.

CHAP. vm.

Lysander was sent to Phocis with a few soldiers and raised more troops there. He besieged Haliartus with the Phocians but was killed in battle along with many Lacedaemonians and their confederates.

Lysander in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lysander, a Spartan and son of Aristocritus, was a military leader whose victories over Antiochus near Ephesus and over the Athenian navy at Aegospotami brought him honor. The Samians erected and inscribed a praising statue for him at Olympia, while Ephesians set up statues after Aegospotami; he also dedicated eagles and victories. He especially opposed Leonidas and helped bring about his deposition. Marching into Phocis and attacking Haliartus, he was killed by Thebans and Athenians.

Narrative, section by section

3 sections; each heading opens the text

BOOK III.--LACONIA.

Lysander marched into Phocis, gathered the Phocians, and attacked Haliartus in Bœotia. The Thebans and Athenians made a sortie, and Lysander and several Lacedæmonians were killed.

CHAPTER VI.

Lysander, a descendant of Lysander son of Aristocritus, especially disliked Leonidas. He associated with Cleombrotus, brought charges against Leonidas, and contributed to his deposition.

BOOK VI.--ELIS. PART II.

The Samians erected a statue at Olympia to Lysander, son of Aristocritus, and dedicated inscriptions praising his fame. After the Athenian fleet was defeated at Aegospotami, the Ephesians also placed statues of Lysander and other Spartans in the temple of Artemis.

Lysander in Pausanias' Description of Greece, Volume II

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Lysander, a Lacedæmonian and son of Aristocritus, commanded the Peloponnesian fleet, gained Cyrus’s money, defeated Antiochus, and captured 100 Athenian ships at Ægos-potamoi. He condemned Eteonicus for injustice but executed Philocles and 4000 Athenian captives without burial. He established Decemvirates and Harmosts, encouraged love of money, and was judged to harm the Lacedæmonians. Crowned by Poseidon at Delphi, he fell attacking Haliartus.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXXII.

Lysander showed naval skill by defeating Antiochus, securing money from Cyrus, and capturing 100 Athenian ships at Ægos-potamoi while their crews were ashore. He also acted justly by condemning Eteonicus in a property dispute with Autolycus, but he was condemned for executing Philocles and 4000 Athenian captives, denying them burial, establishing Decemvirates and Harmosts, and encouraging the Lacedæmonians' desire for money. He fell while attacking Haliartus.
