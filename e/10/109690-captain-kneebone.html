Captain Kneebone in Dragon's Blood

Henry Milner Rideout · 1909

Captain Kneebone is a peppery but capable sea-captain and navigator, associated with the small steamer Kut Sing. Early on, he receives Maurice Heywood and Rudolph aboard her, where two Chinese coolies have plague; hoping to evade quarantine at Koprah, he supplies his visitors with food, drink, and Spanish wine and pins his hopes of returning Home on a lottery. He later wins the Derby Sweepstake Lottery and arrives from Saigon at the nunnery with abundant provisions, servants, and drink. Though retired and claiming to have no ship, he stays through the crisis, hosts a lavish banquet, gives two thousand rupees through Miss Drake to the hospital and kiddies, and gives Heywood half his fortune and a journey Home. During the attack, the little captain takes command of the fugitives and defenses, directs Rudolph to cover, fires on the advancing Chinese, and judges the enemy's likely moves. As ammunition dwindles, he warns that the countermine is not enough, signals the Hakka boat, helps the fugitives board it, and finally recognizes and praises its concealed approach and steersman.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IV THE SWORD-PEN "Wutzler was missing last night," said Heywood, lazily.

Captain Kneebone receives Heywood and Rudolph aboard his small steamer, the Kut Sing. He has two plague cases among his coolies, hopes to evade quarantine at Koprah, and believes that a lottery fortune will enable him to return Home. He gives the visitors food, drink, and Spanish wine.

CHAPTER XV KAU FAI At the top of the nunnery stairs, Rudolph met them with awkward ceremony, and with that smiling air of encouragement which a nurse might use in trying cheerfully to deceive a sick man.

Captain Kneebone arrives at the nunnery with numerous provisions and servants, announcing that he has come to stay for a month. He creates a lavish banquet, announces his lottery winnings, gives Miss Drake two thousand rupees, and offers Maurice Heywood half of his fortune and a journey Home. When the attack starts, he says that he is retired and has no ship, having arrived in a native boat that has already departed.

CHAPTER XVIII SIEGE He never believed that they could hold the northeast corner for a minute, so loud and unceasing was the uproar.

Captain Kneebone orders Rudolph to stay under cover and directs the defenders during the attack. He fires at the advancing Chinese forces, warns of the pung-dong, and predicts that the enemy will either rush the defenses or settle into a siege.

CHAPTER XX THE HAKKA BOAT "Pretty fair," Captain Kneebone said.

Captain Kneebone grudgingly praises the countermine but warns that it is not the end. He grows increasingly concerned about the ammunition shortage, proposes signaling the Hakka boat, lights the escape signal, and helps the fugitives board. He initially believes the boat has abandoned them, then recognizes its concealed approach and praises its steersman.

Captain Kneebone in Dragon's Blood

Henry Milner Rideout · 1909

Captain Kneebone, a wealthy captain of the Tsuen-Chau and friend of Cesare Domenico, assumes command of a boat after Heywood is believed dead. He delays along the shore, watches for danger, and grieves intensely, sharing his sorrow with Miss Drake. The episode ends with his astonished awakening when Heywood returns.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXI THE DRAGON'S SHADOW The lowdah would have set his dirty sails without delay, for the fair wind was already drooping; but at the first motion he found himself deposed, and a usurper in command, at the big steering-paddle.

Captain Kneebone takes command of the boat after Heywood is presumed dead and keeps it waiting along the shore while watching anxiously for danger. He grieves openly, saying that he cannot stand Heywood's loss, but later rests beside Miss Drake and shares his sorrow with her. When Heywood suddenly returns, Kneebone wakes in astonishment and calls for the others to be roused.
