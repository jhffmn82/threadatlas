  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Aegina is a city invaded by an Athenian army, which lays waste its countryside and resolves to raze its city; its defenders later lose a naval battle and submit to Athens.
  • In The Argonautica, Aegina is an island and beach where the Argonauts stop after leaving Anaphe, competing in an innocent race to fetch water and return it to the ship.
  • In The Library, Aegina is formerly called Oenone and is named Aegina; Zeus and Aegina have Aeacus there, and Zeus makes its ants into men for him.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books I-VII, Ægina is an island and kingdom of Æacus, once called Œnopia by ancients and then called Ægina by Æacus after his mother.
  • In Description of Greece, Ægina is an island formerly called Œnone, said to be named for Ægina after Zeus brought her there; surrounding rocks and sunken reefs make access difficult, attributed to Æacus’s defense against pirates and enemies.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Aegina is an island ruled by Aeacus, devastated by Juno’s plague, then repopulated from ants after Jupiter answers Aeacus’s prayer.

Ægina in Description of Greece

Pausanias · 0155~/0175~

Ægina is an island, formerly called Œnone, said to be named for Ægina after Zeus brought her there; surrounding rocks and sunken reefs make access difficult, attributed to Æacus’s defense against pirates and enemies.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER XXIX.

Ægina was said to have been called after Ægina, the daughter of Asopus, who was brought there by Zeus, replacing its former name Œnone. It was regarded as difficult to reach because of surrounding rocks and reefs, which the Æginetans attributed to Æacus’s precautions against pirates and enemies.
