Admetus in Alcestis

Euripides · -0437

Admetus is the king and ruler of Pherae in Thessaly, son of Pheres, husband of Alcestis, and father of their two children. He is portrayed as generous, hospitable, innocent, artistic, affectionate, eloquent, impulsive, and spoiled, yet also unconsciously insincere, self-blinded, and fundamentally selfish in accepting the sacrifice expected of others. When threatened with death, he is saved because Alcestis agrees to die in his place, while his mother and father refuse. After her death, he grieves intensely, orders mourning and funeral honors, promises never to remarry, accepts responsibility for their children, and maintains that a new bride would dishonor Alcestis and be hated by the children. Even while mourning, he conceals Alcestis's death from Heracles and receives him hospitably, refusing to turn away a guest. He quarrels violently with Pheres for not dying in his place, then collapses in grief. Returning to his empty house, he confronts the loss of Alcestis, his happiness, and his honor; public shame and self-judgment lead him to recognize what her sacrifice cost. Though he resists remarriage, he accepts a veiled woman at Heracles's insistence and discovers Alcestis restored to life, becoming again her husband and Heracles's acknowledged friend.

Narrative, section by section

5 sections; each heading opens the text

INTRODUCTION

Admetus is portrayed as generous, innocent, artistic, affectionate, eloquent, impulsive, spoiled, unconsciously insincere, and fundamentally selfish. He accepts Alcestis’s sacrifice, blinds himself with beautiful words, and is publicly shamed by the Chorus, the Handmaid, and Pheres; after her burial, he recognizes that he sacrificed his honour and lost both honour and happiness.

ALCESTIS

Admetus is the King of Pherae whose life was previously spared when Alcestis agreed to die in his place. He grieves over Alcestis, promises never to remarry, accepts responsibility for their children, and orders extensive mourning and funeral honors for her.

CHORUS.

The husband is identified as the son whose mother and father were asked to act for him but dared not do so. His bride died in his place, and the elders say that any new bride after her would be hated by his two children and by them.

Admetus conceals Alcestis's identity and welcomes Heracles into his house because he refuses to turn away a guest, even during his mourning. He accompanies Alcestis's funeral and violently quarrels with Pheres, condemning him for refusing to die in his son's place. After Pheres leaves, Admetus collapses into grief and goes with the funeral procession.

Admetus returns home overwhelmed by Alcestis’s death and fears entering the house, facing the empty bed, chair, and household. He rejects the prospect of another bride, believing that remarriage would dishonor Alcestis, but accepts the veiled woman at Heracles’s insistence and discovers that she is Alcestis restored to life.

Admetus in The Tragedies of Euripides, Volume I

Euripides · -0437

Admetus is a mortal man, son of Pheres, king of Pherae and the Thessalians, ruler of Thessaly, and lord of his palace. He is Alcestis’s husband and the father of their children. When death and mortality threaten him, Alcestis agrees to die in his place, allowing him to survive. As she approaches death, he grieves, promises never to remarry, pledges care for their children and fidelity to her, and arranges extensive mourning and funeral observances. He bitterly condemns Pheres for refusing to die for him and bars Pheres and his wife from his house after their dispute. Though mourning and fearful of life without Alcestis, of his home, children, and public reproach, Admetus remains notably hospitable: he conceals the household calamity from Hercules and receives him as a guest. Widowed, he rejects remarriage and honors Alcestis. Finally, he accepts the woman brought by Heracles, recognizes her as Alcestis after her dedication to the dead is undone, and orders the citizens to celebrate their restored companionship.

5 sections; each heading opens the text

ALCESTIS.

Admetus survives because Alcestis agrees to die in his place, but he grieves as she approaches death. He promises that he will not remarry, will care for their children, and will remain faithful to Alcestis in life and after death.

Admetus promises Alcestis that he will not marry another wife and accepts responsibility for their children. He orders elaborate mourning and funeral observances, receives Hercules as a guest despite his grief, and angrily reproaches Pheres for refusing to die in his place.

CHOR. Desist, for the present calamity is sufficient; and do not, O son,

Admetus condemns Pheres for his lack of courage and for refusing to die for him. After Pheres blames him for Alcestis's death, Admetus orders him and his wife never to enter his house again.

ADMETUS, CHORUS.

Admetus grieves intensely for Alcestis and says that he would rather be dead than live without her. He fears entering his house, facing his children, enduring public reproach, and being accused of having allowed his wife to die in his place. He eventually accepts the woman brought by Heracles, recognizes her as Alcestis, and commands the citizens to celebrate.

notes

Admetus is the person whose companionship with Alcestis becomes possible again only after the ritual undoing of her dedication to the dead. The notes also connect his situation with the misfortune traditionally associated with surviving one’s children.
