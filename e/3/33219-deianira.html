  • In Heroides and Amores, Deianira is Hercules' jealous wife who sends him a poison-smeared cloak after Iole's arrival, causing his death and leading her to resolve on suicide.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Deianira is Oeneus's daughter whom Hercules marries after Meleager's death.
  • In The Historical Library of Diodorus the Sicilian, in Fifteen Books, Dejanira is Hercules' wife who anoints his coat and shirt with Nessus's substance, recognizes her role in his death, and hangs herself.
  • In The Seven Plays in English Verse, Deianira is Heracles' wife who mistakenly uses the Centaur's gift on a robe to restore his affection, realizes it is fatal, and kills herself after Heracles is poisoned.
  • In The Metamorphoses of Ovid, Books VIII-XV, Deïanira is Hercules' wife who sends him Nessus's blood-stained tunic as a love charm, unaware that it will cause his death.
  • In The Library, Deianira is Hercules' wife who is won from Achelous, receives Nessus's supposed love charm, applies it to Hercules' tunic, and hangs herself after seeing its effect.
  • In Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable, Dejanira is Hercules' wife who poisons him with Nessus's blood after fearing Iole, then hangs herself when she realizes what she has done.

Deianira in Heroides and Amores

Ovid · -0024~/-0015~

Deianira is an Aetolian woman, wife of Hercules and sister of Meleager, Tydeus, and Gorge. Jealous and distressed by Iole’s arrival, she sends Hercules a poison-smeared cloak, causing his death; guilt then drives her to resolve on suicide.

Narrative, section by section

1 section; each heading opens the text

IX

Deianira writes to Hercules in jealousy and distress after Iole is brought into the city as a captive and possible new wife. When she hears that Hercules is dying from the cloak she sent, she calls herself wicked, resolves to die, and says that her death will prove her union with him.

Deianira in The Seven Plays in English Verse

Sophocles · -0449/-0429

Deianira is a free-born queen, daughter of Oeneus, wife of Heracles, and mother of Hyllus and other sons. From Pleuron, she lives with Heracles in Trachis during exile, troubled by his long absences and dangers. She has known earlier suitors, including Achelôüs, but her life centers on her marriage, her fear of losing Heracles, and her response to Love's power over human affections. When captive women, including a maiden beloved by Heracles, enter her household, Deianira initially refuses blame: she pities the maiden, accepts the truth, and says she would favor her rather than contend against Heaven. Yet anxiety, jealousy, and marital fidelity lead her to use the Centaur's hidden gift as a love-charm. Following Heracles' prophetic scroll, she anoints and sends him a robe, intending to restore his affection. When the wool used on it dissolves in sunlight, she realizes too late that the gift may be fatal. Hyllus reports Heracles' agony and condemns her, though he later affirms that she acted unknowingly and with good intentions. Feeling guilty for poisoning Heracles and committed to death and vengeance if he is harmed, Deianira laments her abandonment and kills herself with a sword.

Narrative, section by section

4 sections; each heading opens the text

THE TRACHINIAN MAIDENS

Dêanira describes her fearful life as Heracles' wife, since he is repeatedly absent on labours and has now sent no word for fifteen months. She rejoices when told that Heracles is alive and victorious, but is distressed by the captive women and especially by the revelation that Iole has been brought into her household. She questions Lichas about Iole and learns that Heracles' campaign was motivated by love.

DÊ. Nay, I entreat thee by His name, whose fire

DÊ urges Lichas to speak the truth about Heracles and the maiden, declaring that she will not blame either of them for a passion governed by Love. After learning the truth, she resolves to accept the situation rather than struggle against Heaven and prepares a message and gifts for the maiden.

CHORUS.

Deianira explains that she has brought the captive maiden into her household and fears that Heracles will make the younger woman his mate rather than remain devoted to her. She sends Heracles a robe treated with the Centaur's gift, then becomes terrified when the wool used to apply it dissolves in sunlight and concludes that she may have caused Heracles' death. After Hyllus reports Heracles' suffering and condemns her, Deianira leaves in silence.

CHORUS.

Deianira dies by her own hand after discovering that the robe she sent to Heracles has destroyed him. Before her suicide, she laments her abandonment and prepares the fatal blow; Hyllus later declares that she acted with good intentions and without knowing the robe's true effect.

Dejanira in Bulfinch's Mythology: The Age of Fable

Thomas Bulfinch · 1855

Dejanira, the fairest maiden, was sought by Hercules and Achelous, married Hercules, then poisoned him with Nessus’s blood after fearing Iole; realizing this, she hanged herself and became his widow.

Narrative, section by section

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter XII

Dejanira married Hercules, and they lived in peace for three years. Believing that Hercules favored Iole, she treated his white robe with the blood of Nessus, unknowingly poisoning him; after realizing what she had done, she hanged herself.

Chapter XVI

Dejanira was described as the fairest of maidens, and Hercules and Achelous were among the suitors who sought to win her.
