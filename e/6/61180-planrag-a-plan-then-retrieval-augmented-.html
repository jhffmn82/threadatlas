Myeonghwa Lee, Seonho An, and Min-Soo Kim are listed as its authors from the School of Computing at KAIST.

Abstract

The paper studies using LLMs for decision making that requires complex data analysis. It defines Decision QA as answering the best decision for a question, business rules, and database, and introduces the Decision QA benchmark (DQA) with Locating and Building scenarios based on Europa Universalis IV and Victoria 3. It proposes PlanRAG, which first generates a decision-making plan and then retrieves data-analysis queries. PlanRAG outperforms the state-of-the-art iterative RAG method by 15.8% in Locating and 7.4% in Building.

Introduction

The unit introduces Decision QA, a decision-making task in which a language model uses a database, business rules, and a decision-making question to produce the best decision. It presents the DQA benchmark, which contains Locating and Building scenarios derived from 301 situations in Europa Universalis IV and Victoria 3. The unit also introduces PlanRAG, an iterative plan-then-retrieval augmented generation method designed to help language models plan analyses, retrieve data, re-plan, and answer decision questions. Experiments on DQA compare PlanRAG with an existing iterative RAG method and report that PlanRAG is more effective for Decision QA.

Decision QA task .. Statistics of DQA

Decision QA concerns answering the best decision from a decision-making question, business rules, and a structured database that follows a schema. The DQA benchmark includes Locating and Building scenarios, each represented through relational and graph databases. The Locating scenario chooses a trading node to maximize the change in a target country's profit, while the Building scenario chooses a factory building expansion to minimize a good's current price. DQA contains 301 question-database pairs and 602 databases across the two database representations.

Methodology: PlanRAG

PlanRAG is an iterative plan-then-retrieval augmented generation technique for Decision QA that answers the best decision from a question, schema, and rules. Unlike existing RAG, it uses two types of reasoning: making a plan and answering from results retrieved through data analysis queries. A single language model performs planning, retrieving and answering, and re-planning, with queries executed against database D through SQL or Cypher. The process continues until the model determines that no further analysis is needed for the decision.

Experiments / Experimental Setup .. Results and Analysis

The experiments evaluate PlanRAG for the Decision QA task against Single-turn RAG, Iterative RAG, and a PlanRAG variant without re-planning, using GPT-4 with MySQL and Neo4j databases. PlanRAG-LM achieves the highest accuracy in both the Locating scenario and the Building scenario, improving over IterRAG-LM by 15.8% and 7.4%, respectively. Planning and re-planning reduce missed data analysis, candidate-selection, and data-retrieval errors, although reasoning and query-generation errors remain. Re-planning occurs much more often in Building, indicating that its longer database traversals make planning more difficult.

Related Work

This unit reviews NLP tasks using structured data, including Table NLI and Tabular QA, and contrasts existing benchmarks with the Decision QA benchmark. Existing benchmarks generally do not consider business rules or querying over large structured databases, and their tables have far fewer rows than those in Decision QA. It also reviews retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), including single-turn and iterative retrieval-generation methods, and notes that RAG was not powerful enough to solve the decision-making task well in the reported experiments.

Conclusions

The paper explores large language models (LLMs) as a solution for decision making. It proposes Decision QA, a task that identifies the best decision for complex questions requiring business rules and business situations represented in a large database. The authors build DQA from 301 database-question-answer sets drawn from two popular video games that imitate real business situations. They also propose PlanRAG, a planning and re-planning retrieval-augmented generation technique that significantly outperforms SOTA iterative RAG on Decision QA.

Limitations

The paper examines the capability of LLM as a solution for decision making, focusing on Decision QA using graph database or relational database. It does not address Decision QA based on hybrid databases or vector databases, which are left for future research. The authors propose techniques from a high-level RAG perspective and leave low-level methods, such as fine-tuning a model to generate Cypher queries, for future work. They design PlanRAG methodology and implement PlanRAG-LM using a single LM, while its effectiveness in a multiple-LM framework remains unexplored.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical Considerations addresses hallucination and bias risks in language models and explains that RAG methods mitigate but do not eliminate these issues. It advises examining whether generated decisions rely on hallucinated or biased knowledge in real-world applications. The benchmark and simulator were constructed from Europa Universalis IV and Victoria 3 after consideration of the Paradox Interactive end user license agreement, and they are released under the MIT license. Because the source video games depict historical situations and may contain aggressive or outdated knowledge, the datasets anonymize nation names with three-letter codes.

Appendix / Data collection

The data collection process selects a provided savefile from each game and uses a game savefile parser to extract related data into D. The extracted data is stored according to the DB schema for each scenario, and Q is generated by applying components of D to predefined question formats. To control benchmark quality, the Locating scenario includes one problem per country while omitting countries with low TPcountry. The Building scenario excludes problems with multiple answers.

Data instances

The data instances concern two scenarios that share the same business rules R but may use different databases D. In the Locating scenario, each instance has a distinct goal defined by a COUNTRY_NODE and HOME_NODE, asking where to locate a merchant to maximize COUNTRY_NODE’s profit on HOME_NODE. In the Building scenario, each instance uses a different GOODS value and asks which building ID should be increased by five levels to maximally decrease that good’s market price. The instances require language models to retrieve different intermediate nodes and edges to calculate values such as TPR and TS for the best decision.

Simulators for annotation

The unit concerns simulators for annotation developed to evaluate decisions in games and on DQA. Applying every decision to real games is described as impossible because games involve randomness and are not open-sourced. Each simulator takes D with a decision as input, calculates the decision's effects according to business rules, and returns an updated D. By simulating all decision candidates, the system annotates the best decision; in the example, Doab produces a profit increment of 0.23 and Ganges 0.03, making Doab the best decision.

Locating simulator algorithm

The Locating simulator calculates profits for every country in database D. Algorithm 1 first uses trading_power_estimate to initialize TPcountry(n, c) and TPtotal(n), then repeatedly selects nodes with find_top_n and updates flows with flow_estimation. After all nodes have been simulated, the algorithm calculates profit(c) using LV, IV, and the node's trading-power values. The simulator returns the updated database D.

Experimenting with other models

This unit evaluates IterRAG-LM and PlanRAG-LM with GPT-3.5, Llama 2 (70B), Llama 2 (13B), and Phi-2. The experiments use one machine with eight Nvidia A100 (80GB) GPUs, with vLLM used to accelerate inference for open models. Llama 2 and Phi-2 cannot solve any DQA problems, while GPT-3.5 performs better with IterRAG-LM than with PlanRAG-LM. Re-planning cases

This unit describes how PlanRAG-LM re-plans solutions and categorizes the resulting changes to the original plan. Re-planning cases are grouped into Increase, Same, and Decrease according to whether the number of steps rises, stays the same, or falls. The cases are further classified by operations such as re-ordering, replacing, changing targets, adding actions, dividing steps, deleting steps, and merging steps. In the Locating scenario, adding look-ups is the primary strategy, while in the Building scenario, dividing steps into sub-steps is predominant.
